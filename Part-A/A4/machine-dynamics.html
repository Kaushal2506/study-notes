1.1 Meaning of Machine Dynamics

  • Machine Dynamics is the branch of Mechanical Engineering that deals with the forces, torques, energy and motion involved in operating machines.
  • It studies how forces acting on machine components affect their motion and performance.
  • It considers both the motion of machine parts and the forces responsible for producing that motion.
  • It helps engineers design machines that operate smoothly, safely, efficiently and with minimum vibration.

1.2 Difference Between Kinematics and Dynamics

Studies motion without considering forces.Studies motion considering the forces causing it.
Deals with displacement, velocity and acceleration.Deals with force, torque, mass and energy.
Does not consider the cause of motion.Considers the cause of motion.
Example: Velocity of a piston.Example: Force required to move a piston.

1.3 Importance of Machine Dynamics in Marine Engineering

Machine dynamics is particularly important in ships because marine machinery contains several rotating and reciprocating components.

Applications

  • Main propulsion diesel engines.
  • Auxiliary diesel generators.
  • Propeller shafts.
  • Pumps and compressors.
  • Turbochargers.
  • Gearboxes and reduction gears.
  • Cooling water and lubrication systems.
  • Rotating machinery such as turbines.

Important objectives

  • Maintain uniform and stable machine operation.
  • Reduce unwanted speed fluctuations.
  • Prevent excessive vibration.
  • Avoid resonance and mechanical failures.
  • Reduce bearing loads and shaft stresses.
  • Improve machine efficiency and service life.
  • Ensure safe operation of rotating machinery.

2.1 Introduction to Flywheel

  • A flywheel is a heavy rotating mechanical component used to store rotational kinetic energy.
  • It is generally mounted on a rotating shaft.
  • Its primary function is to reduce fluctuations in the speed of a machine during operation.
  • It stores excess energy when the driving torque is greater than the resisting torque.
  • It releases stored energy when the driving torque becomes less than the resisting torque.
  • A flywheel helps maintain a more uniform angular velocity of the rotating shaft.

2.2 Working Principle of Flywheel

A flywheel works on the principle of conservation of energy.

  • When excess energy is available, the flywheel accelerates and stores energy as rotational kinetic energy.
  • When the machine requires more energy than the driving source supplies, the flywheel decelerates and releases stored energy.
  • This continuous process reduces speed fluctuations during one operating cycle.

Two operating conditions

  • Condition 1: Excess driving energy — Driving torque is greater than resisting torque. The flywheel accelerates. Angular velocity increases. Kinetic energy stored increases.
  • Condition 2: Insufficient driving energy — Resisting torque is greater than driving torque. The flywheel decelerates. Angular velocity decreases. Stored kinetic energy is released.

2.3 Construction of Flywheel

A conventional flywheel consists of three main parts:

Parts

  • Hub — The central portion mounted on the shaft. Transfers torque between the shaft and flywheel. Usually has a keyway or another suitable shaft connection.
  • Spokes — Connect the hub to the rim. Provide structural support. Transfer forces between the rim and hub. Reduce overall weight compared with a solid disc construction.
  • Rim — The outer heavy circular portion. Contains most of the flywheel's mass. Stores most of the rotational kinetic energy. Its large radius provides a high mass moment of inertia.

2.4 Energy Stored in a Flywheel

KE = ½ I ω²

Where: KE = Rotational kinetic energy (J); I = Mass moment of inertia (kg m²); ω = Angular velocity (rad/s).

ω = 2πN / 60

Where: N = Rotational speed (RPM).

Important concept: Energy stored depends on the moment of inertia and the square of angular velocity. If angular velocity doubles, kinetic energy becomes four times. If moment of inertia doubles, stored kinetic energy doubles.

2.5 Mass Moment of Inertia

  • Mass moment of inertia represents the resistance of a rotating body to angular acceleration.
  • It depends on the mass distribution around the axis of rotation.
  • Greater mass moment of inertia means greater resistance to changes in rotational speed.
I = ∫ r² dm

Where: r = Distance of a small mass element from the axis; dm = Small mass element.

BodyMoment of inertia
Solid discI = ½ M R²
Thin circular ringI = M R²
Solid cylinderI = ½ M R²
Hollow cylinderI = ½ M (Rₒ² + Rᵢ²)

2.6 Fluctuation of Energy

  • In practical machines, the driving torque is not always constant.
  • The resisting torque may also vary during operation.
  • Therefore, the energy supplied and energy consumed are not equal at every instant.
  • This produces fluctuations in the rotational speed of the shaft.
  • The flywheel absorbs and releases energy to minimize these fluctuations.

Example: Four-stroke diesel engine

A four-stroke engine completes one working cycle in two revolutions of the crankshaft. The four strokes are: 1. Suction stroke. 2. Compression stroke. 3. Power stroke. 4. Exhaust stroke.

Power is produced mainly during the power stroke. During the remaining strokes, the engine requires energy to keep the crankshaft rotating. The flywheel stores energy during the power stroke and releases it during the other strokes.

2.7 Maximum and Minimum Fluctuation of Energy

ΔE = E_max − E_min

Where: ΔE = Maximum fluctuation of energy (J); E_max = Maximum energy stored; E_min = Minimum energy stored.

The flywheel is designed to accommodate this energy fluctuation without allowing excessive speed variation.

2.8 Coefficient of Fluctuation of Speed

C_s = (ω_max − ω_min) / ω
ω = (ω_max + ω_min) / 2

Similarly, in terms of RPM:

C_s = (N_max − N_min) / N

Where: C_s = Coefficient of fluctuation of speed; N_max = Maximum speed; N_min = Minimum speed; N = Mean speed.

2.9 Flywheel Design Equation

ΔE = I ω² C_s
I = ΔE / (ω² C_s)

This equation is commonly used for flywheel design.

2.11 Flywheel in Marine Diesel Engines

  • Marine diesel engines generate fluctuating torque because combustion occurs intermittently.
  • The flywheel helps maintain relatively uniform crankshaft rotation.
  • It stores energy during periods of excess torque.
  • It releases energy during periods of insufficient torque.
  • It helps the engine pass through portions of the cycle where the instantaneous torque is insufficient.
  • It can also provide a mounting surface for the starter ring gear.
  • It contributes to smooth transmission of power through the crankshaft.
Remember: A flywheel controls cyclic speed fluctuations by storing and releasing energy. It does not maintain the average engine speed when the load changes continuously.

3.1 Introduction to Governors

  • A governor is a mechanical or electronic device used to regulate the mean speed of an engine when its load changes.
  • It automatically adjusts the fuel or working-fluid supply according to the load.
  • It prevents excessive changes in engine speed.
  • It maintains the engine speed within a specified operating range.

Example

Consider a diesel generator operating at constant speed. When electrical load increases, the engine tends to slow down. The governor increases fuel supply. Engine power increases. Speed returns towards the required operating speed. When electrical load decreases: Engine speed tends to increase. The governor reduces fuel supply. Engine power decreases. Speed returns towards the required operating speed.

3.2 Working Principle of a Governor

  • Most conventional mechanical governors work on the principle of centrifugal force.
  • Rotating masses are mounted on arms connected to a rotating spindle.
  • When rotational speed increases, centrifugal force increases.
  • The governor balls move outward.
  • The sleeve moves vertically.
  • This movement operates a linkage connected to the fuel-control mechanism.
  • Fuel supply is reduced.
  • When speed decreases, the reverse action occurs.

3.3 Centrifugal Force

F_c = m ω² r

Where: F_c = Centrifugal force (N); m = Mass of rotating ball (kg); ω = Angular velocity (rad/s); r = Radius of rotation (m).

Important: Centrifugal force is directly proportional to mass. It is proportional to the square of angular velocity. Doubling rotational speed produces four times the centrifugal force if radius remains constant.

3.4 Main Components of a Centrifugal Governor

  1. Governor balls: Rotating masses that move outward or inward according to speed.
  2. Arms: Connect the balls to the spindle and sleeve arrangement.
  3. Spindle: Rotates with the engine or driving mechanism.
  4. Sleeve: Moves vertically as the balls change position.
  5. Linkage: Transfers sleeve movement to the control mechanism.
  6. Spring or dead weight: Provides the opposing force required for speed regulation, depending on governor design.
  7. Fuel-control mechanism: Adjusts the quantity of fuel supplied to the engine.

3.5 Types of Governors

Governors can be broadly classified into two categories.

A. Centrifugal Governors

These operate using centrifugal force.

Types

  • Watt Governor — One of the simplest centrifugal governors. Consists of two rotating balls attached to arms. The arms are connected to a central spindle. The sleeve moves vertically as the balls move. Primarily uses the weight of the balls to provide the controlling force. Suitable for relatively low-speed applications. Not commonly used in modern marine engines.
  • Porter Governor — Similar to a Watt governor. Contains an additional central dead weight on the sleeve. The dead weight increases the controlling force. Can operate at higher speeds than a basic Watt governor. Its characteristics depend on the ball weight and central load.
  • Proell Governor — A modification of the Porter governor. The balls are attached to the extended lower arms. Provides a different arrangement of ball movement and mechanical advantage. Used as a classical example in governor theory.
  • Hartnell Governor — A spring-loaded centrifugal governor. Uses a helical compression spring. The spring provides the controlling force. Ball movement causes sleeve movement against spring force. Compact construction. Suitable for relatively high-speed applications.
  • Wilson-Hartnell Governor — A modification of the Hartnell governor. Uses a spring-controlled arrangement. Designed to provide improved governing characteristics.

B. Inertia Governors

  • These operate using the inertia force produced by angular acceleration or deceleration.
  • Their operation depends on changes in rotational speed rather than only the centrifugal force associated with steady rotation.
  • They are more sensitive to changes in speed.
  • They are less commonly used than centrifugal governors.

3.6 Electronic Governors

Modern marine diesel engines commonly use electronic governing systems.

Main components

  • Speed sensor — Measures engine RPM. Usually uses a magnetic pickup or another electronic sensing arrangement.
  • Electronic control unit (ECU) — Compares actual engine speed with the desired speed. Calculates the required correction.
  • Actuator — Operates the fuel rack or fuel injection control mechanism.
  • Fuel control mechanism — Changes the fuel quantity supplied to the engine.

Working: The speed sensor detects actual RPM. The ECU compares actual RPM with the set RPM. If speed falls, the ECU commands increased fuel supply. If speed rises, the ECU commands reduced fuel supply. This process continuously regulates engine speed.

3.7 Important Governor Terminology

Key terms

  • Equilibrium Speed: The speed at which the centrifugal force of the balls is balanced by the controlling force. At this condition, the governor balls remain at a fixed position.
  • Mean Equilibrium Speed: The average of the maximum and minimum equilibrium speeds. N = (N₁ + N₂)/2.
  • Governor Range: The difference between maximum and minimum equilibrium speeds. Range = N_max − N_min.
  • Sensitivity: Indicates how effectively a governor responds to small changes in speed. Sensitivity = Mean equilibrium speed / Range of equilibrium speeds.
  • Stability: A governor is stable when its equilibrium speed increases as the radius of rotation of the balls increases. Each radius of rotation should correspond to a definite equilibrium speed.
  • Isochronism: A governor is isochronous when its equilibrium speed remains the same for all radii of rotation. The range of equilibrium speeds is zero. It is an ideal condition in classical governor theory. Perfect isochronism may lead to hunting if the governing system is not adequately damped.
  • Hunting: Continuous fluctuation of governor movement and engine speed around the equilibrium condition. It occurs when the governor repeatedly overcorrects the fuel supply. It may result from excessive sensitivity, delayed response or insufficient damping.
  • Governor Effort: The mean force exerted at the sleeve for a given change in speed.
  • Governor Power: The work done by the governor at the sleeve during a specified displacement. It is equal to governor effort multiplied by sleeve displacement.

4.1 Introduction to Balancing

  • Balancing is the process of correcting the distribution of mass in a rotating or reciprocating component.
  • Its purpose is to reduce unbalanced forces and moments.
  • Improper balancing causes vibration, noise and additional mechanical stress.
  • It also increases bearing loads and may cause premature failure of machine components.

4.2 Causes of Unbalance

  1. Uneven distribution of material.
  2. Manufacturing errors.
  3. Improper machining.
  4. Eccentric mounting of components.
  5. Non-uniform material density.
  6. Wear and tear during operation.
  7. Deposits of dirt, oil or other material on rotating components.
  8. Damage or deformation of rotating parts.

4.3 Effects of Unbalance

  • Excessive machine vibration.
  • Increased bearing loads.
  • Shaft bending.
  • Increased noise.
  • Loosening of foundation bolts.
  • Fatigue failure of components.
  • Reduced machine efficiency.
  • Premature bearing and seal failure.
  • Damage to connected equipment.

4.4 Static Balancing

Definition: Static balancing is achieved when the centre of mass of a rotating body lies on its axis of rotation. In this condition, there is no resultant centrifugal force due to mass eccentricity during rotation. The rotating body does not have a tendency to rotate to a particular heavy position when supported freely on suitable low-friction bearings or knife edges.

4.5 Principle of Static Balancing

  • A rotating body has an unbalanced mass when its centre of gravity does not lie on the axis of rotation.
  • During rotation, this eccentric mass produces a centrifugal force.
  • Balancing is achieved by adding or removing mass at an appropriate location.
  • The objective is to bring the centre of mass onto the axis of rotation.
F = m ω² r

For a system of rotating masses, the condition for static balance is:

Σ m r = 0

In vector form: Σ m r⃗ = 0. This means that the vector sum of the mass-radius products must be zero.

4.6 Methods of Static Balancing

  • Adding a suitable balancing mass opposite the heavy portion.
  • Removing excess material from the heavy portion.
  • Adjusting the position of existing balancing weights.
  • Using a static balancing machine.

4.7 Examples of Static Balancing

  • Small rotating wheels.
  • Fans.
  • Grinding wheels.
  • Pulleys.
  • Rotating discs.
  • Flywheels.

4.8 Dynamic Balancing

Definition: Dynamic balancing is the process of correcting both the resultant centrifugal force and the resultant couple acting on a rotating body. It ensures that the rotating body produces no resultant unbalanced force or unbalanced moment during rotation. It is particularly important for long rotors and rotating shafts.

4.9 Principle of Dynamic Balancing

Dynamic balancing requires two conditions:

Condition 1: Force balance

Σ m r = 0

Condition 2: Couple balance

Σ m r l = 0

Where: m = Mass of rotating component; r = Radius of rotation; l = Axial distance from the reference plane.

4.10 Static vs Dynamic Balancing

Static BalancingDynamic Balancing
Centre of mass lies on the rotation axis.Resultant centrifugal force and couple are zero.
Corrects force unbalance.Corrects force and couple unbalance.
Can be checked with the rotor stationary.Requires consideration of the rotating condition.
Suitable for many thin discs.Essential for long rotors and high-speed shafts.

Important relationship: Every dynamically balanced rigid rotor is statically balanced. A statically balanced rotor is not necessarily dynamically balanced.

4.11 Single-Plane and Two-Plane Balancing

Methods

  • Single-plane balancing — Balancing correction is applied in one transverse plane. Commonly suitable for thin disc-shaped rotors. Corrects static unbalance.
  • Two-plane balancing — Balancing corrections are applied in two separate transverse planes. Corrects both force and couple unbalance. Used for long rotors and high-speed rotating machinery.

4.12 Balancing in Marine Engineering

Balancing is important in:

Marine equipment

  • Marine diesel engine crankshafts.
  • Propeller shafts.
  • Turbocharger rotors.
  • Generator rotors.
  • Pumps.
  • Fans.
  • Compressors.
  • Engine flywheels.
Marine engineering note: Turbocharger rotors operate at very high rotational speeds. Even a small mass unbalance can produce considerable centrifugal force. Proper balancing is therefore essential to prevent bearing damage, excessive vibration and rotor failure.

5.1 Introduction to Mechanical Vibrations

  • Mechanical vibration is the repeated oscillatory motion of a body about its equilibrium position.
  • It occurs when a mechanical system is disturbed from its equilibrium position and experiences restoring forces.
  • The body moves back and forth around its equilibrium position.
  • Vibrations may be desirable or undesirable depending on the application.

Examples

  • Vibration of a diesel engine.
  • Vibration of a ship's hull.
  • Oscillation of a spring-mass system.
  • Vibration of a propeller shaft.
  • Vibration of a rotating pump.
  • Vibration of compressor components.

5.2 Basic Terminology of Vibration

  1. Equilibrium Position: The position at which the resultant force acting on the body is zero. The body remains at rest at this position if no disturbance is applied.
  2. Amplitude: Maximum displacement of a vibrating body from its mean or equilibrium position. Represented by X or A. Measured in metres or millimetres.
  3. Cycle: One complete oscillation of a vibrating body. For example, movement from one extreme position to the opposite extreme and back again.
  4. Time Period: Time required to complete one full cycle. Represented by T. Measured in seconds. T = 1/f.
  5. Frequency: Number of complete vibration cycles occurring per second. Represented by f. Measured in Hertz (Hz). f = 1/T.
  6. Angular Frequency: ω = 2πf. Measured in rad/s. Represents the rate of change of phase angle.
  7. Natural Frequency: The frequency at which a system vibrates freely after being disturbed. It depends on the mass and stiffness of the system. It is an important property of a mechanical system.
  8. Forced Frequency: The frequency at which an external periodic force acts on the system.
  9. Phase: Phase describes the position of a vibrating body within its oscillation cycle. It is commonly expressed in degrees or radians.

5.3 Classification of Mechanical Vibrations

A. Based on the Excitation

Types

  • Free Vibrations — Occur when a system is displaced from equilibrium and then allowed to vibrate without continuous external periodic excitation. The vibration takes place at the system's natural frequency in an undamped ideal system. Energy initially supplied by displacement is responsible for the motion. Example: A mass attached to a spring and pulled before being released.
  • Forced Vibrations — Occur when an external periodic force continuously acts on a system. The vibration frequency is generally related to the excitation frequency. The amplitude depends on excitation frequency, system properties and damping. Example: Vibration caused by an unbalanced rotating motor.

B. Based on Damping

Types

  • Undamped Vibrations — No energy is lost due to damping. The amplitude remains constant in an ideal free-vibration system. This is mainly a theoretical condition.
  • Damped Vibrations — Energy is continuously dissipated during vibration. The amplitude decreases with time in free vibration. Damping may occur due to friction, fluid resistance or material properties.

C. Based on Motion

Types

  • Longitudinal Vibrations — Particles vibrate parallel to the axis of the component. Also called axial vibrations in suitable mechanical systems. Common in rods, shafts and spring systems.
  • Transverse Vibrations — Particles vibrate perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the component. Common in beams, shafts and structural members.
  • Torsional Vibrations — Occur due to angular twisting and untwisting of a shaft. The shaft experiences oscillatory angular displacement about its longitudinal axis. Common in crankshafts, propeller shafts and transmission systems.

5.4 Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM)

Simple Harmonic Motion is a periodic motion in which acceleration is directly proportional to displacement from the equilibrium position and directed towards that position. It is one of the basic mathematical models used to understand mechanical vibration.

x = A sin(ωt + φ)

Where: x = Instantaneous displacement; A = Amplitude; ω = Angular frequency; t = Time; φ = Phase angle.

v = Aω cos(ωt + φ)
a = −Aω² sin(ωt + φ)
a = −ω² x

Important characteristics

  • Maximum displacement occurs at the extreme positions.
  • Velocity is maximum at the equilibrium position.
  • Velocity is zero at the extreme positions.
  • Acceleration is maximum at the extreme positions.
  • Acceleration is zero at the equilibrium position.

5.5 Spring-Mass System

A basic spring-mass system consists of: Mass m; Spring of stiffness k; Supporting structure.

When the mass is displaced and released: The spring generates a restoring force. The mass moves towards equilibrium. Due to inertia, it passes through equilibrium. The spring force reverses direction. The motion continues as oscillation.

According to Hooke's law:

F = −k x

Where: F = Restoring force (N); k = Spring stiffness (N/m); x = Displacement (m).

5.6 Natural Frequency of a Spring-Mass System

For an undamped spring-mass system:

ω_n = √(k / m)
f_n = (1 / 2π) √(k / m)

Where: k = Spring stiffness (N/m); m = Mass (kg); ω_n = Natural angular frequency (rad/s); f_n = Natural frequency (Hz).

Important observations: Increasing stiffness increases natural frequency. Increasing mass decreases natural frequency. A stiffer system vibrates faster. A heavier system generally vibrates slower.

5.7 Types of Damping in Mechanical Systems

Damping is the process of dissipating vibrational energy.

Types

  • Viscous Damping — Occurs when a body moves through a fluid or a damper containing viscous fluid. Damping force is proportional to velocity. F_d = −c ẋ, where c is the damping coefficient. Examples: Hydraulic shock absorbers. Oil-film damping in bearings.
  • Coulomb or Dry-Friction Damping — Occurs due to friction between two contacting surfaces. The damping force is approximately constant in magnitude and opposes the direction of motion. It is commonly found in mechanical joints and sliding components.
  • Structural or Hysteresis Damping — Occurs due to internal friction within a material during cyclic deformation. Some vibration energy is converted into heat. It is associated with material properties.

5.8 Damping Ratio

The damping ratio indicates the amount of damping present compared with critical damping.

ζ = c / c_c

Where: ζ = Damping ratio; c = Actual damping coefficient; c_c = Critical damping coefficient.

Critical damping coefficient for a spring-mass system:

c_c = 2 √(k m)

6.1 Introduction to Resonance

  • Resonance is a condition in which the amplitude of vibration becomes significantly large when the excitation frequency approaches the natural frequency of a system.
  • It occurs when a periodic external force acts at or near a system's natural frequency.
  • At resonance, energy is transferred efficiently from the excitation source to the vibrating system.
  • The vibration amplitude may become dangerously high if damping is insufficient.

6.2 Conditions for Resonance

Resonance occurs when:

f = f_n

Or:

ω = ω_n

Where: f = Excitation frequency; f_n = Natural frequency; ω = Excitation angular frequency; ω_n = Natural angular frequency.

For a real damped system, the peak amplitude generally occurs near, but not exactly at, the undamped natural frequency.

6.3 Resonance in Mechanical Systems

Examples

  • Rotating machinery.
  • Diesel engine crankshafts.
  • Propeller shafts.
  • Turbine rotors.
  • Ship structures.
  • Engine foundations.
  • Pump and compressor systems.

6.4 Effects of Resonance

  • Excessive vibration amplitude.
  • High dynamic stresses.
  • Fatigue failure.
  • Bearing damage.
  • Loosening of fasteners.
  • Shaft cracking.
  • Noise generation.
  • Damage to connected machinery.
  • Structural damage.

6.5 Resonance in Marine Engineering

Marine cases

  • Main Engine — Marine diesel engines generate periodic excitation forces and moments. If these excitation frequencies coincide with the natural frequencies of engine components, excessive vibration may occur.
  • Propeller Shaft — Propeller shafts can experience torsional vibration. Excitation may come from engine torque fluctuations and propeller loading. Resonance can produce high alternating shaft stresses.
  • Ship Hull — The ship structure has natural vibration frequencies. Engine, propeller and wave-induced excitation can produce structural vibration. Excessive structural vibration may affect equipment, crew comfort and structural integrity.
  • Turbocharger — A turbocharger rotor operates at high rotational speed. Rotor-bearing systems have natural frequencies. Operation near critical speeds can produce excessive vibration.

6.6 Methods of Avoiding Resonance

  1. Change the operating speed.
  2. Increase or decrease system stiffness.
  3. Change the mass distribution.
  4. Increase damping.
  5. Modify the shaft dimensions.
  6. Use vibration isolation systems.
  7. Avoid operating continuously near critical speeds.
  8. Use appropriate balancing techniques.
  9. Improve the rigidity of the foundation.
  10. Use vibration monitoring systems.

6.7 Role of Damping in Resonance

  • Damping reduces the amplitude of vibration.
  • It dissipates energy from the vibrating system.
  • Higher damping generally reduces the peak amplitude near resonance.
  • It helps prevent excessive vibration and mechanical damage.
  • Damping does not necessarily eliminate resonance; it limits its severity.

6.8 Magnification Factor

The magnification factor indicates how much the vibration amplitude increases under forced excitation compared with the corresponding static deflection.

For a single-degree-of-freedom viscously damped system:

M.F. = 1 / √[(1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²]

Where: r = ω/ω_n = Frequency ratio; ζ = Damping ratio; ω = Excitation frequency; ω_n = Natural frequency.

Important: At low frequency ratios, the magnification factor is generally close to 1. Near resonance, the magnification factor increases. Increasing damping reduces the resonance peak. At very high frequency ratios, the response generally decreases.

6.9 Vibration Isolation

  • Vibration isolation is a technique used to reduce the transmission of vibration from one component to another.
  • It is achieved using suitable springs, rubber mounts, resilient supports or other isolation systems.

Applications

  • Marine engine mounting.
  • Pump mounting.
  • Compressor mounting.
  • Generator foundations.
  • Sensitive electronic equipment.
Important: Vibration isolation is different from damping. Isolation reduces the transmission of vibration, whereas damping dissipates vibrational energy.

7.1 Introduction to Critical Speed

  • Critical speed is the rotational speed at which the excitation frequency of a rotating shaft coincides with one of its lateral natural frequencies.
  • At this speed, the shaft may experience excessive lateral vibration.
  • The shaft may bend and rotate around a displaced centreline.
  • This phenomenon is associated with resonance and is commonly called shaft whirling.

7.2 What is Shaft Whirling?

  • Shaft whirling is the lateral movement of a rotating shaft about its original axis.
  • It occurs due to unbalanced centrifugal forces and the dynamic response of the shaft.
  • As shaft speed approaches a critical speed, lateral vibration can become large.
  • If the vibration becomes excessive, the shaft may contact surrounding components, damage bearings or fail due to fatigue.

7.3 Causes of Critical Speed

  1. Mass unbalance in the rotating shaft.
  2. Shaft flexibility.
  3. Shaft length and diameter.
  4. Bearing stiffness.
  5. Bearing support conditions.
  6. Mass of attached components.
  7. Rotor geometry.
  8. Shaft material properties.
  9. Distribution of rotating mass.

7.4 Natural Frequency of a Rotating Shaft

  • A shaft behaves like an elastic mechanical system.
  • When it is displaced laterally, its elasticity produces a restoring force.
  • This results in lateral vibration.
  • The natural frequency depends on shaft stiffness and the mass supported by the shaft.

For a simplified single-degree-of-freedom rotor model:

ω_n = √(k / m)

Where: k = Equivalent lateral stiffness; m = Equivalent supported mass.

The corresponding critical speed in RPM is:

N_c = (60 / 2π) ω_n

7.5 Critical Speed of a Shaft Carrying a Single Disc

For a simply supported shaft carrying a concentrated disc at its centre, assuming the shaft mass is negligible:

ω_n = √(g / δ)

Where: g = Acceleration due to gravity (m/s²); δ = Static deflection of the shaft due to the disc weight (m).

Critical speed in RPM:

N_c = (60 / 2π) √(g / δ)

This is the basic Rayleigh relationship for the simplified single-disc case.

7.6 Factors Affecting Critical Speed

Factors

  • Shaft Diameter — Increasing shaft diameter generally increases bending stiffness. Increased stiffness generally raises the critical speed.
  • Shaft Length — Increasing shaft length generally decreases bending stiffness. This generally lowers the critical speed.
  • Rotor Mass — Increasing supported mass generally reduces natural frequency. Therefore, critical speed generally decreases.
  • Bearing Stiffness — Bearing stiffness influences the overall lateral stiffness of the rotor system. Changes in bearing stiffness can shift critical speeds.
  • Shaft Material — Young's modulus affects shaft bending stiffness. A higher Young's modulus generally increases stiffness for the same geometry.

7.7 Whirling Speed and Critical Speed

  • Whirling speed is the rotational speed at which the shaft exhibits lateral whirl.
  • Critical speed is the speed at which resonance occurs between rotational excitation and the lateral natural frequency.
  • In basic rotor-dynamics problems, these terms are often used interchangeably.

7.8 Methods of Avoiding Critical Speed Problems

  1. Design shafts with sufficient stiffness.
  2. Maintain proper rotor balancing.
  3. Select suitable bearing arrangements.
  4. Avoid prolonged operation near critical speeds.
  5. Use appropriate shaft diameters.
  6. Reduce unnecessary rotor mass.
  7. Use vibration monitoring equipment.
  8. Ensure correct alignment.
  9. Follow the manufacturer's operating speed restrictions.
  10. Use suitable damping and support arrangements.

7.9 Critical Speed in Marine Machinery

Marine cases

  • Main Propulsion Shaft — The propulsion shaft may experience lateral and torsional vibration. Its critical speeds must be considered during design. Proper shaft alignment and bearing support are essential.
  • Turbocharger Shaft — Turbocharger rotors operate at very high speeds. Their rotor-bearing systems may have multiple critical speeds. Proper balancing and manufacturer-specified operating limits are essential.
  • Diesel Generator — Rotating generator components can experience vibration near their natural frequencies. Proper balancing and alignment reduce vibration problems.
  • Pumps and Compressors — High-speed rotating shafts may encounter lateral critical speeds. Excessive vibration can damage seals, bearings and impellers.

  1. A flywheel stores rotational kinetic energy and reduces cyclic fluctuations in speed.
  2. A governor regulates the mean speed of an engine when the load changes.
  3. Flywheels do not control the fuel supply.
  4. Governors do not act as significant energy-storage devices.
  5. Centrifugal force is proportional to the square of rotational speed.
  6. The mass moment of inertia depends on the distribution of mass around the axis of rotation.
  7. Static balancing eliminates resultant centrifugal force.
  8. Dynamic balancing eliminates both resultant centrifugal force and resultant centrifugal couple.
  9. A dynamically balanced rigid rotor is also statically balanced.
  10. A statically balanced rotor is not necessarily dynamically balanced.
  11. Vibration is oscillatory motion about an equilibrium position.
  12. Natural frequency depends on the stiffness and mass of the system.
  13. Increasing stiffness generally increases natural frequency.
  14. Increasing mass generally decreases natural frequency.
  15. Free vibration occurs after an initial disturbance without continuous periodic excitation.
  16. Forced vibration occurs due to an external excitation.
  17. Torsional vibration involves angular twisting and untwisting of a shaft.
  18. Resonance occurs when excitation frequency approaches natural frequency.
  19. Damping dissipates vibrational energy.
  20. Critical damping provides the fastest return to equilibrium without oscillation in a standard second-order system.
  21. Overdamped systems return to equilibrium without oscillation but more slowly.
  22. Underdamped systems oscillate with decreasing amplitude.
  23. Critical speed is associated with resonance in rotating shaft systems.
  24. Shaft whirling is lateral movement of a rotating shaft.
  25. Rotor balancing is especially important in high-speed turbochargers.
  26. Marine propulsion systems must be designed to avoid harmful torsional and lateral vibration.
  27. Excessive vibration can cause fatigue cracks, bearing failures and mechanical damage.
  28. Vibration monitoring is an important part of marine machinery condition monitoring.

11.2 Final Chapter Summary

Machine Dynamics deals with the forces, energy and vibration associated with moving machine components. The six major topics can be remembered as follows:

Six major topics

  • 1. Flywheel — Stores and releases energy to reduce cyclic speed fluctuations.
  • 2. Governor — Regulates mean engine speed according to load changes.
  • 3. Balancing — Eliminates unbalanced forces and couples in rotating components.
  • 4. Mechanical Vibrations — Studies oscillatory motion, frequency, amplitude and natural frequency.
  • 5. Resonance and Damping — Resonance can increase vibration amplitude; damping dissipates vibration energy.
  • 6. Critical Speed — Rotational speed at which a rotor's excitation frequency matches a lateral natural frequency.
My recommendation for your GME preparation: Give extra attention to flywheel versus governor, static versus dynamic balancing, types of vibration, damping ratio, resonance, and critical speed. These concepts have direct applications in marine diesel engines, turbochargers, generators and propulsion shafting.

12.2 Graphs and Engineering Data Interpretation

  • Line graphs show trends over time (e.g., temperature rise, speed variation).
  • Bar charts compare discrete categories (e.g., fuel consumption by engine).
  • Scatter plots reveal correlation between variables (e.g., load vs. efficiency).
  • Pie charts show proportions (e.g., energy loss distribution).
  • Always label axes with quantity and unit; choose suitable scale; note outliers.
  • Slope of a graph = rate of change; area under curve = accumulated quantity.

Pressure p = F / A

Work W = F d

12.4 Heat Balance and Mass Balance

  • Heat balance: Energy input = Energy output + Losses. For an engine: Fuel energy = Brake power + Cooling loss + Exhaust loss + Friction and other losses.
  • Mass balance: Mass in = Mass out + Accumulation. For steady state, Mass in = Mass out.
  • Energy balance equation: Σ Q_in + W_in = Σ Q_out + W_out + ΔE_system.

Example – Engine heat balance

If 100 kW fuel energy is supplied, and 35 kW is converted to brake power, 25 kW is lost to cooling, 30 kW to exhaust, and 10 kW to friction/radiation, then 35 + 25 + 30 + 10 = 100 kW (balanced).

12.5 Basic Statistics, Mean, Standard Deviation and Measurement Uncertainty

Mean x̄ = Σ xᵢ / n
Standard deviation σ = √[ Σ (xᵢ − x̄)² / (n − 1) ]
Standard error SE = σ / √n
Relative uncertainty = (Uncertainty / Measured value) × 100%

Where: xᵢ = individual measurement, n = number of measurements, x̄ = arithmetic mean, σ = sample standard deviation.

Key points

  • Mean gives the central tendency of data.
  • Standard deviation measures the spread or dispersion of measurements.
  • Measurement uncertainty quantifies the reliability of a measurement.
  • Report results as: measured value ± uncertainty (e.g., 25.4 ± 0.2 mm).
  • Combine uncertainties: for addition/subtraction, add absolute uncertainties; for multiplication/division, add relative uncertainties.

Marine engineering applications

  • Engine performance trials (power, fuel consumption, efficiency).
  • Heat balance sheets for main engine and auxiliary systems.
  • Mass balance in fuel, cooling water and lubrication circuits.
  • Measurement of shaft power, torque and speed with uncertainty.
  • Statistical quality control of spare parts and maintenance data.
