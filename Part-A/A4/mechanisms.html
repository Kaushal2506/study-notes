2.1 Kinematics

  • Kinematics is the branch of mechanics that deals with the motion of bodies without considering the forces responsible for producing that motion.
  • It studies: displacement, velocity, acceleration, direction of motion, and relative motion between machine components.
  • Example: Studying how fast a piston moves inside a cylinder without considering the gas pressure acting on it.

2.2 Kinematic Link

A kinematic link is a resistant body or a group of bodies connected together to transmit motion and forces.

  • Links are the basic building blocks of mechanisms.
  • A link must have sufficient strength and rigidity to maintain the required relative motion.

Types of links

1. Rigid Link

  • A link that does not undergo appreciable deformation while transmitting motion.
  • The distance between any two points remains practically constant.
  • Examples: Crankshaft, connecting rod, gear wheel, engine frame.

2. Flexible Link

  • A link that can deform but transmits motion or force through tension.
  • It generally cannot transmit significant compressive force.
  • Examples: Belt, rope, chain, cable.

3. Fluid Link

  • A link in which motion or force is transmitted through a fluid under pressure.
  • Examples: Hydraulic systems, hydraulic brakes, hydraulic actuators.

2.3 Kinematic Pair

  • A kinematic pair is formed when two links are connected together in such a way that their relative motion is constrained.
  • The connection determines the type and direction of relative motion between the links.
  • Kinematic pairs are used to form mechanisms.

Examples: Piston and cylinder, shaft and bearing, nut and screw, two meshing gears.

2.4 Classification of Kinematic Pairs

Kinematic pairs are classified based on the type of contact, relative motion and nature of constraint.

A. Based on Nature of Contact

1. Lower Pair

  • A lower pair is a kinematic pair in which two links have surface contact during relative motion.
  • The contact takes place over an area rather than at a single point or along a line.
  • It generally provides smooth and stable motion.
Kinematic pairExample
Sliding pairPiston and cylinder
Turning pairCrankshaft and bearing

2. Higher Pair

  • A higher pair is a kinematic pair in which two links have point or line contact during relative motion.
  • Contact occurs over a small region compared with a lower pair.
  • Friction and contact stresses can be significant.

Examples: Cam and follower, meshing gear teeth, ball bearing, roller bearing, wheel rolling on a surface.

B. Based on Relative Motion

1. Sliding Pair

  • One link slides relative to another.
  • The motion is linear or translational.
  • Example: Piston moving inside a cylinder.

2. Turning Pair

  • One link rotates relative to another around a fixed axis.
  • Example: Crank rotating about a crankshaft bearing.

3. Rolling Pair

  • One link rolls over another.
  • Example: Wheel rolling over a surface or roller bearing.

4. Screw Pair

  • One link rotates and translates relative to another in a definite relationship.
  • Rotation and translation are connected by the screw thread.
  • Example: Nut moving along a threaded screw.

5. Spherical Pair

  • One link can rotate in different directions relative to another around a common centre.
  • Example: Ball-and-socket joint.

C. Based on Nature of Constraint

1. Completely Constrained Motion

  • Relative motion between two links is restricted to one definite direction or type.
  • The motion is independent of the direction of applied force.
  • Example: A rectangular shaft sliding inside a matching rectangular guide; a properly guided piston moving linearly inside a cylinder.

2. Incompletely Constrained Motion

  • A link can move in more than one possible direction.
  • The relative motion is not restricted to a single definite path.
  • Example: A circular shaft in a circular hole can rotate and slide axially.

3. Successfully Constrained Motion

  • The motion is not completely constrained by the shape of the connecting surfaces alone.
  • An external force or arrangement ensures the required motion.
  • Examples: A vertical shaft in a footstep bearing, where gravity maintains contact; a cam and follower maintained in contact by a spring.

2.5 Degrees of Freedom (DOF)

  • Degree of Freedom is the number of independent parameters required to define the position or configuration of a mechanism completely.
  • It indicates how many independent movements are possible in a mechanism.
  • It is also called mobility.

Examples:

  • A body moving freely in a plane has 3 DOF: translation along X, translation along Y, rotation about an axis perpendicular to the plane.
  • A freely moving rigid body in three-dimensional space has 6 DOF: translation along X, Y, Z and rotation about X, Y, Z.

Importance in mechanisms:

  • A mechanism with one degree of freedom requires only one independent input to determine its complete motion.
  • Most conventional four-bar and slider-crank mechanisms have one degree of freedom.

2.6 Kinematic Chain

  • A kinematic chain is an assembly of links connected by kinematic pairs in such a way that their relative motions are constrained.
  • It forms the basic arrangement from which a mechanism can be developed.
  • When one link of a kinematic chain is fixed, it becomes a mechanism.

Example: A slider-crank chain consists of four links: frame or cylinder, crank, connecting rod, slider or piston.

Types of Kinematic Chains

1. Four-Bar Chain

  • Consists of four links connected by four turning pairs.
  • It is one of the simplest closed kinematic chains.
  • It can produce rotary and oscillating motion.

2. Single Slider-Crank Chain

  • Consists of four links.
  • Contains three turning pairs and one sliding pair.
  • Converts rotary motion into reciprocating motion or vice versa.
  • Used in internal combustion engines and reciprocating pumps.

3. Double Slider-Crank Chain

  • Consists of four links.
  • Contains two turning pairs and two sliding pairs.
  • Used in mechanisms such as elliptical trammels and Oldham couplings.

2.7 Grashof's Law

Grashof's law is used to determine whether a four-bar linkage can have a completely rotating link.

It states:

For a planar four-bar linkage, at least one link can make a complete revolution if:

S + L ≤ P + Q

Where:

  • S = Shortest link.
  • L = Longest link.
  • P = One of the remaining links.
  • Q = Other remaining link.

Types of four-bar mechanisms based on Grashof's law:

  • Grashof chain: At least one link can rotate completely, subject to the link arrangement and choice of fixed link.
  • Non-Grashof chain: No link can make a complete revolution when the linkage remains assembled.
  • Change-point condition: When S + L = P + Q, the mechanism can reach a change-point configuration.
Remember: Grashof's law is particularly important for identifying crank-rocker and double-crank arrangements.

3.1 Introduction

  • A four-bar mechanism is a closed kinematic chain consisting of four rigid links connected by four turning pairs.
  • It is commonly used to convert rotary motion into oscillating motion or to transmit motion between two rotating or oscillating components.
  • It is one of the most widely used mechanisms in mechanical engineering.

3.2 Four Links of a Four-Bar Mechanism

A standard four-bar mechanism contains four links:

1. Fixed Link (Frame)

  • The stationary link of the mechanism.
  • Supports the rotating and oscillating links.
  • Provides the reference for all relative motion.

2. Crank

  • A link connected to the frame through a turning pair.
  • Capable of making a complete 360-degree rotation in a crank mechanism.

3. Coupler (Connecting Link)

  • Connects the crank to the rocker.
  • Transmits motion between them.
  • It generally undergoes complex planar motion, combining translation and rotation.

4. Rocker (Lever)

  • A link connected to the frame through a turning pair.
  • Moves through a limited angular range.
  • Does not complete a full revolution in a conventional crank-rocker arrangement.

3.3 Types of Four-Bar Mechanisms

1. Double Crank Mechanism (Drag-Link Mechanism)

  • Both links connected to the fixed link are capable of complete rotation.
  • The coupler connects the two rotating cranks.
  • It is also called a drag-link mechanism.

Applications: Coupling mechanisms, mechanical transmission systems, some specialized rotary machinery.

2. Crank-Rocker Mechanism

  • One link connected to the frame rotates completely.
  • The other link connected to the frame oscillates through a limited angle.
  • It converts continuous rotary motion into oscillating motion.

Applications: Pumping mechanisms, mechanical linkages, wiper mechanisms, oscillating machinery.

3. Double Rocker Mechanism

  • Both links connected to the fixed link oscillate.
  • Neither link makes a complete revolution.
  • It is used when limited angular motion is required at both ends.

Applications: Mechanical linkages, vehicle suspension linkages, steering linkages.

3.4 Important Terms in Four-Bar Mechanisms

1. Crank

  • A link that rotates through 360 degrees around a fixed pivot.

2. Rocker

  • A link that oscillates between two angular limits.

3. Coupler

  • The intermediate link connecting the crank and rocker.

4. Fixed Link

  • The stationary link that acts as the reference.

5. Dead-Centre Position

  • A configuration in which the mechanism reaches a limiting or toggle position.
  • At certain dead-centre positions, the crank and coupler become collinear.
  • These positions may be associated with zero instantaneous output velocity or a change in the direction of output motion, depending on the mechanism.

3.5 Toggle Mechanism

  • A toggle mechanism is a linkage that uses a near-straight or aligned position of links to generate a large mechanical advantage.
  • When two links approach collinearity, a relatively small input force can generate a large output force.
  • It is used in clamping and pressing mechanisms.

Applications: Toggle clamps, press machines, locking mechanisms, mechanical gripping devices.

3.6 Mechanical Advantage

  • Mechanical advantage is the ratio of output force to input force.
  • It indicates how effectively a mechanism multiplies the applied force.
MA = Output ForceInput Force
  • Near a toggle position, mechanical advantage can become very high in an ideal mechanism.
  • In practical mechanisms, friction, deformation and clearance limit the achievable advantage.

3.7 Applications of Four-Bar Mechanisms

Applications

  • Automobile windshield wipers.
  • Pumping machinery.
  • Mechanical presses.
  • Steering mechanisms.
  • Industrial machines.
  • Linkages used in marine auxiliary machinery.
Important point: A four-bar mechanism is useful whenever a particular motion needs to be transmitted or converted using simple interconnected links.

4.1 Introduction

  • A slider-crank mechanism is a four-link mechanism consisting of three turning pairs and one sliding pair.
  • It converts rotary motion into reciprocating linear motion or reciprocating motion into rotary motion.
  • It is one of the most important mechanisms in mechanical engineering.
  • It is extensively used in internal combustion engines, steam engines, compressors and reciprocating pumps.

4.2 Components of a Slider-Crank Mechanism

A typical slider-crank mechanism consists of four main links.

1. Frame or Cylinder

  • The stationary part of the mechanism.
  • Supports the crankshaft and provides a guide for the slider.
  • Maintains alignment of the moving components.

2. Crank

  • A rotating link connected to the crankshaft.
  • Converts rotary input into movement of the connecting rod.
  • Rotates continuously through 360 degrees.

3. Connecting Rod

  • Connects the crank to the slider or piston.
  • Transmits force and motion between them.
  • Undergoes both angular and translational movement.
  • Experiences tensile and compressive forces during operation.

4. Slider or Piston

  • Moves back and forth along a straight path.
  • It is connected to the connecting rod.
  • In an engine, it moves inside the cylinder.

4.3 Working Principle

The slider-crank mechanism works through the interaction of its rotating crank and connecting rod.

Rotary motion to reciprocating motion:

  1. The crankshaft starts rotating.
  2. The crank pin moves along a circular path.
  3. The connecting rod transmits motion from the crank to the piston.
  4. The piston moves linearly inside the cylinder.
  5. As the crank continues rotating, the piston reverses its direction at the two extreme positions.
  6. This produces continuous reciprocating motion.

Reciprocating motion to rotary motion:

  1. A force acts on the piston.
  2. The piston pushes or pulls the connecting rod.
  3. The connecting rod applies force to the crank pin.
  4. The force produces torque on the crankshaft.
  5. The crankshaft rotates continuously with the help of the flywheel and the engine's other power strokes.

4.4 Important Terms

1. Stroke

  • The distance travelled by the piston between its two extreme positions.
  • It is equal to twice the crank radius.
L = 2r

Where: L = Stroke length, r = Crank radius.

2. Top Dead Centre (TDC)

  • The extreme position of the piston nearest to the cylinder head in a conventional vertical engine.
  • At this position, the piston reverses its direction of motion.

3. Bottom Dead Centre (BDC)

  • The extreme position of the piston farthest from the cylinder head.
  • The piston again reverses its direction at this position.

4. Crank Radius

  • The distance between the centre of the crankshaft and the centre of the crank pin.
  • It determines half of the piston stroke.

5. Connecting Rod Length

  • The distance between the centre of the crank pin and the centre of the piston pin.
  • It is generally greater than the crank radius.

6. Crank Angle

  • The angle made by the crank with the line of stroke.
  • It determines the instantaneous position of the crank and piston.

7. Obliquity of Connecting Rod

  • The angle made by the connecting rod with the line of stroke.
  • It changes continuously as the crank rotates.
  • It affects piston velocity, acceleration and side thrust.

8. Dead Centre

  • The position at which the crank and connecting rod are collinear in the ordinary in-line slider-crank mechanism.
  • The piston is at an extreme position.
  • The piston velocity is zero at the exact dead-centre position.

4.5 Slider-Crank Mechanism in a Marine Diesel Engine

  • A marine diesel engine uses a slider-crank arrangement to convert the reciprocating motion of the piston into rotary motion of the crankshaft.
  • During combustion, high-pressure gases act on the piston crown.
  • The piston transfers this force to the connecting rod.
  • The connecting rod applies force to the crankshaft through the crank pin.
  • The crankshaft converts the applied force into rotational torque.
  • The rotating crankshaft drives the propeller through the propulsion system.
Additional marine engineering point:
  • Large two-stroke crosshead marine engines use a crosshead between the piston rod and connecting rod.
  • The crosshead guides the piston rod and helps prevent connecting-rod angular forces from being transmitted directly to the piston and cylinder liner.
  • The crosshead guides also carry the side thrust generated by the connecting rod.
  • This arrangement is particularly important in large slow-speed marine diesel engines.

This section is particularly important because it explains how the movement of a rotating crank produces the reciprocating movement of a piston.

5.1 Crank Motion

  • The crank rotates around a fixed centre called the crankshaft centre.
  • Its crank pin follows a circular path.
  • The crank rotates through 360 degrees during one complete revolution.
  • The crank's angular position is measured using the crank angle.

Angular Displacement

  • Angular displacement is the angle through which a rotating body moves about a fixed axis.
  • It is generally measured in radians or degrees.
1 revolution = 360° = 2π radians

Angular Velocity

  • Angular velocity is the rate of change of angular displacement with respect to time.
  • It indicates how quickly the crank rotates.
ω = dθdt

Where: ω = Angular velocity (rad/s), θ = Angular displacement (radians), t = Time (seconds).

For constant crank speed:

ω = 2πN60

Where: N = Crankshaft speed in RPM.

Angular Acceleration

  • Angular acceleration is the rate of change of angular velocity with respect to time.
  • It indicates how quickly the rotational speed changes.
α = dωdt

Where: α = Angular acceleration (rad/s²), ω = Angular velocity (rad/s).

If the crankshaft rotates at constant speed:

α = 0

5.2 Connecting Rod Motion

  • The connecting rod is connected between the crank pin and piston pin.
  • It transmits force between the piston and crankshaft.
  • It undergoes combined angular and translational motion.
  • The connecting rod angle changes continuously during crankshaft rotation.

Important characteristics:

  1. Its crank-pin end follows a circular path.
  2. Its piston-pin end follows a straight-line path.
  3. The connecting rod continuously changes its inclination.
  4. Its angular velocity varies during crankshaft rotation.
  5. It experiences tensile and compressive forces depending on the operating condition.

5.3 Piston Displacement

Piston displacement is the linear distance travelled by the piston from a selected reference position.

For a slider-crank mechanism:

  • Let r = Crank radius.
  • Let l = Connecting rod length.
  • Let θ = Crank angle measured from the line of stroke.
  • Let x = Distance of the piston from the crankshaft centre along the line of stroke.

From the geometry of the mechanism:

x = r cos θ + √(l² − r² sin² θ)

The piston displacement measured from TDC is:

s = (r + l) − x

Therefore:

s = r(1 − cos θ) + l − √(l² − r² sin² θ)

This is the exact geometrical expression for piston displacement.

For a long connecting rod compared with the crank radius, an approximate expression is:

s ≈ r(1 − cos θ) + r²4l(1 − cos 2θ)

Understanding the equation:

  • The first term represents the main harmonic component of piston motion.
  • The second term represents the correction due to connecting rod angularity.
  • This correction causes piston motion to deviate from simple harmonic motion.

5.4 Piston Velocity

  • Piston velocity is the rate of change of piston displacement with respect to time.
  • It indicates how fast the piston moves inside the cylinder.
  • Piston velocity is not constant during one crankshaft revolution.

For a constant crank angular velocity:

v = dsdt

Using the approximate displacement equation:

v ≈ rω (sin θ + r2l sin 2θ)

Where: v = Piston velocity (m/s), r = Crank radius (m), ω = Angular velocity (rad/s), l = Connecting rod length (m).

Important observations:

  • At TDC, piston velocity is zero.
  • At BDC, piston velocity is zero.
  • Piston velocity is maximum somewhere between TDC and BDC.
  • The maximum velocity does not necessarily occur at exactly 90 degrees crank angle.
  • Piston velocity increases with crankshaft speed.

5.5 Piston Acceleration

  • Piston acceleration is the rate of change of piston velocity with respect to time.
  • It indicates how rapidly the piston velocity changes.
  • Piston acceleration is important for determining inertia forces in an engine.

Using the approximate displacement expression:

a ≈ rω² (cos θ + rl cos 2θ)

Where: a = Piston acceleration (m/s²), r = Crank radius (m), ω = Angular velocity (rad/s), l = Connecting rod length (m).

Important observations:

  • Piston acceleration is maximum near TDC.
  • It is generally negative and has a large magnitude near BDC.
  • Acceleration is not constant during piston movement.
  • Acceleration increases approximately with the square of crankshaft speed.

Marine engineering importance

  • Piston acceleration produces inertia forces.
  • These forces act on the piston, connecting rod, crankshaft and bearings.
  • At high engine speeds, inertia forces become significant.
  • Proper balancing and adequate strength of engine components are essential.

5.6 Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM) of a Piston

  • Simple Harmonic Motion is a type of oscillatory motion in which acceleration is directly proportional to displacement from the mean position and is directed towards the mean position.
  • A piston connected to an infinitely long connecting rod would approximately execute SHM when driven by a uniformly rotating crank.
  • In a real engine, the connecting rod has finite length, so piston motion is not perfectly simple harmonic.

Important SHM equations:

x = A cos θ
v = −Aω sin θ
a = −Aω² cos θ

Where: A = Amplitude, ω = Angular velocity, θ = Angular position.

Remember:
  • At the extreme positions, velocity is zero and acceleration magnitude is maximum.
  • At the mean position, velocity magnitude is maximum and acceleration is zero.

6.1 Introduction

  • Velocity analysis determines the speed and direction of movement of different links in a mechanism.
  • Acceleration analysis determines the rate of change of velocity of the moving links.
  • These analyses help engineers understand the dynamic behaviour of machines.
  • They are important in the design of engines, pumps, compressors and transmission systems.

6.2 Types of Velocity

1. Linear Velocity

  • Rate of change of linear displacement.
  • Measured in metres per second (m/s).
v = dsdt

2. Angular Velocity

  • Rate of change of angular displacement.
  • Measured in radians per second (rad/s).
ω = dθdt

3. Relative Velocity

  • Velocity of one point or body measured with respect to another moving point or body.
VB/A = VB − VA

Where: VB/A = Velocity of B relative to A, VB = Absolute velocity of B, VA = Absolute velocity of A.

6.3 Linear Velocity of a Rotating Body

When a body rotates about a fixed axis, every point on the body moves in a circular path.

The linear velocity of a point is:

v = rω

Where: v = Linear velocity, r = Distance from the axis of rotation, ω = Angular velocity.

Important points:

  • Linear velocity is always tangential to the circular path.
  • It is perpendicular to the radius joining the point to the centre of rotation.
  • Points farther from the axis have higher linear velocity when angular velocity is the same.

6.4 Types of Acceleration

Acceleration in mechanisms is generally classified into two main components.

1. Tangential Acceleration

  • Tangential acceleration acts along the tangent to the circular path.
  • It is responsible for changing the magnitude of linear velocity.
  • It occurs when the angular velocity of a rotating body changes.
at = rα

Where: at = Tangential acceleration (m/s²), r = Radius (m), α = Angular acceleration (rad/s²).

2. Radial or Centripetal Acceleration

  • Radial acceleration acts towards the centre of rotation.
  • It is responsible for changing the direction of linear velocity.
  • It exists even when the body rotates at constant angular velocity.
ar = v²r = rω²

Where: ar = Radial acceleration (m/s²), v = Linear velocity (m/s), r = Radius (m).

Remember the difference:

Tangential AccelerationRadial Acceleration
Acts along tangentActs towards centre
Changes speedChanges direction
at = rαar = rω²
Depends on angular accelerationExists during circular motion

The total acceleration of a point in circular motion is the vector sum of its tangential and radial acceleration components.

6.5 Relative Velocity Analysis of a Mechanism

  • Relative velocity analysis is used when two or more connected links move simultaneously.
  • The velocity of one point is determined using the known velocity of another point and their relative motion.

For two points A and B on the same rigid link:

VB = VA + VB/A

For a rotating rigid link:

VB/A = ωAB × AB

In magnitude:

VB/A = ωAB · AB
Important rule: The relative velocity of two points on a rigid rotating link is perpendicular to the line joining those points.

Example in a connecting rod:

  • The crank pin moves with a known velocity.
  • The piston pin moves along the cylinder axis.
  • Relative velocity between the two ends is perpendicular to the connecting rod.
  • These conditions help determine the angular velocity of the connecting rod and the piston velocity.

6.6 Relative Acceleration Analysis

  • Relative acceleration analysis is used to determine the acceleration of one point relative to another point on a moving link.
  • It is particularly useful for connecting rods and other links undergoing combined translation and rotation.

For two points A and B on the same rigid link:

aB = aA + aB/A

The relative acceleration consists of two components:

1. Normal or Radial Component

  • Acts along the link towards the centre of rotation.
  • It is responsible for changing the direction of relative velocity.
an = ω²r

2. Tangential Component

  • Acts perpendicular to the link.
  • It is responsible for changing the magnitude of relative velocity.
at = αr

Therefore:

aB/A = an + at
Important: Relative acceleration analysis is a vector analysis. The directions of the components must be considered before combining them.

6.7 Velocity and Acceleration in Slider-Crank Mechanism

In a slider-crank mechanism:

  • The crank rotates about a fixed axis.
  • The connecting rod undergoes angular and translational motion.
  • The piston moves in a straight line.
  • All three moving components have different velocity and acceleration characteristics.

Velocity characteristics:

  1. Crank-pin velocity is tangential to its circular path.
  2. Piston velocity is along the line of stroke.
  3. Connecting-rod angular velocity changes continuously.
  4. Piston velocity is zero at TDC and BDC.
  5. Piston velocity is generally maximum between the two dead centres.

Acceleration characteristics:

  1. Crank-pin acceleration has radial and tangential components.
  2. For constant crankshaft speed, the crank pin has only radial acceleration.
  3. Connecting-rod acceleration has normal and tangential relative components.
  4. Piston acceleration changes continuously during the stroke.
  5. Piston acceleration is particularly high near TDC and BDC.

7. Practical Applications in Marine Engineering

Understanding mechanisms is important for marine engineers because several shipboard machines use these principles.

1. Marine Diesel Engine

  • Slider-crank mechanism.
  • Converts reciprocating piston motion into crankshaft rotation.
  • Transmits combustion forces.

2. Reciprocating Air Compressor

  • Uses a slider-crank arrangement.
  • Converts crankshaft rotation into piston reciprocation.
  • Compresses air inside the cylinder.

3. Reciprocating Pump

  • Uses a crank and connecting rod.
  • Produces suction and delivery strokes.
  • Used for transferring liquids.

4. Engine Valve Mechanism

  • Uses cams, followers and rocker arms.
  • Converts camshaft rotation into valve movement.
  • Controls the opening and closing of engine valves.

5. Crankshaft and Connecting Rod Assembly

  • Transmits power from piston to crankshaft.
  • Experiences alternating forces and inertia loads.
  • Requires proper lubrication and bearing maintenance.

10. Final Revision – Key Points to Memorise

Fundamental concepts
  • Mechanism transmits or transforms motion.
  • Kinematic link is a component of a mechanism.
  • Kinematic pair connects two links.
  • Kinematic chain is a combination of interconnected links.
  • Fixing one link of a kinematic chain produces a mechanism.
Kinematic pairs
  • Lower pair = Surface contact.
  • Higher pair = Point or line contact.
  • Sliding pair = Linear motion.
  • Turning pair = Rotational motion.
  • Screw pair = Combined rotational and translational motion.
  • Rolling pair = Rolling motion.
Four-bar mechanism
  • Four links and four turning pairs.
  • Main links: Frame, crank, coupler and rocker.
  • Types: Double crank, crank-rocker and double rocker.
  • Grashof's law determines the possibility of complete rotation.
Slider-crank mechanism
  • Four links.
  • Three turning pairs and one sliding pair.
  • Converts rotary motion into reciprocating motion.
  • Stroke = Twice the crank radius.
  • Piston reverses direction at TDC and BDC.
Crank and connecting rod
  • Crank rotates continuously.
  • Connecting rod transmits motion and force.
  • Connecting rod undergoes combined motion.
  • Piston motion is approximately SHM for a very long connecting rod.
Velocity and acceleration
  • Linear velocity = rω.
  • Tangential acceleration = rα.
  • Radial acceleration = rω².
  • Tangential acceleration changes speed.
  • Radial acceleration changes direction.
  • Piston velocity is zero at TDC and BDC.

  1. Kinematic pairs: Understand lower pairs, higher pairs and the different types of constrained motion.
  2. Degrees of freedom: Understand why a slider-crank mechanism has one degree of freedom.
  3. Four-bar mechanisms: Learn the crank, rocker, coupler and Grashof's law.
  4. Slider-crank mechanism: Understand its construction, working and application in a marine diesel engine.
  5. TDC and BDC: Understand their positions and their relationship with crankshaft rotation.
  6. Piston motion: Understand why piston velocity is zero at dead centres.
  7. Connecting rod: Understand its angular movement and the forces transmitted through it.
  8. Velocity and acceleration: Understand radial and tangential components.
  9. Marine application: Understand how combustion pressure on a piston produces crankshaft torque.
My recommendation: Before moving to the next Theory of Machines topic, make sure you can draw and label a four-bar mechanism and a slider-crank mechanism from memory. These two diagrams will help you understand a large part of the subject.
