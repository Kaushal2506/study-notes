1.1 Introduction to Lathe Machine

  1. A lathe machine is a machine tool in which the workpiece rotates about its axis while a cutting tool moves against it to remove material.
  2. It is commonly called the mother of machine tools because it can perform a wide variety of machining operations.
  3. The lathe is primarily used for producing cylindrical, conical and threaded components.
  4. It can also perform facing, grooving, knurling, drilling and boring operations.
  5. The cutting tool is generally stationary in its cutting position while the workpiece rotates.

1.2 Main Parts of a Lathe Machine

  1. Bed – Main supporting structure; supports headstock, tailstock and carriage; provides accurate guideways; made of cast iron for vibration damping.
  2. Headstock – Located at the left-hand end; houses main spindle, bearings and speed-changing mechanism; provides rotational motion to the workpiece.
  3. Main Spindle – Rotating shaft housed inside the headstock; transmits power to the workpiece; holds chuck, faceplate or other work-holding devices.
  4. Tailstock – Located on the right-hand side; supports the free end of long workpieces using a centre; can hold drilling, reaming and tapping tools; can be moved along the bed.
  5. Carriage – Supports and moves the cutting tool along the lathe bed. Its components include:
    • Saddle – H-shaped component that slides along the bed.
    • Cross-slide – Moves the cutting tool perpendicular to the spindle axis.
    • Compound rest – Provides angular movement and is used for taper turning.
    • Tool post – Holds and positions the cutting tool.
    • Apron – Contains mechanisms for controlling carriage and feed movements.
  6. Lead Screw – Long threaded shaft used mainly for producing accurate screw threads; transmits motion to the carriage through the half-nut mechanism; provides synchronized movement between spindle and carriage.
  7. Feed Rod – Transmits power from the headstock to the carriage feed mechanism; used for automatic longitudinal and cross feeding during ordinary turning; reduces wear on the lead screw.
  8. Chuck – Work-holding device fitted to the spindle; holds the workpiece firmly during machining. Common types:
    • Three-jaw universal chuck.
    • Four-jaw independent chuck.
    • Collet chuck.
    • Magnetic chuck.

1.3 Types of Lathe Machines

  1. Speed Lathe – Simple lathe with a high-speed spindle; generally no power feed mechanism; used for wood turning, polishing and spinning.
  2. Engine Lathe – Common conventional lathe machine; has power feed and different spindle speeds; used for turning, facing, threading, drilling and other operations.
  3. Bench Lathe – Small lathe mounted on a workbench; used for small and precision components.
  4. Tool Room Lathe – High-accuracy lathe; used for manufacturing tools, dies, gauges and precision components.
  5. Capstan and Turret Lathe – Designed for repetitive production; multiple cutting tools can be mounted and used in sequence; suitable for mass production.
  6. CNC Lathe – Computer Numerical Control lathe; operations controlled by programmed instructions; provides high accuracy, repeatability and productivity.

1.4 Important Lathe Operations

  1. Straight Turning – Reducing the diameter of a cylindrical workpiece uniformly along its length; workpiece rotates while the cutting tool moves parallel to the axis of rotation; produces a cylindrical surface; used for shafts, pins, rods and cylindrical machine components.
  2. Step Turning – Produces different diameters at different sections of a workpiece; each section has a specified length and diameter; used for stepped shafts, axles and machine spindles.
  3. Facing – Producing a flat surface at the end of a cylindrical workpiece; cutting tool moves perpendicular to the axis of rotation; commonly performed to obtain a smooth and flat end surface and to bring the workpiece to the required length.
  4. Taper Turning – Produces a conical surface by gradually changing the diameter along the length of the workpiece; diameter decreases or increases uniformly along the axis; used for machine tool centres, tapered shafts and pipe fittings.

  5. Thread Cutting – Producing helical grooves on the external or internal surface of a workpiece; tool moves longitudinally at a synchronized rate with workpiece rotation; lead screw maintains the required relationship between spindle rotation and tool movement. Applications: bolts, nuts, studs, threaded shafts, lead screws, pipe threads.
  6. Parting-Off – Cutting a finished component from the remaining workpiece; a narrow cutting tool is fed radially into the rotating workpiece; also called cut-off operation.
  7. Grooving – Produces a narrow recess or groove on the external or internal surface; a specially shaped grooving tool is used; used for circlip grooves, oil grooves and sealing-ring grooves.
  8. Knurling – A metal-forming operation; a hardened knurling roller is pressed against the rotating workpiece; produces a regular rough pattern on the surface; generally does not remove material in the form of chips; improves grip and prevents slipping. Types: straight, diamond, diagonal. Applications: handles, knobs, hand-operated adjustment screws, tool grips.
  9. Drilling on a Lathe – Drill bit held in the tailstock; workpiece rotates while the drill advances along the axis; produces a cylindrical hole.
  10. Boring on a Lathe – Enlarges an already existing hole; boring tool held in the tool post; improves diameter accuracy and alignment.
  11. Reaming on a Lathe – Finishes an already drilled or bored hole; reamer held in the tailstock; produces a more accurate hole with improved surface finish.
  12. Form Turning – Specially shaped cutting tool used to produce a required profile; shape of the tool is transferred to the workpiece; used for curved profiles, grooves and decorative shapes.

2.1 Drilling

  1. Drilling is a machining operation used to produce a circular cylindrical hole in a solid workpiece.
  2. It is generally performed using a rotating drill bit.
  3. The drill moves axially into the workpiece.
  4. A twist drill is the most commonly used drilling tool.
  5. Drilling is commonly performed on drilling machines, lathes and CNC machining centres.

2.2 Main Parts of a Twist Drill

  1. Shank – The portion used to hold the drill in the machine; may be straight or tapered.
  2. Flutes – Helical grooves along the drill body; allow chips to escape from the hole; help deliver cutting fluid to the cutting zone.
  3. Cutting Lips – Two main cutting edges at the drill point; remove material during drilling.
  4. Chisel Edge – Located at the centre of the drill point; connects the two cutting lips; pushes material rather than cutting efficiently; requires considerable thrust force.
  5. Web – Central portion connecting the two flutes; provides strength to the drill.
  6. Margin – Narrow raised surface along the drill body; guides the drill inside the hole.

2.3 Important Drill Angles

  • Point Angle: The angle between the two main cutting lips when viewed from the side. A common general-purpose drill point angle for metals is approximately 118°.
  • Helix Angle: The angle of the helical flute. It influences chip removal and cutting performance.
  • Lip Clearance Angle: Provides clearance behind the cutting edge and prevents excessive rubbing.
  • Chisel Edge Angle: Relates to the geometry of the chisel edge at the drill point.

2.4 Types of Drilling Machines

  1. Portable drilling machine.
  2. Sensitive drilling machine.
  3. Upright or pillar drilling machine.
  4. Radial drilling machine.
  5. Gang drilling machine.
  6. Multiple-spindle drilling machine.
  7. Deep-hole drilling machine.
  8. CNC drilling machine.

2.5 Important Drilling Operations

  1. Drilling – Producing a new hole in a solid material.
  2. Reaming – Finishing and accurately sizing an existing hole.
  3. Boring – Enlarging and correcting an existing hole using a single-point cutting tool.
  4. Counterboring – Enlarging the upper portion of an existing hole cylindrically; produces a flat-bottomed recess; used to accommodate bolt heads and socket-head screws.
  5. Countersinking – Produces a conical enlargement at the entrance of a hole; used for accommodating countersunk screw heads; common included angles: 60°, 82°, 90° and 120°.
  6. Spot Facing – Produces a flat circular seating surface around a hole; used when the original surface is rough or uneven; provides proper seating for nuts, bolts and washers.
  7. Tapping – Produces internal screw threads inside a drilled or bored hole; a tool called a tap is used; used for manufacturing threaded holes.
  8. Trepanning – Produces large holes by cutting an annular groove; removes material in the form of a cylindrical core; saves material and cutting power in large-hole applications.
  9. Centre Drilling – Produces a small centre hole at the end of a workpiece; used to support a workpiece between centres during turning.

2.6 Boring

  1. Boring is the operation of enlarging an already existing hole using a single-point cutting tool.
  2. It is used to correct the hole diameter, straightness, concentricity and alignment.
  3. It can be performed on lathes, boring machines and machining centres.
  4. Unlike drilling, boring does not normally produce the initial hole.
  5. It is particularly useful for machining large holes that cannot be conveniently produced using a standard drill.

Types of Boring:

  • Horizontal Boring: The boring spindle or bar operates horizontally.
  • Vertical Boring: The workpiece is generally mounted on a rotating table, and the boring operation is performed vertically.
  • Line Boring: Used to machine a series of aligned holes.
  • Back Boring: Enlarges a hole from the rear side when access is available.

Applications of Boring: Engine cylinder blocks, pump housings, bearing housings, large machine frames, marine engine components, cylinder liners and other precision bores.

2.7 Reaming

  1. Reaming is a finishing operation used to improve the dimensional accuracy and surface finish of an existing hole.
  2. A multi-edge cutting tool called a reamer is used.
  3. It removes a very small amount of material.
  4. Reaming is generally performed after drilling or boring.
  5. It produces a hole of more accurate and uniform diameter.
  6. Reaming cannot normally correct a severely misaligned or badly positioned hole.

Types of Reamers: Hand reamer, machine reamer, straight-fluted reamer, spiral-fluted reamer, adjustable reamer, taper reamer, shell reamer.

3.1 Milling

  1. Milling is a machining process in which material is removed using a rotating multi-point cutting tool.
  2. The cutting tool is called a milling cutter.
  3. The workpiece is generally fed against the rotating cutter.
  4. Each cutting tooth removes a small amount of material.
  5. Milling is used for producing flat surfaces, slots, grooves, gears and complex profiles.

3.2 Working Principle of Milling

  • The milling cutter rotates at a specified speed.
  • The workpiece is clamped on the machine table.
  • The table moves the workpiece against the rotating cutter.
  • Each cutter tooth removes material during its engagement with the workpiece.
  • Repeated cutting action produces the required shape.

3.3 Main Parts of a Milling Machine

  1. Base – Main foundation; supports the entire machine; helps absorb vibrations.
  2. Column – Vertical supporting structure; houses the driving mechanism and spindle-related components.
  3. Knee – Supports the saddle and table; moves vertically along the column.
  4. Saddle – Mounted on the knee; provides crosswise movement to the table.
  5. Table – Holds the workpiece or vice; provides longitudinal movement.
  6. Spindle – Holds and rotates the milling cutter directly or through a tool holder.
  7. Arbor – A shaft used to mount milling cutters on horizontal milling machines.

3.4 Types of Milling Machines

  1. Horizontal Milling Machine – Spindle axis is horizontal; cutter is mounted on an arbor; suitable for slab milling, side milling and heavy material removal.
  2. Vertical Milling Machine – Spindle axis is vertical; commonly uses end mills and face mills; suitable for slots, pockets, faces and profiles.
  3. Universal Milling Machine – Table can swivel to different angles; suitable for helical grooves, spiral gears and angular machining.
  4. CNC Milling Machine – Controlled by computer programs; provides high dimensional accuracy; can produce complex profiles automatically.
  5. Bed-Type Milling Machine – Worktable is mounted directly on the machine bed; used for heavy-duty machining operations.

3.5 Important Milling Operations

  1. Plain or Slab Milling – Produces a flat horizontal surface; cutter axis is parallel to the machined surface; cylindrical milling cutter is used; suitable for large flat surfaces.
  2. Face Milling – Produces flat surfaces using a cutter with cutting edges on its face and periphery; cutter axis is generally perpendicular to the machined surface; used for large flat surfaces with good finish.
  3. End Milling – Uses an end mill having cutting edges on its end and periphery; used for producing slots, pockets, steps and profiles; commonly performed on vertical milling machines.
  4. Side Milling – Used for machining vertical or side surfaces; cutter has cutting teeth on its circumference and sides.
  5. Angular Milling – Produces surfaces at an angle other than 90°; an angular milling cutter is used; used for V-grooves and angular surfaces.
  6. Form Milling – Produces irregular or curved profiles; a specially shaped cutter is used; the profile of the cutter is transferred to the workpiece.
  7. Slot Milling – Produces slots or grooves; commonly performed using end mills or slotting cutters.
  8. Straddle Milling – Two side milling cutters are mounted on the same arbor; two parallel vertical surfaces are machined simultaneously.
  9. Gang Milling – Two or more milling cutters are mounted on a common arbor; multiple surfaces can be machined in one pass; increases productivity.
  10. Gear Milling – Used to produce gear teeth; special gear cutters are used; common in conventional gear manufacturing.

3.6 Up Milling and Down Milling

ParameterUp MillingDown Milling

Key points

  • Up Milling (Conventional): Cutter rotates opposite to feed; chip thickness starts from zero and increases; cutting force tends to lift workpiece; more suitable for machines with backlash.
  • Down Milling (Climb): Cutter rotates same direction as feed; chip thickness starts at maximum and decreases; cutting force presses workpiece against table; generally better surface finish; requires rigid machine and proper backlash control.

3.7 Shaping Machine

  1. A shaping machine is used to produce flat, horizontal, vertical and angular surfaces.
  2. It uses a single-point cutting tool.
  3. The cutting tool moves in a reciprocating motion over the stationary or slowly fed workpiece.
  4. Material is generally removed during the forward stroke.
  5. The return stroke is normally idle and takes place faster in a quick-return mechanism.

3.8 Main Parts of a Shaping Machine

  • Base: Supports the entire machine.
  • Column: Main vertical structure of the machine.
  • Ram: Reciprocates horizontally and carries the tool head.
  • Tool Head: Holds the cutting tool.
  • Clapper Box: Allows the tool to lift slightly during the return stroke, preventing unnecessary rubbing.
  • Table: Supports and holds the workpiece.
  • Cross Rail: Supports the table and allows vertical adjustment.
  • Vice: Holds the workpiece securely.

3.9 Working Principle of Shaping Machine

  1. The workpiece is clamped on the machine table.
  2. The cutting tool is fitted in the tool head.
  3. The ram moves forward and backward.
  4. During the forward stroke, the tool removes material.
  5. During the return stroke, the tool generally does not cut.
  6. After each stroke, a small feed is given to the workpiece.
  7. Repeated strokes produce the required flat surface.

3.10 Quick Return Mechanism

  • Used in shaping and slotting machines.
  • Makes the return stroke faster than the cutting stroke.
  • Reduces idle time and improves productivity.
  • Common mechanisms include crank and slotted-link mechanisms and hydraulic quick-return systems.

3.11 Types of Shaping Machines

  1. Horizontal shaper.
  2. Vertical shaper.
  3. Universal shaper.
  4. Crank-type shaper.
  5. Hydraulic shaper.

3.12 Applications of Shaping

  • Producing flat surfaces.
  • Producing vertical and angular surfaces.
  • Cutting slots and grooves.
  • Producing keyways.
  • Machining dovetail surfaces.
  • Producing small quantities of simple machine components.

4.1 Grinding

  1. Grinding is an abrasive machining process used to remove small amounts of material from a workpiece.
  2. A rotating grinding wheel is used as the cutting tool.
  3. The grinding wheel consists of numerous hard abrasive particles bonded together.
  4. Each abrasive particle acts like a tiny cutting edge.
  5. Grinding is commonly used for achieving high dimensional accuracy and excellent surface finish.
  6. It is especially useful for machining hardened materials.

4.2 Working Principle of Grinding

  • The grinding wheel rotates at high speed.
  • The workpiece is brought into contact with the wheel.
  • Abrasive grains remove tiny amounts of material.
  • Material is removed in the form of very small chips.
  • The operation produces an accurate surface with fine finish.

4.3 Main Elements of a Grinding Wheel

  1. Abrasive Grains – Actual cutting particles; remove material from the workpiece; common abrasives include aluminium oxide, silicon carbide, diamond and cubic boron nitride.
  2. Bond – Holds the abrasive grains together; provides strength to the grinding wheel; controls the rate at which worn abrasive grains are released.
  3. Pores – Small empty spaces between abrasive grains; provide space for chip accommodation; allow coolant to reach the grinding zone; help prevent excessive loading and heat generation.

4.4 Types of Abrasive Materials

  1. Aluminium Oxide (Al₂O₃) – Commonly used for grinding steels and ferrous materials; suitable for carbon steel, alloy steel and high-speed steel; tough and durable abrasive.
  2. Silicon Carbide (SiC) – Harder and more brittle than aluminium oxide; commonly used for cast iron, non-ferrous metals and non-metallic materials; suitable for brass, aluminium and ceramics.
  3. Diamond – One of the hardest known materials; used for grinding hard and brittle materials; commonly used for carbide tools, ceramics and glass; not generally suitable for grinding ferrous materials at high temperatures because diamond reacts with iron.
  4. Cubic Boron Nitride (CBN) – Very hard synthetic abrasive; suitable for hardened steels and superalloys; commonly used in precision grinding of ferrous materials.

4.5 Grinding Wheel Specification

A grinding wheel specification generally includes: abrasive type, grain size, grade, structure and bond type.

  1. Grain Size – Indicates the size of abrasive particles; coarse grains for heavy material removal; fine grains for finishing operations; higher grit number generally means smaller abrasive particles.
  2. Grade – Indicates the strength with which the bond holds the abrasive grains; soft-grade wheels release grains more easily; hard-grade wheels hold abrasive grains more strongly. Grade does not indicate the hardness of the abrasive material itself.
  3. Structure – Indicates the spacing between abrasive grains; open structure has larger spaces; dense structure has smaller spaces; open structures help accommodate chips and reduce loading.
  4. Bond – Holds the abrasive particles together. Common bond types: vitrified, resinoid, rubber, shellac, silicate, metal.

4.6 Types of Grinding Machines

  1. Surface Grinding Machine – Used to produce flat surfaces; workpiece generally held on a magnetic chuck or suitable fixture; commonly used for precision flat components.
  2. Cylindrical Grinding Machine – Used for grinding external cylindrical surfaces; workpiece generally rotates between centres or is held in a chuck; used for shafts, axles and cylindrical components.
  3. Internal Grinding Machine – Used for grinding internal cylindrical surfaces; small grinding wheels are used inside the bore; suitable for accurate bearing bores and internal surfaces.
  4. Centreless Grinding Machine – Used for grinding cylindrical workpieces without holding them between centres or in a chuck; workpiece is supported between a grinding wheel and a regulating wheel; suitable for mass production.
  5. Tool and Cutter Grinding Machine – Used for sharpening and reconditioning cutting tools; suitable for drills, milling cutters and other tools.

4.7 Grinding Operations

  • Surface grinding – produces flat surfaces.
  • Cylindrical grinding – produces accurate external cylindrical surfaces.
  • Internal grinding – finishes internal holes.
  • Centreless grinding – grinds cylindrical components without conventional workholding.
  • Form grinding – produces specific profiles.
  • Thread grinding – produces accurate screw threads.
  • Creep-feed grinding – removes relatively large amounts of material in a single deep pass using specially designed equipment.

4.8 Grinding Wheel Dressing and Truing

Dressing vs Truing

  • Dressing – Removing loaded material and worn abrasive grains from the grinding wheel surface; exposes fresh, sharp cutting edges; restores the cutting ability of the wheel.
  • Truing – Restoring the correct geometrical shape of the grinding wheel; ensures that the wheel runs concentrically and has the required profile.
Remember: Dressing = Restoring cutting ability. Truing = Restoring geometrical shape.

4.9 Surface Finishing Processes

  1. Honing – Abrasive finishing process; uses abrasive stones mounted on a rotating and reciprocating tool; commonly used for finishing internal cylindrical surfaces; produces a characteristic cross-hatch pattern; improves roundness, straightness and surface finish; used for engine cylinders and hydraulic cylinders.
  2. Lapping – Precision finishing process; uses fine abrasive particles mixed with a suitable liquid or paste; abrasive slurry placed between the workpiece and a lap; very small amounts of material removed; produces extremely fine surface finish and high dimensional accuracy. Applications: valve seats, precision gauges, mechanical seals, optical components, precision mating surfaces.
  3. Polishing – Removes minor surface irregularities; improves appearance and smoothness; uses abrasive belts, wheels, compounds or polishing materials; commonly used for decorative and functional finishing.
  4. Buffing – Uses a soft rotating wheel and fine polishing compounds; mainly improves surface appearance and brightness; generally removes very little material; often performed after polishing.
  5. Superfinishing – Precision abrasive finishing process; uses fine abrasive stones with controlled pressure and oscillating motion; removes surface peaks and improves surface quality; commonly used for bearing surfaces, crankshafts and precision shafts.
  6. Sand Blasting – Uses high-velocity abrasive particles directed towards a surface; removes rust, scale, paint and contaminants; produces a roughened surface suitable for coating and painting.
  7. Shot Peening – Uses small spherical shots directed at a metal surface; produces compressive residual stresses in the surface layer; improves fatigue resistance and helps reduce crack initiation; a surface treatment rather than a conventional material-removal finishing operation.

5.1 Cutting Speed

  1. Cutting speed is the relative speed between the cutting edge and the workpiece surface at the cutting zone.
  2. It is the speed at which material is removed from the workpiece.
  3. It is generally expressed in metres per minute (m/min).
  4. Cutting speed depends on workpiece material, cutting tool material, machining operation and cutting conditions.
  5. Excessively high cutting speed can increase temperature and tool wear.
V = π D N1000

Where V = cutting speed in m/min, D = workpiece diameter in mm, N = spindle speed in rpm, π = 3.1416.

N = 1000 Vπ D
Important: Larger workpiece diameter at the same RPM produces higher cutting speed. Higher RPM at the same diameter produces higher cutting speed.

5.2 Spindle Speed

  • Spindle speed is the number of revolutions completed by the spindle in one minute.
  • It is expressed in revolutions per minute (rpm).
  • It determines how fast the workpiece or cutting tool rotates.
  • Example: If a lathe spindle rotates at 500 rpm, it completes 500 revolutions in one minute.

5.3 Feed

  1. Feed is the distance travelled by the cutting tool relative to the workpiece during a specified cutting movement.
  2. It determines how quickly fresh material is presented to the cutting tool.
  3. It is generally expressed in mm/revolution for turning.
  4. Feed affects chip thickness, material removal rate and surface finish.

  • Higher feed increases material removal rate.
  • Higher feed generally increases cutting forces.
  • Higher feed can produce a rougher surface finish.
  • Lower feed generally improves surface finish, within suitable operating conditions.

5.4 Depth of Cut

  1. Depth of cut is the perpendicular distance between the original workpiece surface and the final machined surface.
  2. It is generally expressed in millimetres.
  3. It determines the thickness of material removed in a single pass.
  4. Greater depth of cut generally increases cutting force and power requirements.
t = D₁ − D₂2

Where t = depth of cut in mm, D₁ = initial diameter, D₂ = final diameter. The division by 2 is necessary because the diameter reduces on both sides of the workpiece.

5.5 Material Removal Rate (MRR)

Material Removal Rate is the volume of material removed per unit time. It is an important measure of machining productivity.

For turning:

MRR = π D N f t

Where D = workpiece diameter in mm, N = spindle speed in rpm, f = feed in mm/revolution, t = depth of cut in mm. Unit: mm³/min.

For drilling:

MRR = π D²4 f N

Where D = drill diameter in mm, f = feed in mm/revolution, N = spindle speed in rpm.

For milling:

MRR = w × d × F

Where w = width of cut in mm, d = depth of cut in mm, F = table feed in mm/min.

6.1 Cutting Tool

  1. A cutting tool is used to remove material from a workpiece during machining.
  2. It must possess adequate hardness, toughness and wear resistance.
  3. It must retain its hardness at elevated temperatures.
  4. It should resist chemical reactions with the workpiece material.
  5. It should withstand cutting forces without excessive deformation or fracture.

6.2 Properties of a Good Cutting Tool Material

  1. High Hardness – Must be harder than the workpiece material; helps the tool penetrate and remove material.
  2. Hot Hardness – Ability to retain hardness at high temperatures; important because machining generates significant heat.
  3. Wear Resistance – Ability to resist material loss during continuous cutting.
  4. Toughness – Ability to withstand impact and mechanical shock without breaking.
  5. Thermal Stability – Ability to withstand temperature changes without losing performance.
  6. Chemical Stability – Ability to resist chemical reactions with the workpiece and surrounding environment.

6.3 Types of Cutting Tool Materials

  1. High-Speed Steel (HSS) – Alloy steel containing tungsten, molybdenum, chromium and vanadium; retains hardness at relatively high temperatures; tough and resistant to shock; used for drills, taps, reamers and milling cutters.
  2. Cemented Carbide – Manufactured from tungsten carbide particles bonded with cobalt; provides high hardness and wear resistance; can operate at higher cutting speeds than HSS; widely used in industrial turning, milling and boring.
  3. Ceramic Tools – Made from aluminium oxide and silicon nitride; have high hot hardness and wear resistance; suitable for high-speed machining under appropriate conditions; generally more brittle than carbide.
  4. Cubic Boron Nitride (CBN) – Extremely hard synthetic cutting tool material; excellent wear resistance; suitable for machining hardened steels and hard ferrous materials; commonly used in precision finishing.
  5. Diamond Tools – Extremely high hardness and abrasion resistance; used for machining non-ferrous materials, composites and abrasive materials; not generally used for conventional high-temperature cutting of ferrous metals because of chemical interaction with iron.

6.4 Tool Wear

  1. Tool wear is the gradual loss of cutting tool material during machining.
  2. It occurs because of friction, heat, mechanical forces and chemical reactions.
  3. Tool wear reduces cutting efficiency and dimensional accuracy.
  4. Excessive tool wear can result in poor surface finish and tool failure.
  5. Cutting conditions, tool material, workpiece material and cutting fluid affect tool wear.

6.5 Types of Tool Wear

  1. Flank Wear – Occurs on the flank surface of the cutting tool (the surface facing the newly machined workpiece surface); caused mainly by friction and abrasion between the tool and workpiece; one of the most common forms of tool wear; excessive flank wear reduces dimensional accuracy and surface finish.
  2. Crater Wear – Occurs on the rake face of the cutting tool; caused mainly by high temperature, diffusion and interaction with flowing chips; a crater-shaped depression develops on the rake face; weakens the cutting edge.
  3. Notch Wear – Occurs near the depth-of-cut line; often caused by workpiece surface scale, oxidation and local abrasion; can lead to premature cutting edge failure.
  4. Nose Wear – Occurs at the tool nose; affects the surface finish and dimensional accuracy of the component; can result from abrasion, adhesion and excessive cutting forces.
  5. Chipping – Small pieces of cutting tool material break away from the cutting edge; usually caused by mechanical shock, vibration or excessive cutting forces; common with brittle cutting tool materials.
  6. Plastic Deformation – Occurs when the cutting edge deforms under high temperature and pressure; the tool loses its original geometry; common when cutting temperature exceeds the tool material's strength limits.
  7. Thermal Cracking – Cracks develop due to repeated heating and cooling of the cutting tool; common in interrupted cutting and certain milling operations; can cause sudden tool failure.

6.6 Mechanisms of Tool Wear

  1. Abrasion – Hard particles or hard constituents in the workpiece scratch and wear the cutting tool; produces gradual material loss.
  2. Adhesion – Workpiece material sticks to the cutting tool surface; during chip movement, some tool material may be pulled away; can result in built-up edge formation.
  3. Diffusion – At high temperatures, atoms move between the tool and workpiece materials; can weaken the cutting tool surface; particularly important at high cutting speeds.
  4. Oxidation – Chemical reaction between tool material and oxygen at elevated temperatures; can cause wear of the cutting tool.
  5. Fatigue – Repeated mechanical or thermal loading causes damage; can lead to cracks and chipping.

6.7 Built-Up Edge (BUE)

  1. Built-up edge is the accumulation of workpiece material on the cutting edge of a cutting tool.
  2. It generally occurs under conditions of high friction and adhesion.
  3. It is common at relatively low to medium cutting speeds, depending on the workpiece and tool combination.
  4. It changes the effective geometry of the cutting tool.
  5. It can result in poor surface finish and dimensional inaccuracies.
  6. It may periodically break away and damage the machined surface.

Methods to reduce BUE

  • Use an appropriate higher cutting speed.
  • Use suitable cutting fluid.
  • Use a sharp cutting tool.
  • Select suitable tool geometry.
  • Reduce excessive friction.

6.8 Tool Life

  1. Tool life is the period for which a cutting tool performs satisfactorily before it must be replaced or reconditioned.
  2. It is generally measured in minutes of actual cutting time.
  3. Tool life depends on cutting speed, feed, depth of cut, workpiece material and cutting tool material.
  4. Higher cutting speeds generally reduce tool life.

Taylor's Tool Life Equation:

V Tⁿ = C

Where V = cutting speed, T = tool life, n = tool life exponent, C = constant for a particular tool-workpiece combination and cutting conditions.

  • Higher cutting speed generally results in shorter tool life.
  • The exponent n depends on tool and workpiece materials.
  • The equation is an empirical relationship used under specified machining conditions.
  • It is commonly used to estimate the relationship between cutting speed and tool life.

6.9 Cutting Fluids

  1. Cutting fluids are liquids or gases supplied to the machining zone during cutting operations.
  2. They are used to control heat generation, reduce friction and improve machining performance.
  3. They are also called coolants or metalworking fluids, although their functions may extend beyond cooling.
  4. Cutting fluids are especially important in operations where high temperatures and friction occur.

6.10 Functions of Cutting Fluids

  1. Cooling – Removes heat generated during machining; prevents excessive temperature rise; helps maintain dimensional accuracy; reduces thermal damage to the workpiece.
  2. Lubrication – Reduces friction between the tool, chip and workpiece; reduces cutting forces; helps reduce tool wear; can improve surface finish.
  3. Chip Removal – Helps flush chips away from the cutting zone; prevents chips from interfering with machining; reduces the possibility of chip recutting.
  4. Corrosion Protection – Protects machine components and workpieces from rust and corrosion when properly formulated.
  5. Improved Surface Finish – Reduces friction and built-up edge formation under suitable conditions; helps achieve a better surface finish.
  6. Increased Tool Life – Reduces thermal and mechanical stresses on cutting tools; helps control tool wear.

6.11 Types of Cutting Fluids

  1. Straight Cutting Oils – Mineral oils or specially formulated oils used without dilution; provide excellent lubrication; commonly used in tapping, threading and certain heavy-duty cutting operations; generally have lower cooling capacity than water-based fluids.
  2. Soluble Oils – Oil-based concentrates mixed with water to form an emulsion; provide both cooling and lubrication; commonly used in general machining operations.
  3. Synthetic Fluids – Usually water-based fluids containing chemical additives without conventional mineral-oil base; provide good cooling; commonly used in grinding and high-speed machining applications.
  4. Semi-Synthetic Fluids – Contain a lower proportion of oil than conventional soluble-oil emulsions; provide a balance of cooling and lubrication; used in a wide range of machining operations.
  5. Gaseous Coolants – Air, compressed air and other gases may be used for cooling and chip removal; useful in selected dry or near-dry machining applications.

6.12 Methods of Applying Cutting Fluids

  1. Flood Cooling: A large quantity of coolant is continuously supplied to the cutting zone.
  2. Mist Cooling: Coolant is supplied as fine droplets mixed with air.
  3. High-Pressure Cooling: Coolant is delivered at high pressure directly to the cutting zone.
  4. Through-Tool Cooling: Coolant flows through internal passages in the cutting tool.
  5. Minimum Quantity Lubrication (MQL): A very small quantity of lubricant is delivered in an air stream.

6.13 Dry Machining

  1. Dry machining is performed without supplying conventional cutting fluid.
  2. It reduces coolant consumption and disposal requirements.
  3. It can reduce environmental and maintenance costs.
  4. It is suitable for certain materials, tools and machining conditions.
  5. Heat management and tool selection are particularly important in dry machining.

6.14 Difference Between Cooling and Lubrication

CoolingLubrication
Removes heatReduces friction
Controls temperatureReduces tool-chip friction
Helps prevent thermal damageHelps reduce wear
Often provided by water-based fluidsOften provided by oil-based fluids
Remember: A cutting fluid may perform both cooling and lubrication, but some fluids are better at one function than the other.

7.2 Machining and Maintenance of Marine Machinery

  1. Cylinder Liner Maintenance – Cylinder liners may require honing or reconditioning to restore the required surface condition. Honing produces a cross-hatch pattern that helps retain lubricating oil.
  2. Crankshaft Maintenance – Crankshaft journals may require precision grinding during reconditioning. Proper journal diameter and surface finish are essential for bearing performance.
  3. Pump Shaft Maintenance – Turning and grinding are used during shaft manufacturing and repair. Accurate shaft dimensions help maintain alignment and proper bearing operation.
  4. Valve Maintenance – Valve seats may require grinding or lapping. Proper seating ensures effective sealing and reduces leakage.
  5. Bearing Maintenance – Bearing surfaces require appropriate dimensional accuracy and surface finish. Poor surface quality may result in friction, overheating and premature failure.
  6. Flange and Pipe Connections – Drilling and facing are used to manufacture flange connections. Accurate bolt-hole positioning and flat mating surfaces help ensure proper sealing.

7.3 Workshop Safety During Machining

General Safety Rules

  • Always wear suitable safety goggles or face protection.
  • Avoid loose clothing, jewellery and loose sleeves near rotating machinery.
  • Tie back long hair.
  • Never wear gloves near rotating spindles, chucks or other rotating parts because they can become entangled.
  • Ensure the workpiece is securely clamped before starting the machine.
  • Remove the chuck key immediately after tightening the workpiece.
  • Never touch rotating workpieces or cutting tools.
  • Stop the machine before measuring the workpiece.
  • Remove chips using a brush or suitable chip hook, never with bare hands.
  • Never attempt to stop a rotating spindle by hand.
  • Use machine guards wherever provided.
  • Ensure adequate lighting and ventilation.
  • Keep the workshop floor clean and free from oil spills.
  • Use the correct cutting speed and feed.
  • Stop the machine before making adjustments or changing tools.
Lathe Safety: Never wear gloves while operating a lathe. Do not measure a rotating workpiece. Ensure the chuck and workpiece are properly secured. Keep hands away from rotating components.
Grinding Safety: Inspect the grinding wheel for cracks or damage. Perform a ring test where applicable and in accordance with the wheel manufacturer's instructions. Ensure the wheel speed does not exceed its rated maximum speed. Use a proper wheel guard. Wear a face shield and safety goggles. Stand clear of the wheel plane during starting. Do not apply excessive pressure.
Drilling Safety: Secure the workpiece in a vice or suitable fixture. Do not hold small workpieces by hand. Remove chips with a brush. Stop the spindle before changing the drill bit.

8.7 Important Points to Remember

Lathe Machine

  • Lathe is commonly called the mother of machine tools.
  • Workpiece rotates and the cutting tool is fed against it.
  • Three-jaw chuck is generally self-centering.
  • Four-jaw chuck has independently adjustable jaws.
  • Facing produces a flat end surface.
  • Straight turning produces a cylindrical surface.
  • Taper turning produces a conical surface.
  • Knurling produces a rough pattern without conventional chip removal.
  • Thread cutting produces helical grooves.
  • Lead screw is primarily used for thread cutting.

Drilling, Boring and Reaming

  • Drilling produces a new hole.
  • Boring enlarges an existing hole.
  • Reaming improves hole accuracy and surface finish.
  • Twist drill is the most common drilling tool.
  • A common general-purpose twist drill point angle is 118°.
  • Flutes help remove chips.
  • Tapping produces internal threads.
  • Counterboring produces a cylindrical recess.
  • Countersinking produces a conical recess.
  • Spot facing produces a flat seating surface around a hole.

Milling and Shaping

  • Milling uses a rotating multi-point cutter.
  • Shaping uses a reciprocating single-point tool.
  • Up milling: chip thickness increases from zero to maximum.
  • Down milling: chip thickness decreases from maximum to zero.
  • Down milling generally provides better surface finish.
  • Horizontal milling uses a horizontal spindle.
  • Vertical milling uses a vertical spindle.
  • Quick-return mechanism reduces idle time.
  • Clapper box allows the tool to lift during the return stroke.
  • Gang milling uses multiple cutters on a common arbor.

Grinding and Surface Finishing

  • Grinding is an abrasive machining process.
  • Abrasive grains act as tiny cutting edges.
  • Aluminium oxide is commonly used for grinding steel.
  • Silicon carbide is commonly used for cast iron and non-ferrous materials.
  • Diamond is unsuitable for conventional high-temperature grinding of ferrous materials.
  • CBN is suitable for hardened steels.
  • Dressing restores the cutting ability of a grinding wheel.
  • Truing restores the geometrical shape of a grinding wheel.
  • Honing produces a characteristic cross-hatch pattern.
  • Lapping produces extremely fine surface finish and accuracy.
  • Centreless grinding does not require conventional chuck or centre workholding.
  • Shot peening introduces compressive residual stresses.

Cutting Parameters

  • Cutting speed is generally expressed in m/min.
  • Feed in turning is generally expressed in mm/revolution.
  • Depth of cut is generally expressed in mm.
  • Higher cutting speed generally reduces tool life.
  • Higher feed generally increases surface roughness.
  • Higher depth of cut generally increases cutting force.
  • MRR represents the volume of material removed per unit time.
  • Taylor's tool life equation is V Tⁿ = C.

Tool Wear and Cutting Fluids

  • Flank wear occurs on the flank face.
  • Crater wear occurs on the rake face.
  • Notch wear occurs near the depth-of-cut line.
  • Built-up edge is formed by adhesion of workpiece material to the cutting edge.
  • Abrasion, adhesion and diffusion are important tool-wear mechanisms.
  • HSS is tough and widely used for drills and taps.
  • Carbide tools generally allow higher cutting speeds than HSS.
  • Cutting fluids provide cooling and lubrication.
  • Soluble oils provide a balance of cooling and lubrication.
  • Straight cutting oils provide good lubrication.
  • Flood cooling supplies a large quantity of coolant.
  • MQL stands for Minimum Quantity Lubrication.
