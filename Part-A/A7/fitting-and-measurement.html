Fitting and Measurement – Mechanical Engineering Fundamentals

Subject: Workshop Technology Level: Mechanical Engineering Graduate

These notes cover the fundamental fitting operations, measuring instruments, precision measurement techniques and calibration procedures required for a Mechanical Engineering Graduate preparing for Graduate Marine Engineering (GME) Pre-Sea Training.

A1. Introduction to Fitting

Fitting is a manufacturing and workshop process in which a workpiece is manually shaped, finished, assembled or adjusted to obtain the required dimensions and fit.

It involves removing small amounts of material using hand tools and checking dimensions using measuring instruments. Fitting is generally performed after basic manufacturing operations such as casting, forging, rolling or machining.

When fitting is used

  • Components require accurate dimensions.
  • Parts need to be assembled with proper clearance or interference.
  • Small adjustments are required.
  • Machining alone cannot achieve the required final finish or fit.

Objectives of fitting

  • To achieve the required dimensions and tolerances.
  • To obtain accurate mating surfaces.
  • To remove burrs, sharp edges and excess material.
  • To improve surface finish.
  • To produce accurate holes, slots and threads.
  • To ensure proper assembly and functioning of machine components.

Common fitting operations

  1. Marking and layout.
  2. Sawing.
  3. Chipping.
  4. Filing.
  5. Drilling.
  6. Reaming.
  7. Tapping.
  8. Die threading.
  9. Scraping.
  10. Deburring and finishing.

A2. Hacksawing

Definition: Hacksawing is a metal-cutting operation performed using a hacksaw blade to remove material from a workpiece. It is commonly used for cutting metal rods, pipes, bars, plates and other workshop materials. The cutting action takes place through the teeth of the blade. A hacksaw generally performs cutting during the forward stroke.

Main parts of a hacksaw

  1. Frame – Holds and supports the hacksaw blade. May be fixed or adjustable.
  2. Handle – Provides a comfortable grip and allows the operator to apply cutting force.
  3. Blade – Thin, hardened steel strip containing cutting teeth.
  4. Blade pins – Hold the blade at both ends of the frame.
  5. Tensioning mechanism – Maintains proper tension in the blade.

Teeth per inch (TPI)

TPI represents the number of teeth present in one inch of blade length. It determines the size and spacing of the teeth.

Blade teethTypical application
14 TPIThick sections and soft materials
18 TPIMedium-thick sections
24 TPIGeneral-purpose cutting
32 TPIThin sections and small tubes
Important principle: Coarse blades have fewer teeth per inch and larger spaces between teeth. Fine blades have more teeth per inch and smaller spaces between teeth. At least 2–3 teeth should ideally remain engaged with the workpiece during cutting to avoid tooth damage.

Types of hacksaw blades (based on construction)

  • All-hard blade: Hardened throughout its entire body. Offers high cutting hardness. More brittle compared with flexible blades.
  • Flexible blade: Teeth are hardened while the remaining body is relatively flexible. Reduces the possibility of blade breakage.
  • Bi-metal blade: Made by combining different metals. Usually has high-speed steel cutting teeth and a flexible alloy-steel backing.

Hacksawing procedure

  1. Select a suitable blade according to the material and thickness.
  2. Fix the blade in the frame. Ensure that the teeth point in the forward cutting direction.
  3. Tighten the blade properly. Mark the cutting line on the workpiece.
  4. Hold the workpiece firmly in a bench vice. Position the blade at the starting point.
  5. Apply light pressure to establish a cutting groove.
  6. Continue cutting using smooth and controlled strokes.
  7. Apply pressure during the forward stroke and release pressure during the return stroke.
  8. Reduce pressure when approaching the end of the cut. Remove burrs after cutting.
DefectPossible cause
Blade breakageExcessive pressure or incorrect tension
Rough cuttingDull or unsuitable blade
Teeth strippingToo few teeth engaged
Crooked cuttingIncorrect blade alignment
Excessive vibrationImproper clamping
Slow cuttingIncorrect blade selection or dull teeth
Remember: Hacksaw cutting generally occurs on the forward stroke. Higher TPI means finer teeth. Lower TPI means coarser teeth. Blade tension is essential for accurate cutting.

A3. Filing

Definition: Filing is a metal-removal operation performed using a file. A file is a hardened steel tool containing multiple small cutting teeth. It removes small quantities of material from a workpiece through repeated strokes. Filing is mainly used for sizing, shaping, smoothing and finishing metal surfaces.

Classification of files (cross-sectional shape)

TypeApplication
Flat fileFlat surfaces and edges
Hand fileFinishing flat surfaces
Square fileSquare holes and rectangular slots
Round fileEnlarging circular holes
Half-round fileFlat and curved surfaces
Triangular fileInternal angles and V-shaped grooves
Knife-edge fileNarrow grooves and acute angles

Based on tooth arrangement

  • Single-cut file: Has one set of parallel teeth. Used for fine finishing.
  • Double-cut file: Has two sets of intersecting teeth. Removes material faster.
  • Rasp-cut file: Has individually raised teeth. Used for soft materials.
  • Curved-cut file: Has curved rows of teeth. Used for certain soft metals.

Based on grade or coarseness

GradeCharacteristics
RoughVery coarse material removal
BastardCoarse filing
Second cutMedium material removal
SmoothFine finishing
Dead smoothVery fine finishing

Filing methods

  • Cross filing: File is moved diagonally across the workpiece. Used for rapid material removal.
  • Draw filing: File is held across the workpiece and moved along its length. Used for smooth surface finish.
  • Straight filing: File is moved approximately parallel to the length of the workpiece.
  • Lathe filing: File is applied carefully to a rotating workpiece.
Remember: Single-cut = finer finishing. Double-cut = faster material removal. Draw filing = smooth finishing. Round file = circular holes. Triangular file = internal corners and V-grooves.

A4. Tapping

Definition: Tapping is a machining operation used to produce internal screw threads inside a drilled hole. The tool used is called a tap. It is commonly performed manually using a tap wrench or mechanically using a tapping machine.

Types of hand taps

  • Taper tap: Has a long chamfer at its cutting end. Gradually starts the internal thread.
  • Second tap / Intermediate tap: Has a shorter chamfer than the taper tap. Deepens the partially formed threads.
  • Plug / Bottoming tap: Has a very short chamfer. Produces threads close to the bottom of a blind hole.

Tapping drill size

For a standard metric ISO coarse thread, a commonly used approximate formula is:

Tap drill diameter ≈ D − P

Where D = Nominal thread diameter in mm, P = Thread pitch in mm.

Example: M10 × 1.5

10 − 1.5 = 8.5 mm

Therefore, the approximate tapping drill diameter is 8.5 mm.

ProblemCause
Tap breakageExcessive force or misalignment
Damaged threadsIncorrect drill size
Rough threadsDull tap or poor lubrication
Cross-threadingTap not aligned properly
Difficult rotationInsufficient lubrication or chip accumulation
Incomplete bottom threadsIncorrect tap selection
Remember: Tapping produces internal threads, while die threading produces external threads.

B1. Fundamentals of Measurement

Definition: Measurement is the process of determining the magnitude of a physical quantity by comparing it with an accepted standard.

Important terms

  • Accuracy – Closeness of a measured value to the true or accepted reference value.
  • Precision – Closeness of repeated measurements to one another.
  • Resolution – Smallest change an instrument can display.
  • Least count – Smallest measurement increment an instrument can directly indicate.
  • Repeatability – Ability to produce similar results under the same conditions.
  • Sensitivity – Ability to respond to small changes in the measured quantity.
  • Error – Difference between measured value and true/reference value.
  • Tolerance – Permissible variation in a component's dimension.
Error = Measured value − Reference value
Tolerance = Upper limit − Lower limit

Accuracy vs Precision

AccuracyPrecision
Closeness to true valueCloseness among repeated readings
Indicates correctnessIndicates consistency
Affected by systematic errorsAffected by random variation
Important for dimensional correctnessImportant for repeatability

B2. Vernier Calliper

Definition: A vernier calliper is a precision measuring instrument used to measure internal dimensions, external dimensions, depths and step dimensions. It consists of a main scale and a sliding vernier scale.

Least count of vernier calliper

LC = 1 MSD − 1 VSD
LC = (Value of 1 MSD) / (Number of vernier divisions)

Example

If 1 MSD = 1 mm and 10 VSD = 9 mm, then:

1 VSD = 9/10 = 0.9 mm
LC = 1 − 0.9 = 0.1 mm

Reading a vernier calliper

Final Reading = Main Scale Reading + Vernier Reading
Vernier Reading = n × LC

Example

Main scale reading = 24 mm, coinciding vernier division = 6, LC = 0.1 mm.

Vernier Reading = 6 × 0.1 = 0.6 mm
Final reading = 24 + 0.6 = 24.6 mm
Zero error: Occurs when the instrument displays a non-zero reading when jaws are fully closed. Positive zero error → subtract correction. Negative zero error → add correction.
Correct Reading = Observed Reading − Zero Error

Advantages & Limitations

  • Versatile – measures internal, external, depth and step dimensions.
  • Portable and easy to use; more accurate than a steel rule.
  • Generally less precise than a micrometer.
  • Reading errors may occur due to parallax or poor alignment.

B3. Micrometer

Definition: A micrometer is a precision measuring instrument used to measure small dimensions accurately. It works on the principle of a precision screw and nut mechanism.

Least count of a micrometer

LC = Pitch of screw / Number of divisions on thimble

Example

Screw pitch = 0.5 mm, thimble divisions = 50.

LC = 0.5 / 50 = 0.01 mm

Reading of an outside micrometer

Final Reading = Sleeve Reading + Thimble Reading
Thimble Reading = n × LC

Example

Sleeve reading = 12.5 mm, thimble reading = 28 divisions, LC = 0.01 mm.

Thimble Reading = 28 × 0.01 = 0.28 mm
Final reading = 12.5 + 0.28 = 12.78 mm

Vernier calliper vs Micrometer

ParameterVernier calliperMicrometer
PrincipleVernier scaleScrew and nut
Typical least count0.02–0.1 mm0.01 mm
Measuring rangeRelatively largeRelatively small
PrecisionGenerally lowerGenerally higher
Remember: A micrometer generally offers finer resolution than a standard vernier calliper.

B4. Dial Gauge / Dial Indicator

Definition: A dial gauge is a precision comparative measuring instrument used to detect small linear movements and dimensional variations. It consists of a graduated circular dial and a movable contact plunger or lever.

Applications

  • Checking shaft runout.
  • Checking surface flatness and parallelism.
  • Checking alignment of machine components.
  • Checking concentricity and eccentricity.
  • Checking bearing movement and clearances.

Total Indicator Reading (TIR)

TIR = Rmax − Rmin

Example

Maximum reading = +0.08 mm, minimum reading = −0.04 mm.

TIR = 0.08 − (−0.04) = 0.12 mm
Remember: Dial gauge = comparative measurement and deviation checking.

B5. Feeler Gauge

Definition: A feeler gauge is a precision measuring tool consisting of thin metal blades of known thickness. It is used to measure small gaps and clearances between two surfaces.

Applications in marine engineering

  • Checking valve clearance in diesel engines.
  • Checking piston ring end gaps.
  • Measuring spark plug gaps.
  • Checking bearing and machine clearances.
  • Checking gaps between mating surfaces.
Remember: Feeler gauge measures small gaps and clearances using blades of known thickness.

C1. Bore Gauge

Definition: A bore gauge is a precision measuring instrument used to measure the internal diameter of holes, cylinders and bores. It is particularly useful for detecting variations in internal diameter at different positions.

Applications

  • Measuring engine cylinder bore diameter.
  • Measuring bearing housing dimensions.
  • Checking pump casing bores.
  • Checking cylinder wear.
  • Measuring taper and ovality in cylindrical bores.

Working principle

A bore gauge generally works as a comparative measuring instrument. It is first set against a known reference dimension using a micrometer or setting ring. Changes in the internal diameter cause movement of the measuring contacts, which is indicated on the dial.

Important bore measurement terms

Taper = (D₁ − D₂) / L
Ovality = Dmax − Dmin
Marine Engineering Application: Bore gauges are particularly important when inspecting marine diesel engine cylinder liners for wear, taper and ovality.

C2. Depth Gauge

Definition: A depth gauge is a measuring instrument used to determine the depth of holes, slots, recesses, grooves and steps. It generally consists of a base that rests on the reference surface and a measuring rod or blade that moves into the feature.

Types of depth gauges

  • Vernier depth gauge: Uses a main scale and vernier scale.
  • Micrometer depth gauge: Uses a precision screw mechanism.
  • Digital depth gauge: Displays depth electronically.

Applications

  • Measuring the depth of drilled holes.
  • Measuring recesses in machine components.
  • Measuring groove depth and counterbore depth.
  • Checking machined steps.
Remember: A depth gauge measures the vertical distance from a reference surface to the bottom of a feature.

D1. Surface Plate

Definition: A surface plate is a precision-engineered flat reference surface used for inspection, marking and measurement. It provides a stable reference plane for checking the accuracy of mechanical components.

Types

  • Granite surface plate: Good dimensional stability, corrosion-resistant, non-magnetic, good wear characteristics.
  • Cast-iron surface plate: Can be resurfaced and reconditioned. Requires protection against rust.

Grades (common metrology classifications)

GradeApplication
Grade 0Precision inspection and laboratory applications
Grade 1General precision inspection
Grade 2Workshop inspection
Remember: A surface plate provides a reference plane for precision measurement and inspection.

D2. Straightedge

Definition: A straightedge is a precision tool used to check the straightness of surfaces and edges. It is generally a long, straight, rigid bar with a precisely finished working edge.

Types

  • Steel straightedge: Made from hardened or precision-finished steel.
  • Knife-edge straightedge: Has a narrow working edge for checking small deviations.
  • Precision straightedge: Manufactured to high dimensional accuracy.
  • Granite straightedge: Provides a stable reference edge.

Straightedge vs Surface plate

Surface plateStraightedge
Provides a reference planeProvides a reference straight line or edge
Used for flatness inspectionUsed for straightness inspection
Generally a large flat surfaceGenerally a long narrow tool
Supports components and instrumentsPlaced against or over a component
Remember: Surface plate = reference plane; straightedge = reference straight line.

E1. Precision Measurement

Definition: Precision measurement is the process of determining dimensions with a high degree of accuracy, repeatability and resolution.

Important concepts

  • Absolute measurement: Obtained directly from the instrument scale.
  • Comparative measurement: Dimension compared against a known reference.
  • Direct measurement: Measured quantity obtained directly from the instrument.
  • Indirect measurement: Measured quantity calculated from other measured quantities.

Sources of measurement errors

Error types

  • Instrumental errors: Zero error, wear of measuring surfaces, backlash, calibration error.
  • Environmental errors: Temperature changes, humidity, vibration, dust.
  • Personal errors: Incorrect positioning, parallax error, excessive measuring force, incorrect scale reading.
  • Method errors: Incorrect alignment, improper clamping, incorrect reference selection.

Temperature effects

Precision dimensional measurements are commonly referenced to a standard temperature of 20°C.

ΔL = α L₀ ΔT

Example

Steel component: L₀ = 100 mm, α = 12×10⁻⁶/°C, ΔT = 20°C.

ΔL = 12×10⁻⁶ × 100 × 20 = 0.024 mm

The component expands by 0.024 mm.

E2. Calibration

Definition: Calibration is the process of establishing the relationship between the readings of a measuring instrument and the corresponding values provided by measurement standards.

Calibration vs Adjustment vs Verification

TermMeaning
CalibrationDetermines the relationship between instrument reading and a reference standard. Identifies errors.
AdjustmentChanges the instrument so its readings correspond more closely to required values.
VerificationProvides objective evidence that specified requirements are fulfilled.

Calibration standards

  • Gauge blocks / slip gauges: Precision blocks used for calibrating micrometers and callipers.
  • Setting rings: Precision rings used for setting and checking bore gauges.
  • Master standards: High-accuracy reference instruments.
  • Reference weights: Used for calibrating weighing instruments.

Traceability

Metrological traceability is the property of a measurement result whereby it can be related to a reference through a documented, unbroken chain of calibrations.

International standards → National standards → Reference standards / Calibration labs → Working standards → Workshop instruments

Measurement uncertainty

Measurement uncertainty is a parameter that characterizes the dispersion of values that could reasonably be attributed to a measured quantity. It is not the same as measurement error.

Importance in marine engineering: Incorrect measurements can lead to incorrect bearing clearances, improper component assembly, excessive vibration, increased friction and wear, lubrication problems, reduced machinery efficiency and premature component failure.
Remember: Calibration establishes measurement relationships. Adjustment changes instrument characteristics. Verification checks conformity. Traceability connects measurement results to recognized standards.

G3. Important Facts to Memorize

  1. Hacksawing generally cuts on the forward stroke.
  2. Higher TPI means finer hacksaw teeth.
  3. Double-cut files generally remove material faster than single-cut files.
  4. Draw filing is used for smooth finishing.
  5. Tapping produces internal threads.
  6. Taper taps have a longer chamfer than bottoming taps.
  7. Tap drill diameter is smaller than nominal thread diameter.
  8. Vernier callipers work using the difference between two scale divisions.
  9. Micrometers work on the screw-and-nut principle.
  10. A ratchet stop helps maintain consistent measuring force.
  11. A dial gauge is commonly used for runout and alignment checks.
  12. TIR is the difference between maximum and minimum indicator readings.
  13. A feeler gauge measures small clearances.
  14. A bore gauge measures internal diameters.
  15. A depth gauge measures the depth of holes and recesses.
  16. A granite surface plate is corrosion-resistant and non-magnetic.
  17. Standard reference temperature for dimensional metrology is commonly 20°C.
  18. Calibration does not necessarily mean adjustment.
  19. Measurement uncertainty is not the same as measurement error.
  20. Cylinder liner wear can be evaluated through bore diameter, taper and ovality measurements.
