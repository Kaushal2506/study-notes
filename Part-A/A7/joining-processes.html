Arc welding is a fusion welding process in which heat is generated by an electric arc between an electrode and the workpiece or between two electrodes. The heat melts the edges of the base metal and, when required, the filler metal. The molten metal cools and solidifies to form a permanent joint.

1.1 Principle of Arc Welding

  • Electrical energy is converted into thermal energy.
  • An electric arc is established between the electrode and the workpiece.
  • The arc produces extremely high temperatures — approximately 5,000–6,000°C or higher.
  • The molten metal forms a weld pool, which cools and solidifies into a welded joint.

1.2 Main Components of Arc Welding Equipment

  1. Power Source — supplies electrical energy; can be AC or DC; controls current and voltage.
  2. Electrode — conducts current to establish the arc; in consumable-electrode welding it also supplies filler metal.
  3. Electrode Holder — holds the electrode and provides electrical connection.
  4. Welding Cables — carry current between power source, holder and workpiece.
  5. Earth Clamp / Work Clamp — connects the workpiece to the welding circuit.
  6. Workpiece — the metal components being joined.
  7. Protective Equipment — helmet, gloves, clothing, safety shoes, respiratory protection.

1.3 Shielded Metal Arc Welding (SMAW)

SMAW is also known as Manual Metal Arc Welding (MMA), stick welding, or shielded arc welding. It is one of the most common arc welding processes used in fabrication and maintenance.

Working principle

  1. A flux-coated consumable electrode is connected to the welding power source.
  2. The workpiece is connected to the other terminal.
  3. The electrode is brought into contact and slightly withdrawn to establish an arc.
  4. The arc generates heat to melt the electrode tip and the base metal.
  5. The electrode coating decomposes and produces shielding gases.
  6. The coating also forms slag over the molten weld pool.
  7. The slag protects the weld metal during solidification.
  8. After cooling, the slag is removed using a chipping hammer and wire brush.

Functions of Electrode Coating

  • Shielding — produces gases that protect molten metal from oxygen and nitrogen.
  • Slag Formation — forms a protective slag layer; prevents rapid cooling and contamination.
  • Arc Stabilization — helps maintain a stable and consistent arc.
  • Deoxidation — deoxidizing agents react with oxygen; reduce porosity and oxidation.
  • Alloying — may introduce alloying elements to improve mechanical properties.
  • Metal Deposition — some coatings contain iron powder to increase deposition rate.

1.4 Types of Welding Electrodes

A. Consumable Electrodes

  • Melt during welding; act as both current-carrying electrode and filler metal.
  • Examples: SMAW coated electrodes, MIG welding wire, flux-cored wire.

B. Non-Consumable Electrodes

  • Do not normally melt or supply filler metal.
  • Example: tungsten electrode used in TIG welding.

Common Electrode Coating Types

  • Cellulosic — strong gas shield, deep penetration, often used for pipeline welding.
  • Rutile — high titanium dioxide, stable arc, smooth weld appearance, easy to operate.
  • Basic / Low-Hydrogen — calcium carbonate and calcium fluoride; low-hydrogen deposits; good toughness; for structural and critical fabrication.
  • Iron Powder — increases deposition rate and productivity.

DCEN – Direct Current Electrode Negative (Straight Polarity)

  • Electrode connected to negative terminal; workpiece to positive.
  • More heat generated at the positive workpiece in conventional theory.
  • Often produces deeper penetration in conventional applications.

DCEP – Direct Current Electrode Positive (Reverse Polarity)

  • Electrode connected to positive terminal; workpiece to negative.
  • More heat generated at the positive electrode in conventional theory.
  • Different penetration and melting characteristics compared with DCEN.
Important: Heat distribution and penetration depend on the welding process, electrode type, shielding gas and operating conditions. Always follow the electrode manufacturer's recommended polarity.

1.6 Welding Positions

  1. Flat Position (1G / 1F) — welding from the upper side; gravity helps; generally the easiest.
  2. Horizontal Position (2G / 2F) — horizontal joint or fillet; gravity pulls metal downward; requires pool control.
  3. Vertical Position (3G / 3F) — vertical direction (up or down); requires good control of heat input and molten metal.
  4. Overhead Position (4G / 4F) — from underneath the joint; gravity pulls metal away; requires considerable skill.

Note: G denotes groove weld and F denotes fillet weld.

1.7 Advantages of Arc Welding

  • Produces strong and permanent joints.
  • Suitable for a wide range of metals.
  • Equipment is relatively economical, especially SMAW.
  • Can be used in workshops and field locations.
  • Suitable for thick and thin metal sections, depending on the process.
  • Can be used in different welding positions.
  • Does not necessarily require external gas cylinders.
  • Useful for shipbuilding and repair work.

1.8 Disadvantages of Arc Welding

  • Requires trained and skilled operators.
  • Produces heat-affected zones.
  • Can cause distortion and residual stresses.
  • Produces welding fumes and radiation.
  • Slag removal is required in SMAW.
  • Welding defects may develop due to incorrect technique.
  • Some processes require careful electrode storage.
  • Requires proper electrical safety precautions.

1.9 Applications in Marine Engineering

Marine Applications

  • Joining ship hull plates.
  • Fabrication of bulkheads and stiffeners.
  • Welding structural supports.
  • Repairing damaged steel structures.
  • Fabrication of brackets and foundations.
  • Repairing machinery components.
  • Joining pipelines and support structures, subject to applicable procedures and approvals.

Key Facts – Arc Welding

  • Arc temperature: approximately 5,000–6,000°C or higher.
  • SMAW uses a flux-coated consumable electrode.
  • DCEN = straight polarity, DCEP = reverse polarity.
  • 1G = flat, 2G = horizontal, 3G = vertical, 4G = overhead.

MIG/MAG welding is an arc welding process in which a continuously fed consumable wire electrode produces an electric arc with the workpiece. The welding zone is protected by externally supplied shielding gas. The process is also known as Gas Metal Arc Welding (GMAW).

Common applicationAluminium, copper alloys and other non-ferrous metalsCarbon steel and many low-alloy steels

Inert Gases

  • Argon (Ar) and Helium (He).
  • Do not normally react chemically with molten weld metal under ordinary conditions.

Active Gases

  • Carbon dioxide (CO₂); oxygen (O₂) in controlled mixtures with argon.
  • Can influence arc characteristics, penetration, metal transfer and weld chemistry.

2.2 Working Principle of MIG/MAG Welding

  1. Welding wire is supplied continuously from a spool.
  2. A wire-feeding mechanism pushes the wire through the torch.
  3. The wire passes through a contact tip, which supplies welding current.
  4. An electric arc is established between the wire and the workpiece.
  5. The arc melts the wire electrode and the base metal.
  6. Shielding gas flows through the nozzle and protects the molten weld pool.
  7. Molten filler metal transfers from the wire to the weld pool.
  8. The molten metal solidifies and forms a permanent joint.

2.3 Main Components of MIG/MAG Equipment

  1. Power Source — usually DC output, constant-voltage characteristics.
  2. Wire Spool — holds continuous electrode wire.
  3. Wire Feeder — controls wire feed speed.
  4. Welding Torch — directs wire and shielding gas.
  5. Contact Tip — transfers current to the wire.
  6. Gas Cylinder — stores shielding gas.
  7. Gas Regulator and Flowmeter — controls and indicates gas flow.
  8. Gas Nozzle — directs shielding gas toward the welding zone.
  9. Work Clamp — completes the electrical circuit.
  10. Cooling System — air or water cooling depending on current.

2.4 Metal Transfer Modes in MIG/MAG Welding

Metal transfer refers to how molten electrode metal moves from the wire tip into the weld pool.

A. Short-Circuit Transfer

  • Wire electrode repeatedly touches the weld pool; short circuit occurs.
  • Current increases and the wire tip melts or detaches; arc re-establishes.
  • Characteristics: low heat input, suitable for thin materials, useful for root passes and out-of-position welding, can produce spatter if parameters are incorrect.

B. Globular Transfer

  • Molten metal transfers in relatively large droplets, usually larger than the wire diameter.
  • Gravity and electromagnetic forces influence transfer; often produces more spatter.
  • Commonly associated with CO₂-rich shielding gas conditions.

C. Spray Transfer

  • Molten metal transfers as very fine droplets, smaller than the wire diameter.
  • Requires relatively high current and suitable shielding gas conditions.
  • Produces a stable arc and high deposition rate; suitable for thicker materials and flat or horizontal welding.

D. Pulsed Spray Transfer

  • Current alternates between high peak current and lower background current.
  • Peak current helps detach droplets; background current maintains the arc.
  • Allows spray-like transfer at lower average heat input than conventional spray transfer.
  • Useful for thinner materials and positional welding.

2.5 Advantages of MIG/MAG Welding

  • High welding speed and high deposition rate.
  • Continuous wire feeding improves productivity.
  • No slag formation with conventional solid-wire GMAW.
  • Suitable for automated and robotic welding.
  • Produces clean welds with less post-weld cleaning.
  • Suitable for different material thicknesses.
  • Can be used for a wide range of ferrous and non-ferrous metals.

2.6 Disadvantages of MIG/MAG Welding

  • Equipment is more complex than basic SMAW equipment.
  • Shielding gas cylinders increase equipment requirements.
  • Wind can disturb shielding gas and cause porosity.
  • Requires clean surfaces for good welding quality.
  • Wire feeding problems can interrupt welding.
  • Equipment is less convenient for some remote field repairs.
  • Gas flow and torch distance must be controlled.
  • Incorrect settings can cause spatter, lack of fusion and burn-through.

2.7 Applications in Marine Engineering

Marine Applications

  • Ship structural fabrication.
  • Welding steel plates and stiffeners.
  • Fabrication of machinery foundations.
  • Joining stainless-steel components using suitable wire and shielding gas.
  • Production of structural assemblies.
  • Workshop fabrication of metal components.
Important marine point: The welding procedure, filler material, gas selection and operator qualifications must meet the applicable classification society and approved welding requirements.

Key Facts – MIG/MAG

  • MIG = Metal Inert Gas (Argon, Helium).
  • MAG = Metal Active Gas (CO₂, Argon-CO₂ mixtures).
  • Uses a continuously fed consumable wire electrode.
  • Transfer modes: short-circuit, globular, spray, pulsed spray.
  • No slag formation with conventional solid-wire GMAW.

TIG welding stands for Tungsten Inert Gas welding. It is also known as Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW). It is a fusion welding process in which a non-consumable tungsten electrode is used to establish the electric arc, and an inert shielding gas protects the weld pool from atmospheric contamination.

3.1 Principle of TIG Welding

  • An electric arc is generated between a non-consumable tungsten electrode and the workpiece.
  • The heat produced by the arc melts the base metal.
  • The tungsten electrode does not normally melt during the welding operation.
  • An external filler rod may be manually added to the molten weld pool.
  • Argon or helium is used to shield the weld area.
  • The molten metal solidifies to form a high-quality weld.

3.2 Main Components of TIG Welding Equipment

  1. Power Source — supplies AC or DC; DC commonly used for steels, AC for aluminium and magnesium.
  2. Tungsten Electrode — non-consumable; produces and maintains the arc.
  3. Welding Torch — holds the tungsten electrode; directs shielding gas.
  4. Ceramic Nozzle — directs and distributes shielding gas.
  5. Shielding Gas Cylinder — usually contains argon or helium.
  6. Filler Rod — supplies additional metal when required.
  7. Foot Pedal / Current Control — allows operator to control current.
  8. Cooling System — high-current torches may use water cooling.

3.3 Tungsten Electrode

  • Tungsten is used because of its extremely high melting point — approximately 3,422°C.
  • It can withstand high arc temperatures without melting under normal TIG welding conditions.
  • Electrode types: pure tungsten, thoriated tungsten, ceriated tungsten, lanthanated tungsten, zirconiated tungsten.
  • Electrode selection depends on material, current type, welding current and applicable safety requirements.

3.4 Shielding Gases Used in TIG Welding

A. Argon

  • Most commonly used TIG shielding gas.
  • Provides good arc stability; allows easy arc starting; suitable for many metals; good shielding at relatively low flow rates.

B. Helium

  • Produces a hotter arc under suitable conditions.
  • Can increase penetration and welding speed; useful for thicker materials and highly conductive metals.
  • More expensive than argon.

C. Argon-Helium Mixture

  • Combines characteristics of both gases.
  • Can provide higher heat input than pure argon under suitable conditions.

Why is AC used for Aluminium TIG Welding?

  • Aluminium develops a tough aluminium oxide layer.
  • Aluminium oxide has a much higher melting point than aluminium itself.
  • Aluminium melts at approximately 660°C; aluminium oxide melts at approximately 2,050°C.
  • During AC TIG welding, the electrode-positive portion provides cathodic cleaning action that helps break up and remove the oxide layer.
  • The electrode-negative portion provides effective heating of the workpiece.

3.6 Working Procedure of TIG Welding

  1. Clean the workpiece to remove oil, grease, rust and dirt.
  2. Select the appropriate tungsten electrode.
  3. Install the electrode in the TIG torch.
  4. Connect the shielding gas supply.
  5. Select the correct current type, polarity and welding parameters.
  6. Start the arc using the torch's arc-starting system.
  7. Maintain the correct electrode-to-work distance.
  8. Move the torch steadily along the joint.
  9. Add filler rod separately when required.
  10. Allow the weld pool to solidify under suitable shielding conditions.

3.7 Advantages of TIG Welding

  • Produces high-quality and clean welds.
  • Provides excellent control over heat input.
  • Produces very little spatter.
  • Does not require flux; no slag formation.
  • Suitable for thin materials.
  • Can weld many ferrous and non-ferrous metals.
  • Produces attractive weld appearance.
  • Suitable for critical and precision applications.

3.8 Disadvantages of TIG Welding

  • Welding speed is relatively low.
  • Requires a highly skilled operator.
  • Equipment is more expensive than basic SMAW equipment.
  • Requires clean base metal.
  • Shielding gas can be disturbed by wind.
  • Filler metal must often be supplied separately.
  • Requires careful torch and filler rod coordination.
  • Productivity is generally lower than MIG/MAG welding for many production applications.

3.9 Applications in Marine Engineering

Marine Applications

  • Stainless-steel pipe fabrication.
  • Thin-wall pipe welding.
  • Precision welding of machinery components.
  • Aluminium fabrication.
  • Welding of corrosion-resistant components.
  • Root-pass welding in certain pipe welding procedures.
  • Repair of selected precision components.

Key Facts – TIG Welding

  • TIG = Tungsten Inert Gas; also GTAW.
  • Non-consumable tungsten electrode; melting point ≈ 3,422°C.
  • AC TIG for aluminium and magnesium; DC TIG for steel and stainless steel.
  • Argon most common; helium for hotter arc and higher penetration.
  • Aluminium melts at ≈ 660°C; aluminium oxide at ≈ 2,050°C.

Gas welding is a fusion welding process in which heat is generated by burning a fuel gas with oxygen. The most common gas welding process is oxy-acetylene welding.

4.1 Oxy-Acetylene Welding

  • Uses acetylene as the fuel gas and oxygen as the supporting gas.
  • The gases mix in the welding torch and burn to produce a high-temperature flame.
  • Flame temperature: approximately 3,100–3,200°C under suitable conditions.

Chemical reaction

2C₂H₂ + 5O₂ → 4CO₂ + 2H₂O

This represents complete combustion of acetylene.

4.2 Main Equipment Used in Gas Welding

  1. Oxygen Cylinder — stores oxygen under high pressure.
  2. Acetylene Cylinder — stores acetylene dissolved in a suitable solvent within a porous mass.
  3. Pressure Regulator — reduces cylinder pressure to working pressure.
  4. Pressure Gauges — indicate cylinder and delivery pressure.
  5. Gas Hoses — carry oxygen and acetylene to the torch.
  6. Welding Torch — mixes oxygen and acetylene.
  7. Nozzle / Tip — directs the flame toward the workpiece.
  8. Filler Rod — supplies additional metal.
  9. Flashback Arrestor — prevents a flame from travelling backwards into the hose or gas supply.

4.3 Types of Oxy-Acetylene Flames

A. Neutral Flame

  • Produced when oxygen and acetylene are supplied in approximately equal chemical proportions.
  • Has a clearly defined inner cone and outer envelope; no acetylene feather.
  • Does not significantly oxidize or carburize the weld metal under normal conditions.
  • Commonly used for welding mild steel and many other metals with suitable procedures.

B. Carburizing / Reducing Flame

  • Produced when excess acetylene is supplied.
  • Contains three visible zones: inner cone, acetylene feather, outer envelope.
  • Provides a reducing atmosphere; can introduce carbon into some molten metals.
  • Used for certain high-carbon steels, hard-facing operations and selected non-ferrous applications.

C. Oxidizing Flame

  • Produced when excess oxygen is supplied.
  • Has a shorter and sharper inner cone.
  • Produces an oxidizing atmosphere; can oxidize the molten metal.
  • Commonly used for oxy-fuel welding of certain copper alloys, such as brass.
Remember:
Neutral flame = Balanced gases.
Carburizing flame = Excess acetylene.
Oxidizing flame = Excess oxygen.

4.4 Working Procedure of Gas Welding

  1. Clean the surfaces to be joined.
  2. Prepare the edges according to the required joint design.
  3. Connect oxygen and acetylene cylinders to their respective regulators and hoses.
  4. Check the equipment for leakage.
  5. Set the required working pressures.
  6. Ignite the torch using an approved spark lighter.
  7. Adjust the flame to the required type.
  8. Heat the joint edges until a molten pool forms.
  9. Add filler metal if required.
  10. Move the torch along the joint at a controlled speed.
  11. Allow the weld to cool naturally under suitable conditions.

4.5 Advantages of Gas Welding

  • Equipment is portable.
  • Does not require an electrical power supply.
  • Suitable for thin metal sheets.
  • Can be used for welding, heating and brazing.
  • Provides good control of heat input.
  • Useful for maintenance and repair work.
  • Can be used for cutting operations with suitable equipment.

4.6 Disadvantages of Gas Welding

  • Lower productivity than many arc welding processes.
  • Heat-affected zone is generally wider.
  • Greater possibility of distortion.
  • Not ideal for thick structural steel sections.
  • Requires careful handling of compressed gas cylinders.
  • Flame adjustment requires operator skill.
  • Acetylene and oxygen create significant fire and explosion hazards if mishandled.

4.7 Gas Cutting (Oxy-Fuel Cutting)

Gas cutting is a metal-cutting process that uses a fuel-gas flame to heat steel to its ignition temperature. A high-pressure stream of pure oxygen is then directed onto the heated metal. The oxygen reacts chemically with the hot iron to form iron oxides, and the oxygen jet blows away the molten and oxidized material, producing a cut.

Important distinction:
Gas welding melts the metal using a flame to join components.
Oxy-fuel cutting uses oxidation of heated metal and an oxygen jet to separate material.

Principle of Oxy-Fuel Cutting

  1. The metal is heated using an oxy-fuel flame.
  2. The steel reaches its ignition temperature in oxygen.
  3. A concentrated jet of pure oxygen is released through the cutting nozzle.
  4. Iron reacts with oxygen to form iron oxides.
  5. The oxidation reaction generates additional heat.
  6. The oxygen jet removes the oxides and molten material.
  7. A continuous cut is produced as the torch moves forward.

Chemical Reaction

3Fe + 2O₂ → Fe₃O₄

Iron oxide formation is an exothermic reaction, meaning it releases heat.

Important Requirements for Oxy-Fuel Cutting

  • The metal should be capable of oxidizing readily.
  • Its oxide should have a melting point low enough, relative to the base metal, to be removed by the oxygen jet.
  • The oxidation reaction should produce sufficient heat.
  • The oxide and molten products must be removable by the oxygen stream.

Why is oxy-fuel cutting mainly used for carbon steel?

  • Iron reacts readily with oxygen at high temperatures.
  • The iron oxides produced can be removed by the oxygen jet.
  • Aluminium and stainless steel form oxide layers with high melting temperatures, making ordinary oxy-fuel cutting unsuitable or inefficient.

Applications of Gas Cutting

  • Cutting thick steel plates.
  • Ship hull plate preparation.
  • Cutting structural steel sections.
  • Removing damaged steel components.
  • Scrap metal cutting.
  • Workshop fabrication and maintenance.

Advantages of Gas Cutting

  • Portable equipment.
  • Can cut thick steel sections.
  • Relatively economical.
  • Useful for field operations.
  • Does not require an electric arc.

Disadvantages of Gas Cutting

  • Produces a heat-affected zone.
  • Can cause thermal distortion.
  • Cut quality depends on operator skill.
  • Not suitable for all metals.
  • Requires careful control of oxygen and fuel gas.

Key Facts – Gas Welding & Cutting

  • Oxy-acetylene flame temperature ≈ 3,100–3,200°C.
  • Neutral flame = balanced gases; carburizing = excess acetylene; oxidizing = excess oxygen.
  • Gas cutting uses oxidation of heated steel with a pure oxygen jet.
  • Oxy-fuel cutting is especially suitable for carbon steel.
  • 3Fe + 2O₂ → Fe₃O₄ (exothermic).

Both brazing and soldering are metal-joining processes in which a filler metal is melted to join components. In both processes, the base metals remain below their melting temperatures.

Most important difference:
In brazing, filler metal melts above 450°C.
In soldering, filler metal melts below 450°C.
In both processes, the base metals remain below their melting temperatures.

5.1 Brazing

Brazing is a metal-joining process in which a filler metal having a liquidus temperature above 450°C is melted and distributed between closely fitted components. The base metals do not melt. The molten filler metal flows into the joint by capillary action, and after cooling, a strong joint is formed.

Principle of Brazing

Brazing mainly works on the principle of capillary action — the ability of a liquid to flow through a narrow gap due to surface tension and adhesive forces. Molten filler metal enters the small gap between the two surfaces, spreads through the joint, and solidifies, creating a bonded joint.

Brazing Materials

  • Silver-based alloys.
  • Copper-based alloys.
  • Brass-based alloys.
  • Aluminium-silicon alloys.
  • Nickel-based alloys.

Selection depends on base metal, operating temperature, service environment and joint strength requirements.

Flux Used in Brazing

  • Removes surface oxides.
  • Prevents further oxidation during heating.
  • Improves wetting of the base metal.
  • Allows molten filler metal to flow into the joint.

Common flux materials include borax-based and fluoride-based compositions.

Brazing Procedure

  1. Clean the surfaces to remove oil, grease and oxide layers.
  2. Prepare the joint with a suitable clearance.
  3. Apply appropriate flux if required.
  4. Assemble the components.
  5. Heat the joint uniformly.
  6. Introduce the filler metal.
  7. Allow capillary action to distribute the molten filler metal.
  8. Allow the joint to cool.
  9. Remove residual flux and clean the assembly.

Advantages of Brazing

  • Base metals do not melt.
  • Can join dissimilar metals.
  • Produces relatively low distortion compared with fusion welding.
  • Suitable for thin sections.
  • Can produce leak-tight joints.
  • Suitable for complex assemblies.
  • Can be used for batch and mass production.
  • Can join components that are difficult to weld.

Disadvantages of Brazing

  • Joint strength is generally lower than a properly designed fusion weld in comparable materials.
  • Requires proper joint clearance.
  • Filler metal and flux selection are important.
  • Residual flux may cause corrosion if not removed.
  • Service temperature is limited by the filler metal.
  • Requires good surface preparation.

Applications of Brazing

  • Heat exchangers.
  • Refrigeration systems.
  • Air-conditioning components.
  • Copper tubing.
  • Carbide tool tips.
  • Radiators.
  • Electrical and electronic assemblies.
  • Certain marine piping and equipment components.

5.2 Soldering

Soldering is a metal-joining process in which a filler metal called solder melts below 450°C. The base metals remain solid. Molten solder flows between the surfaces and forms a joint after solidification. It is commonly used for electrical and electronic connections.

Principle of Soldering

  • The components are heated to a temperature sufficient to melt the solder.
  • The molten solder wets the surfaces.
  • It spreads over the joint area.
  • After cooling, the solder solidifies and creates a mechanical and electrical connection.

Types of Solder

  • Tin-Lead Solder — traditionally used in electronics; common historical composition 60% tin and 40% lead; lead-containing solder is restricted or prohibited in many applications.
  • Lead-Free Solder — commonly contains tin with silver, copper or other alloying elements; used in modern electronics manufacturing.
  • Tin-Silver Solder — used where improved mechanical properties are required.

Tools Used in Soldering

  • Soldering iron.
  • Solder wire.
  • Flux.
  • Soldering stand.
  • Cleaning sponge or tip cleaner.
  • Desoldering pump.
  • Protective equipment.

Types of Soldering

A. Soft Soldering — uses solder with a melting temperature below 450°C; commonly used in electronics; produces joints with relatively low mechanical strength.

B. Hard Soldering — sometimes used informally to describe higher-temperature soldering operations; terminology varies by industry. In standard manufacturing classification, brazing is the process using filler metal above 450°C.

Advantages of Soldering

  • Low operating temperature.
  • Suitable for delicate components.
  • Easy to perform.
  • Low equipment cost.
  • Suitable for electrical connections.
  • Can join thin metal sheets.
  • Produces low thermal distortion.

Disadvantages of Soldering

  • Low joint strength compared with welding and many brazed joints.
  • Not suitable for high-temperature applications.
  • Not suitable for heavy structural loads.
  • Joint quality depends on surface cleanliness.
  • Some solders contain hazardous materials.
  • Excessive heating can damage sensitive components.

Applications of Soldering

  • Printed circuit boards (PCB).
  • Electrical wiring.
  • Electronic components.
  • Instrumentation circuits.
  • Small copper connections.
  • Low-temperature electrical joints.

Welding defects are imperfections or discontinuities in a welded joint that reduce its quality, reliability or performance. They may occur due to incorrect welding parameters, poor preparation, unsuitable filler materials, contamination or improper techniques. In marine engineering, welding defects can be particularly serious because ship structures and machinery may experience vibration, cyclic loading, pressure and corrosive environments.

6.1 Classification of Welding Defects

A. External Defects — visible on the surface: undercut, overlap, excessive reinforcement, surface cracks, spatter, crater defects.

B. Internal Defects — present inside the weld or below its surface: porosity, slag inclusion, lack of fusion, incomplete penetration, internal cracks, internal cavities.

6.2 Common Welding Defects in Detail

1. Porosity

  • Definition: Small cavities or gas pockets inside or on the surface of the weld metal, occurring when gases become trapped during solidification.
  • Causes: Moisture, oil/grease/dirt contamination, incorrect shielding gas flow, gas hose leakage, excessive arc length, damp electrodes, improper shielding.
  • Prevention: Clean base metal, dry electrodes, correct gas flow, check connections, avoid excessive arc length, protect from wind.

2. Slag Inclusion

  • Definition: Non-metallic slag trapped inside the weld metal; common in flux-based processes such as SMAW and flux-cored arc welding.
  • Causes: Inadequate cleaning between passes, incorrect electrode angle, low welding current, poor joint preparation, improper technique, narrow groove angle.
  • Prevention: Remove slag after every pass, correct electrode angle, proper current, sufficient groove preparation, clean the joint before subsequent passes.

3. Lack of Fusion

  • Definition: Weld metal fails to properly fuse with the base metal or with a previous weld pass; incomplete metallurgical bond.
  • Causes: Low welding current, excessive welding speed, incorrect electrode angle, poor surface preparation, improper torch manipulation, excessive travel speed.
  • Prevention: Proper current, correct travel speed, proper joint preparation, correct electrode angle, clean surfaces before welding.

4. Incomplete Penetration

  • Definition: Weld metal does not extend through the required root region; leaves an unwelded portion at the root.
  • Causes: Low welding current, excessive welding speed, incorrect root gap, improper root face thickness, incorrect electrode size, poor joint preparation.
  • Prevention: Correct root gap, suitable welding current, correct electrode size, correct joint preparation, appropriate root-pass techniques.

5. Undercut

  • Definition: A groove formed at the toe of the weld that remains unfilled by weld metal; reduces effective thickness and acts as a stress concentration point.
  • Causes: Excessive welding current, excessive arc length, high welding speed, incorrect electrode angle, poor technique.
  • Prevention: Recommended current, correct arc length, controlled welding speed, proper electrode manipulation, avoid excessive heat input.

6. Overlap

  • Definition: Molten weld metal flows over the surface of the base metal without properly fusing with it; irregular projection at the weld toe.
  • Causes: Low welding speed, excessive filler metal deposition, incorrect electrode angle, low welding current in certain conditions, poor manipulation.
  • Prevention: Appropriate travel speed, correct current, control filler metal deposition, proper electrode angle.

7. Cracks

  • Definition: Fractures or separations in the weld metal or heat-affected zone; among the most serious welding defects.
  • Types: Hot cracks (elevated temperatures during solidification), cold cracks (after cooling, may occur hours later, associated with hydrogen), crater cracks (at end of weld bead), longitudinal cracks (parallel to weld axis), transverse cracks (perpendicular to weld axis).
  • Causes: High residual stresses, hydrogen contamination, rapid cooling, incorrect filler metal, poor joint design, unsuitable parameters, improper termination.
  • Prevention: Suitable low-hydrogen electrodes, correct storage, preheat when required, control cooling rate, suitable filler material, fill end crater properly, follow approved procedures.

8. Excessive Reinforcement

  • Definition: Too much weld metal deposited above the surface of the base metal; excessively raised weld bead.
  • Causes: Excessive filler metal deposition, low welding speed, incorrect electrode manipulation, unsuitable parameters.
  • Prevention: Control filler deposition, proper travel speed, correct parameters, uniform electrode movement.

9. Burn-Through

  • Definition: Excessive heat melts through the full thickness of the base metal and creates a hole; common in thin plates and sheet metal.
  • Causes: Excessive welding current, slow travel speed, excessive heat input, excessive root gap, incorrect electrode size.
  • Prevention: Reduce current, increase travel speed, suitable electrode diameter, control root gap, proper techniques.

10. Spatter

  • Definition: Small droplets of molten metal expelled from the welding arc and deposited around the weld area; common in MIG/MAG and SMAW.
  • Causes: Incorrect voltage and current, excessive arc length, poor grounding, incorrect shielding gas, unstable arc conditions.
  • Prevention: Adjust parameters, correct arc length, proper gas selection, good electrical connections.

11. Crater Defect

  • A depression formed at the termination of a weld bead; if not filled properly, may develop into a crater crack; can become a stress concentration point.
  • Prevention: Gradually reduce current where equipment permits, fill the crater before stopping the arc, use appropriate termination technique.

12. Distortion

  • Definition: Unwanted change in shape or dimensions due to non-uniform heating and cooling during welding.
  • Causes: Uneven heating/cooling, excessive heat input, improper welding sequence, incorrect clamping and fixture arrangement.
  • Types: Longitudinal shrinkage, transverse shrinkage, angular distortion, bowing, buckling.
  • Prevention: Proper welding sequence, suitable clamping and fixtures, control heat input, balanced welding, suitable tack welding, pre-setting or other distortion-control methods.

6.3 Welding Inspection and Testing

Welding inspection is the process of examining welded joints to ensure they meet specified quality, dimensional and safety requirements. It helps identify welding defects before the component is put into service.

Importance of Welding Inspection in Marine Engineering

  • Ensures structural integrity of ship hulls.
  • Prevents leakage in tanks and pipelines.
  • Ensures reliability of pressure-containing equipment.
  • Reduces possibility of structural failure.
  • Improves fatigue resistance and service life.
  • Helps meet classification society requirements.
  • Ensures compliance with approved welding procedures.
  • Improves safety of ships, crew and equipment.

Classification of Inspection Methods

  • Destructive Testing (DT) — test specimen is damaged or destroyed; used to determine mechanical properties and weld performance.
  • Non-Destructive Testing (NDT) — component examined without destroying its usefulness; used to detect surface and internal defects.

6.4 Destructive Testing (DT)

Tensile Test

  • Determines strength when subjected to a pulling force; specimen pulled until fracture.
  • Properties: ultimate tensile strength, yield strength, percentage elongation, reduction in area.

Bend Test

  • Evaluates ductility and soundness of welded joint; specimen bent around a former or mandrel.
  • Types: face bend, root bend, side bend.
  • Detects lack of fusion, incomplete penetration, evaluates weld ductility.

Impact Test

  • Determines ability to resist sudden loading; notched specimen struck by a pendulum; energy absorbed measured.
  • Common test: Charpy V-notch test.
  • Evaluates toughness and resistance to brittle fracture; important for low temperatures and dynamic loads.

Hardness Test

  • Measures resistance to indentation or local plastic deformation.
  • Common tests: Brinell, Rockwell, Vickers.
  • Identifies excessively hard regions, evaluates heat-treatment effects, assesses brittle microstructures.

Macro and Micro Examination

  • Macro: Specimen cut, polished and often etched; cross-section examined at low magnification; reveals penetration, fusion boundaries, bead shape, internal imperfections.
  • Micro: Specimen examined using a microscope; reveals grain structure, phases, microstructural changes.

6.5 Non-Destructive Testing (NDT)

NDT methods are extremely important in marine engineering because welded components can be inspected without destroying them.

1. Visual Testing (VT)

  • Examination using the human eye, sometimes assisted by optical instruments; generally the first inspection method.
  • Equipment: welding inspection gauge, magnifying glass, flashlight, mirror, measuring scale, borescope.
  • Detects: surface cracks, undercut, overlap, excessive reinforcement, surface porosity, incorrect dimensions, visible distortion.
  • Advantages: simple, economical, quick; can be performed before, during and after welding.
  • Limitations: cannot reliably detect internal defects; requires access and lighting; very small defects may be missed.

2. Liquid Penetrant Testing (PT / DPT)

  • Detects surface-breaking defects in non-porous materials; coloured or fluorescent penetrant enters surface openings by capillary action; developer draws it back to the surface.
  • Procedure: clean surface → apply penetrant → dwell time → remove excess → apply developer → development time → inspect → clean.
  • Detects: surface cracks, surface porosity, surface laps, surface-breaking discontinuities.
  • Advantages: simple, relatively inexpensive, suitable for many non-magnetic materials, detects very fine surface-breaking defects.
  • Limitations: only defects open to the surface; surface must be clean; not for porous surfaces.
  • Applications: stainless-steel weld inspection, aluminium components, non-magnetic machinery components.

3. Magnetic Particle Testing (MT / MPI)

  • Detects surface and near-surface discontinuities in ferromagnetic materials; component magnetized; magnetic particles applied; defects cause flux leakage that attracts particles.
  • Principle: magnetize → apply particles → flux leaks at discontinuities → particles accumulate → inspect → demagnetize if required.
  • Detects: surface cracks, near-surface cracks, lack of fusion close to surface.
  • Advantages: fast, highly effective for surface/near-surface defects, can detect fine cracks.
  • Limitations: only ferromagnetic materials; not for aluminium, copper, austenitic stainless steel in ordinary MT; defect orientation affects detectability.
  • Applications: carbon-steel ship structures, steel shafts, ferromagnetic machinery components, steel weld inspection.

4. Radiographic Testing (RT)

  • Uses X-rays or gamma rays to examine internal condition; radiation passes through material and exposes detector; differences in thickness and density produce image variations.
  • Sources: X-ray machine; gamma-ray sources such as Iridium-192 and Cobalt-60.
  • Detects: porosity, slag inclusion, internal cavities, certain cracks, incomplete penetration, some lack of fusion.
  • Advantages: permanent image/digital record, detects internal defects, useful for pipe and pressure-vessel welds, provides location and nature of many internal discontinuities.
  • Limitations: radiation hazard, strict safety controls, relatively expensive, access to both sides often required, planar defects may be difficult to detect depending on orientation.
Important Safety Point: Radiographic testing must be conducted in a controlled radiation area. Warning signs, barriers and radiation monitoring are essential. Unauthorized personnel must remain outside the controlled area.

5. Ultrasonic Testing (UT)

  • Uses high-frequency sound waves to detect internal discontinuities; probe sends waves into component; waves reflect from boundaries or defects; reflected signals analysed.
  • Principle: transducer generates waves → couplant applied → waves enter material → reflect from defects or opposite surface → returning signals received → instrument displays → inspector interprets.
  • Types: pulse-echo, through-transmission, phased array (PAUT), time of flight diffraction (TOFD).
  • Detects: internal cracks, lack of fusion, incomplete penetration, slag inclusions.
  • Advantages: detects internal defects, accurate defect location and depth, no ionizing radiation, can inspect thick components, often requires access from only one side.
  • Limitations: requires skilled operators, interpretation can be difficult, surface condition and geometry affect results, certain small or unfavourably oriented defects difficult to detect, couplant generally required for conventional contact testing.

6. Eddy Current Testing (ET)

  • Electromagnetic NDT method for electrically conductive materials; alternating current passes through a coil and generates a changing magnetic field; induces eddy currents in the material; defects disturb the eddy-current flow and change the coil's electrical response.
  • Detects: surface cracks, near-surface cracks, certain corrosion-related defects, thickness variations, material property variations.
  • Advantages: no direct electrical contact necessary in many applications, fast inspection, no liquid couplant, detects surface and near-surface defects, suitable for automated inspection.
  • Limitations: only electrically conductive materials, penetration depth limited, material properties affect readings, requires calibration and skilled interpretation.

Key Facts – Defects & Inspection

  • Porosity = gas cavities; slag inclusion = trapped non-metallic slag.
  • Lack of fusion = failure of weld metal to fuse properly; incomplete penetration = weld does not reach required root.
  • Undercut = groove at weld toe; overlap = weld metal flows over base metal without proper fusion.
  • Cracks = fractures in weld or HAZ; burn-through = excessive melting through material.
  • Spatter = small droplets of expelled molten metal; distortion = unwanted dimensional/shape change.
  • VT = visual; PT = dye penetrant; MT = magnetic particle; RT = radiographic; UT = ultrasonic; ET = eddy current.
