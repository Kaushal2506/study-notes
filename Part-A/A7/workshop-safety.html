1.1 Introduction to Workshop Safety

Meaning of Workshop Safety

  • Workshop safety refers to the rules, procedures and precautions followed to prevent accidents, injuries, equipment damage and loss of life during workshop operations.
  • It ensures that workers, machines, tools and the working environment remain safe.
  • Workshop safety is particularly important in:
    • Machining operations.
    • Grinding and cutting.
    • Welding and gas cutting.
    • Material handling.
    • Drilling, milling and turning.
    • Maintenance and repair activities.

Objectives of Workshop Safety

  1. Prevent accidents and occupational injuries.
  2. Protect workers from mechanical, electrical, chemical and thermal hazards.
  3. Prevent fire and explosions.
  4. Avoid damage to machinery, tools and equipment.
  5. Maintain a clean and organised workplace.
  6. Improve productivity by reducing accidents and machine downtime.
  7. Ensure compliance with workplace safety regulations.
  8. Develop safe working habits and discipline.

General Workshop Safety Rules

  1. Wear appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE).
  2. Never operate a machine without proper training.
  3. Never remove or bypass machine guards.
  4. Keep hands away from rotating and moving components.
  5. Do not wear loose clothing, ties, jewellery or accessories near rotating machinery.
  6. Tie back long hair and secure it properly.
  7. Never clean, adjust or repair a machine while it is running.
  8. Switch off and isolate the power supply before maintenance.
  9. Keep floors clean, dry and free from obstructions.
  10. Use the correct tool for the job.
  11. Never use damaged tools or defective equipment.
  12. Know the locations of emergency exits, fire extinguishers and first-aid facilities.
  13. Report unsafe conditions, near misses and accidents immediately.
  14. Never engage in horseplay or careless behaviour inside the workshop.

1.2 Definition of PPE

  • Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) refers to specialised clothing, equipment and devices worn by workers to protect themselves from workplace hazards.
  • PPE acts as a protective barrier between the worker and potential sources of injury.
  • PPE is considered the last line of defence in the hierarchy of hazard controls.
  • PPE does not eliminate the hazard; it reduces the risk of injury or exposure.

Examples: Safety helmets, safety shoes, gloves, goggles, face shields, earplugs and respirators.

1.3 Types of PPE and Their Functions

1. Safety Helmet (Hard Hat)

  • Protects the head against falling objects, impact and accidental contact with overhead structures.
  • Provides protection against certain electrical hazards when specifically rated.
  • Commonly used in workshops, construction sites and engine rooms.
  • Must be inspected for cracks, damage and deterioration.

2. Safety Shoes

  • Protect feet against falling tools, heavy components and crushing injuries.
  • Steel-toe or composite-toe shoes provide impact and compression protection.
  • Slip-resistant soles reduce the risk of slipping.
  • Some safety footwear also provides puncture and electrical hazard protection.

3. Safety Goggles

  • Protect eyes against flying particles, dust, sparks and chemical splashes.
  • Provide better eye coverage than ordinary spectacles.
  • Essential during grinding, drilling, chipping and chemical handling.
  • Must fit properly and provide adequate protection for the specific hazard.

4. Face Shield

  • Protects the face against flying particles, sparks, splashes and molten metal.
  • Used during grinding, cutting and certain chemical operations.
  • Should be worn along with safety goggles when eye protection is required.
  • A face shield alone is generally not sufficient eye protection.

5. Safety Gloves

  • Protect hands against cuts, abrasions, burns, chemicals and sharp edges.
  • Different gloves are designed for different hazards.
  • Leather gloves are commonly used for welding and handling hot materials.
  • Cut-resistant gloves are used for sharp metal sheets.
  • Chemical-resistant gloves are selected according to the chemical involved.
Important: Do not wear ordinary gloves near rotating machinery such as lathes and drilling machines because they may get caught in rotating parts.

6. Welding Helmet

  • Protects the face, eyes and neck against welding radiation, sparks and molten metal.
  • Contains a suitable filter lens to protect against ultraviolet (UV) and infrared (IR) radiation.
  • Prevents arc eye, also known as photokeratitis.
  • Auto-darkening helmets automatically adjust the lens shade when the welding arc starts.

7. Hearing Protection

  • Protects ears against excessive industrial noise.
  • Two common types:
    • Earplugs: Inserted into the ear canal.
    • Earmuffs: Cover the entire external ear.
  • Used near compressors, grinders, engines and other noisy machinery.
  • Selection depends on noise level, exposure duration and required noise reduction.

8. Respiratory Protection

  • Protects the respiratory system against dust, fumes, gases and harmful airborne particles.
  • Common types:
    • Disposable particulate respirators.
    • Half-face respirators.
    • Full-face respirators.
    • Supplied-air respirators.
  • Welding operations may require suitable fume extraction and respiratory protection.
  • The respirator must be appropriate for the contaminant and exposure conditions.

9. Protective Clothing and Aprons

  • Protect the body against sparks, heat, molten metal and chemical splashes.
  • Flame-resistant clothing is used during welding and hot work.
  • Leather aprons provide additional protection against sparks and hot particles.
  • Clothing should fit properly without loose ends.

10. Leg and Foot Protection

  • Welding spats and leather gaiters protect lower legs and footwear against sparks and molten metal.
  • Knee protection may be used for prolonged kneeling activities.
  • Special protective clothing may be necessary when handling hot materials.

1.5 Important PPE Precautions

  1. Inspect PPE before every use.
  2. Replace damaged or defective PPE immediately.
  3. Use PPE that matches the particular hazard.
  4. Ensure proper fitting and adjustment.
  5. Keep PPE clean and store it in a designated location.
  6. Never share personal respiratory protection without appropriate cleaning and hygiene arrangements.
  7. Do not assume that one type of PPE protects against every hazard.
  8. PPE must be used along with engineering controls and safe working procedures.

1.6 Hierarchy of Hazard Controls

The hierarchy of hazard controls represents the preferred order of controlling workplace risks.

Hierarchy of Controls

  • 1. Elimination: Completely remove the hazard.
  • 2. Substitution: Replace the hazardous process or material with a safer one.
  • 3. Engineering Controls: Isolate people from the hazard using guards, enclosures or ventilation.
  • 4. Administrative Controls: Use training, warning signs, procedures and work schedules.
  • 5. PPE: Protect the individual using helmets, gloves, goggles and respirators.
Remember: Elimination is the most effective control, while PPE is the last line of defence.

2.1 Definition of Machine Guarding

  • Machine guarding refers to the use of physical barriers, protective devices and safety systems to prevent workers from coming into contact with dangerous machine parts.
  • It prevents injuries caused by rotating, reciprocating, cutting, crushing and moving components.
  • Machine guarding is an important engineering control.

2.2 Purpose of Machine Guarding

  1. Prevent accidental contact with moving machine parts.
  2. Protect workers from entanglement, crushing and cutting injuries.
  3. Prevent flying chips, sparks and fragments from reaching operators.
  4. Reduce the possibility of accidental machine operation.
  5. Protect operators during normal machine operations.
  6. Reduce workshop accidents and downtime.

2.3 Common Machine Hazards

A. Rotating Parts

  • Rotating components can catch clothing, hair, gloves or body parts.
  • Examples:
    • Shafts.
    • Spindles.
    • Pulleys.
    • Gears.
    • Chucks.
    • Rotating cutting tools.
Danger: Entanglement, pulling-in injuries and severe bodily injuries.

B. Reciprocating Parts

  • These parts move repeatedly in a straight line or along a defined path.
  • Examples:
    • Shaper machine ram.
    • Hydraulic press ram.
    • Power hacksaw blade.
Danger: Crushing, trapping and impact injuries.

C. Point of Operation

  • The point of operation is the location where the machine performs work on the material.
  • Examples:
    • Cutting point of a lathe.
    • Drilling point of a drill.
    • Punching point of a press.
    • Grinding contact point.
Danger: Cuts, amputations and crushing injuries.

D. In-running Nip Points

  • Nip points are locations where two moving parts rotate towards each other or where a moving part passes close to a fixed part.
  • Examples:
    • Between two meshing gears.
    • Between a belt and pulley.
    • Between two rollers.
    • Between a chain and sprocket.
Danger: Fingers, hands or clothing may get trapped and pulled inside.

2.4 Types of Machine Guards

1. Fixed Guard

  • A permanent guard attached to the machine.
  • Cannot be moved during normal operation.
  • Requires tools to remove.
  • Provides protection against dangerous moving parts.

Example: Fixed metal enclosure around gears, belts and pulleys.

2. Interlocked Guard

  • Connected to an electrical or mechanical interlocking system.
  • Opening the guard stops the machine or prevents it from starting.
  • The machine cannot operate normally while the guard is open.

Example: Interlocked safety door on a CNC machine.

3. Adjustable Guard

  • Can be adjusted according to the size of the material or workpiece.
  • Provides protection while allowing access to the required working area.

Example: Adjustable guard on a band saw or woodworking machine.

4. Self-Adjusting Guard

  • Automatically adjusts according to the movement or size of the material.
  • Exposes only the portion required for the operation.
  • Returns to its protective position when the workpiece is removed.

Example: Retractable guard on a circular saw.

5. Presence-Sensing Guard

  • Uses sensors to detect the presence of a person or body part in a dangerous zone.
  • Stops or prevents machine operation when the protected area is entered.
  • Commonly uses light curtains, laser scanners or pressure-sensitive devices.

Example: Light curtain fitted to a power press.

2.5 Safety Devices Used in Machines

1. Emergency Stop Button (E-Stop)

  • Used to stop a machine quickly during an emergency.
  • Usually coloured red with a yellow background.
  • Must be easily accessible.
  • It is a supplementary safety measure, not a replacement for machine guards.

2. Two-Hand Control

  • Requires the operator to press two separate buttons simultaneously.
  • Keeps both hands away from the dangerous operating area during the hazardous part of the cycle.
  • Commonly used in power presses.

3. Limit Switch

  • Detects the position or movement of machine components.
  • Can stop or control machine operation when a specific position is reached.

4. Light Curtain

  • Uses infrared or optical beams to create a protective sensing field.
  • If the beam is interrupted, the safety system initiates a stop or prevents hazardous movement.

5. Pressure-Sensitive Mat

  • Detects the presence or weight of a person.
  • Stops the machine or prevents its operation when the mat is activated.

2.7 Safety Precautions While Operating Machines

  1. Inspect the machine before starting.
  2. Ensure all guards are correctly fitted.
  3. Never operate a machine with a missing or damaged guard.
  4. Keep hands away from moving parts.
  5. Never wear loose clothing or jewellery.
  6. Secure long hair.
  7. Remove the chuck key before starting a lathe.
  8. Clamp the workpiece securely.
  9. Do not measure or adjust a workpiece while the machine is running.
  10. Never remove metal chips using bare hands; use a brush or suitable tool.
  11. Stop the machine before cleaning.
  12. Isolate the machine before maintenance.
  13. Do not leave a running machine unattended.
  14. Know the location and operation of emergency stop controls.

2.8 Lockout/Tagout (LOTO)

  • Lockout/Tagout is a safety procedure used to prevent the accidental starting or release of hazardous energy during maintenance, repair and servicing.
  • It ensures that machines remain safely isolated while workers perform maintenance.

Types of hazardous energy

  1. Electrical energy.
  2. Mechanical energy.
  3. Hydraulic energy.
  4. Pneumatic energy.
  5. Thermal energy.
  6. Stored energy in springs, flywheels and pressurised systems.

LOTO Procedure

  1. Inform affected personnel about the maintenance work.
  2. Identify all energy sources connected to the equipment.
  3. Shut down the machine using the normal stopping procedure.
  4. Isolate all energy sources using appropriate isolating devices.
  5. Apply personal locks and warning tags.
  6. Release or safely restrain stored energy.
  7. Verify zero-energy condition by testing that the equipment cannot operate.
  8. Carry out the maintenance work.
  9. After maintenance, ensure all tools and personnel are clear.
  10. Remove locks and tags according to the authorised procedure.
  11. Restore energy and restart the equipment safely.
Important points:
  • Each authorised worker should control their own personal lock.
  • Never remove another worker's lock without following the formal authorised procedure.
  • Pressing the emergency stop button alone does not constitute energy isolation.
  • Stored energy must be released or restrained before work begins.

3.1 Grinding Operations

Definition of Grinding

  • Grinding is a machining process in which material is removed from a workpiece using an abrasive grinding wheel.
  • The grinding wheel rotates at high speed and removes small amounts of material in the form of fine particles.
  • Grinding is commonly used for:
    • Surface finishing.
    • Removing excess material.
    • Sharpening cutting tools.
    • Removing burrs.
    • Achieving dimensional accuracy.
    • Preparing surfaces for welding.

Types of Grinding Machines

  • Bench Grinder: Used for sharpening tools and removing burrs.
  • Angle Grinder: Used for cutting, grinding and surface preparation.
  • Surface Grinder: Used for producing flat and smooth surfaces.
  • Cylindrical Grinder: Used for grinding cylindrical external or internal surfaces.

3.2 Hazards Associated with Grinding

  1. Flying particles: Small metal particles and abrasive fragments may enter the eyes.
  2. Wheel bursting: A damaged or incorrectly operated grinding wheel may break at high speed.
  3. Sparks: Grinding sparks can ignite flammable materials.
  4. Noise: High noise levels can cause hearing damage.
  5. Dust: Grinding may produce harmful metal and abrasive dust.
  6. Entanglement: Loose clothing, gloves or hair can get caught in rotating components.
  7. Burns: Workpieces become hot due to friction.
  8. Electric shock: Damaged electrical cables or improper earthing can cause electric shock.
  9. Kickback: Portable angle grinders may suddenly jerk if the wheel binds or catches.

3.3 Grinding Wheel Safety

A grinding wheel is a rotating abrasive tool. Its safe operation depends on correct selection, inspection, mounting and operating speed.

Important Precautions

  1. Inspect the wheel for cracks, chips and other visible damage.
  2. Check that the wheel is suitable for the material and operation.
  3. Ensure the maximum rated speed of the wheel is equal to or greater than the machine's operating speed.
  4. Never exceed the maximum rated RPM of the grinding wheel.
  5. Use the correct wheel mounting flanges and blotters where specified.
  6. Ensure the wheel is mounted and tightened according to the manufacturer's instructions.
  7. Use proper wheel guards.
  8. Stand clear of the wheel's plane during initial startup.
  9. Allow the wheel to reach full operating speed before grinding.
  10. Never apply excessive pressure.
  11. Never use a wheel that has been dropped or subjected to suspected damage without proper inspection.
  12. Do not use the side of a wheel unless it is specifically designed for side grinding.
  13. Never force a workpiece against the wheel.
  14. Stop the machine if unusual vibration or noise occurs.

Ring Test

  • The ring test is a method used to check a vitrified grinding wheel for cracks before mounting.
  • The wheel is suspended freely, and it is lightly tapped using a non-metallic implement.
  • A sound, undamaged wheel generally produces a clear ringing sound.
  • A cracked wheel may produce a dull or dead sound.
Important:
  • The ring test is not applicable to every type of abrasive wheel, such as certain small, mounted or resin-bonded wheels.
  • A successful ring test does not guarantee that a wheel is free from defects.
  • Visual inspection and manufacturer's instructions must also be followed.

3.4 Bench Grinder Safety Precautions

  1. Ensure the grinding wheel guard is properly installed.
  2. Maintain the work rest as close to the wheel as practicable, generally not more than 3 mm (1/8 inch).
  3. Maintain the upper tongue guard as close to the wheel as practicable, generally not more than 6 mm (1/4 inch).
  4. Adjust guards only when the machine is stopped.
  5. Never adjust the work rest while the wheel is rotating.
  6. Hold the workpiece firmly with both hands where appropriate.
  7. Grind only on the intended working surface of the wheel.
  8. Do not grind excessively small objects that cannot be held safely.
  9. Do not apply excessive pressure.
  10. Use the correct grinding wheel for the material.

Note: These clearances are commonly cited benchmarks for bench and pedestal grinders; applicable regulations and the equipment manufacturer's instructions must be followed.

3.5 Angle Grinder Safety Precautions

  1. Select the correct disc for cutting or grinding.
  2. Check the disc for cracks and damage.
  3. Ensure the disc's maximum rated speed is compatible with the grinder's speed.
  4. Use the correct guard and side handle.
  5. Hold the grinder firmly with both hands.
  6. Secure the workpiece before starting.
  7. Keep sparks directed away from people and combustible materials.
  8. Never force the disc into the workpiece.
  9. Avoid twisting or tilting a cutting disc in the cut.
  10. Allow the wheel to stop completely before putting the grinder down.
  11. Disconnect the power supply before changing the disc.
  12. Never use a cutting disc for side grinding unless specifically designed for it.

3.6 Welding Operations

Definition of Welding

  • Welding is a permanent joining process in which two or more materials are joined by applying heat, pressure or both.
  • Filler material may or may not be used.
  • Welding is widely used in shipbuilding, ship repair, fabrication, pipelines, structural work and machinery maintenance.

Common Types of Welding

1. Arc Welding (SMAW)

  • Also called Shielded Metal Arc Welding or Manual Metal Arc Welding (MMA).
  • Uses an electric arc between a consumable electrode and the workpiece.
  • Electrode coating produces shielding gases and slag.
  • Commonly used in ship repair and fabrication.

2. MIG/MAG Welding (GMAW)

  • Uses a continuously fed consumable wire electrode.
  • Shielding gas protects the weld pool.
  • MIG generally uses inert shielding gas.
  • MAG uses active shielding gas.

3. TIG Welding (GTAW)

  • Uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode.
  • Uses inert shielding gas, commonly argon.
  • Filler metal may be added separately.
  • Produces high-quality, clean welds.

4. Gas Welding

  • Uses heat generated by burning a fuel gas with oxygen.
  • Oxy-acetylene is a common combination.
  • Used for welding, brazing, heating and cutting.

3.7 Hazards Associated with Welding

A. Electric Shock

  • Arc welding uses electrical current to generate heat.
  • Contact with live electrode holders, damaged cables or exposed electrical parts can cause electric shock.
  • Wet conditions increase the risk of electric shock.
  • Electric shock can cause severe burns, cardiac arrest or death.

Precautions:

  1. Inspect welding cables and connections.
  2. Ensure proper electrical earthing and equipment condition.
  3. Keep hands and clothing dry.
  4. Use suitable insulated welding gloves.
  5. Do not touch live electrical components.
  6. Switch off the welding machine before changing connections or carrying out maintenance.
  7. Avoid welding in wet areas unless appropriate controls and specialised equipment are provided.

B. Arc Eye (Photokeratitis)

  • Arc eye is an injury to the cornea caused by exposure to intense ultraviolet radiation from a welding arc.
  • It is also known as welder's flash.
  • Symptoms include:
    • Eye pain.
    • Redness.
    • Excessive watering.
    • Sensitivity to light.
    • A gritty or sandy sensation in the eyes.

Precautions:

  1. Use a suitable welding helmet with the correct filter shade.
  2. Use welding screens or curtains to protect nearby workers.
  3. Never look directly at an active welding arc.
  4. Ensure assistants and nearby personnel have appropriate eye protection.

C. Welding Fumes and Gases

  • Welding produces fumes containing fine metal particles and gases.
  • The composition depends on the base metal, electrode, coating and welding process.
  • Some welding fumes contain hazardous substances such as manganese, chromium and nickel compounds.
  • Harmful gases may include ozone, nitrogen oxides and carbon monoxide.

Precautions:

  1. Provide adequate ventilation.
  2. Use local exhaust ventilation (LEV) near the welding point.
  3. Select appropriate respiratory protection based on the exposure assessment.
  4. Avoid welding on painted, galvanised or coated materials without proper precautions.
  5. Never weld in confined spaces without an authorised confined-space entry procedure and atmospheric monitoring.

D. Burns and Fire Hazards

  • Welding produces sparks, hot metal and molten droplets.
  • These can cause skin burns and ignite combustible materials.

Precautions:

  1. Wear flame-resistant clothing.
  2. Use leather welding gloves.
  3. Cover exposed skin.
  4. Remove flammable materials from the work area.
  5. Use welding screens to protect nearby workers.
  6. Keep suitable fire extinguishers readily available.
  7. Assign a fire watch where required.

E. Explosion Hazards

  • Welding near flammable gases, vapours or combustible dust can cause explosions.
  • Containers that previously held fuel, oil or chemicals may contain explosive residues or vapours.

Precautions:

  1. Never weld on a tank or drum without proper cleaning, isolation, testing and authorisation.
  2. Never weld on a pressurised container.
  3. Ensure gas cylinders are properly secured.
  4. Keep oxygen cylinders away from oil and grease.
  5. Check hoses, regulators and connections for leaks.
  6. Use suitable flashback arrestors in oxy-fuel systems.

3.8 Gas Welding and Gas Cutting Safety

  1. Secure oxygen and fuel gas cylinders in an upright position.
  2. Keep cylinders away from heat sources and direct flames.
  3. Store oxygen cylinders separately from fuel gas cylinders according to applicable separation requirements.
  4. Never allow oil or grease to contact oxygen cylinder valves or fittings.
  5. Use the correct regulator for each gas.
  6. Inspect hoses and connections regularly.
  7. Use leak-detection solution, not a naked flame, to check for gas leaks.
  8. Fit suitable flashback arrestors and non-return valves as required.
  9. Open cylinder valves according to the manufacturer's instructions.
  10. Close cylinder valves when the equipment is not in use.
  11. Keep cylinders away from electrical circuits and welding sparks.
  12. Do not use damaged cylinders or defective regulators.
  13. Do not use oxygen as a substitute for compressed air to clean clothing or the body.

3.9 Welding Screens

  • Welding screens are protective barriers used to prevent nearby workers from being exposed to welding radiation, sparks and flying particles.
  • They are commonly made from specially designed flame-resistant, UV-filtering materials.
  • They help prevent accidental exposure to welding arcs.
  • They should not obstruct ventilation or emergency access.
Remember: Welding screens protect nearby people, while welding helmets protect the welder.

4.1 Definition of Material Handling

  • Material handling refers to the movement, lifting, lowering, transportation and storage of materials within a workplace.
  • It may be performed manually or using mechanical equipment.
  • Safe material handling prevents injuries, material damage and accidents.

Examples:

  • Lifting metal plates.
  • Moving engine components.
  • Handling pipes and steel sections.
  • Transporting heavy tools.
  • Loading and unloading machinery.
  • Moving spare parts in a workshop or engine room.

4.2 Hazards Associated with Manual Handling

  1. Back injuries due to incorrect lifting posture.
  2. Muscle strain and ligament injuries.
  3. Slipped or crushed fingers.
  4. Foot injuries caused by falling materials.
  5. Cuts from sharp edges.
  6. Injuries caused by dropping heavy objects.
  7. Slips and falls while carrying loads.
  8. Fatigue caused by repetitive lifting.
  9. Injuries caused by lifting loads beyond individual capability.

4.3 Correct Manual Lifting Technique

Steps for Safe Lifting

  1. Assess the load – Check its weight, size and shape. Determine whether it is safe to lift manually. Check whether the load contains sharp edges or slippery surfaces.
  2. Plan the movement – Identify the destination. Remove obstacles from the path. Ensure adequate lighting and clear visibility.
  3. Position yourself – Stand close to the load. Keep feet apart to provide a stable base. Position one foot slightly ahead if necessary.
  4. Bend your knees – Bend your knees and hips. Keep your back in a comfortable neutral position. Avoid bending forward excessively.
  5. Get a firm grip – Hold the load securely. Use both hands where possible. Ensure the grip does not slip.
  6. Lift smoothly – Lift using your leg muscles. Keep the load close to your body. Avoid sudden or jerky movements.
  7. Avoid twisting – Do not twist your back while carrying a load. Turn by moving your feet.
  8. Lower carefully – Bend your knees and hips. Lower the load slowly. Keep your fingers clear of trapping points.
Important: If a load is too heavy, awkward or unsafe, use mechanical lifting equipment or seek assistance.

4.4 Mechanical Lifting Equipment

Mechanical equipment is used when materials are too heavy, large or difficult to handle manually.

1. Overhead Crane

  • Used to lift and transport heavy loads horizontally within workshops and factories.
  • Travels along overhead rails.
  • Commonly used for lifting large machinery, engine components and steel structures.

2. Chain Hoist

  • Used for lifting heavy loads vertically.
  • Can be manually operated or electrically powered.
  • Commonly used in workshops and engine rooms.

3. Hydraulic Jack

  • Uses hydraulic pressure to lift heavy loads.
  • Operates according to Pascal's law.
  • Commonly used for lifting machinery, vehicles and heavy components.
Important: Never work underneath a load supported only by a hydraulic jack. Use suitable mechanical supports.

4. Forklift

  • Used for lifting, transporting and stacking palletised materials.
  • Operated by trained and authorised personnel.
  • Load capacity and load centre must be considered.

5. Gantry Crane

  • Consists of a bridge supported by legs that travel on wheels or rails.
  • Used for lifting heavy components in workshops, shipyards and open areas.

6. Pallet Truck

  • Used to move palletised loads over short distances.
  • Available in manual and powered versions.
  • Commonly used in warehouses and stores.

4.5 Lifting Accessories and Their Uses

1. Wire Rope Sling

  • Made from steel wires twisted into strands.
  • Used for lifting heavy machinery and steel structures.
  • Must be inspected for broken wires, corrosion, kinks and deformation.

2. Chain Sling

  • Made from linked metal chains.
  • Used for lifting heavy and hot materials where suitable.
  • Must be inspected for cracks, stretching and damaged links.

3. Webbing Sling

  • Made from synthetic fibres such as polyester.
  • Lightweight and flexible.
  • Suitable for lifting loads that require protection from scratching.
  • Must be checked for cuts, abrasion, chemical damage and heat damage.

4. Shackle

  • A U-shaped or bow-shaped metal connector with a removable pin.
  • Used to connect slings, hooks and lifting points.
  • Must have a suitable rated capacity.

5. Lifting Hook

  • Used to connect lifting equipment to the load.
  • Usually includes a safety latch to prevent accidental disengagement.
  • Must be inspected for cracks, deformation and excessive throat opening.

6. Eye Bolt

  • A threaded bolt with an eye-shaped head.
  • Used as a lifting attachment point.
  • Must be correctly selected, installed and aligned for the intended loading direction.

4.6 Important Lifting Terminology

A. Safe Working Load (SWL)

  • Safe Working Load is the maximum load that lifting equipment is designated to lift safely under specified conditions.
  • It is marked on lifting appliances or accessories where applicable.
  • The load must never exceed the permitted limit.

B. Working Load Limit (WLL)

  • Working Load Limit is the maximum load that lifting equipment or an accessory is designed to carry under specified conditions.
  • It is commonly marked on modern lifting accessories.
  • WLL is determined by the manufacturer and must not be exceeded.

C. Rated Capacity

  • The rated capacity is the maximum load that a lifting machine can safely handle under specified operating conditions.
  • For cranes and forklifts, the capacity may depend on configuration, boom length, load radius and load centre.

D. Safety Factor

  • The safety factor is the ratio of the breaking load to the working load or rated load, as defined for the equipment.

  • A higher safety factor provides a greater margin between the rated working load and the load at which failure occurs.
  • Safety factors vary depending on equipment type, applicable standards and operating conditions.

Example

If a component has a breaking load of 50 kN and a working load of 10 kN:

SF = 5010 = 5

The safety factor is 5.

E. Centre of Gravity (CG)

  • Centre of gravity is the point through which the total weight of a body acts.
  • Correct positioning of lifting points is essential for stable lifting.
  • If the lifting hook is not positioned correctly relative to the centre of gravity, the load may tilt or swing.

F. Sling Angle

  • The sling angle is the angle made by the sling with the horizontal or vertical, depending on the stated convention.
  • Sling tension increases as the sling becomes more horizontal.
  • A smaller angle with the horizontal produces higher tension in each sling leg.

For a symmetrical two-leg sling with angle θ measured from the horizontal:

T = W2 sin θ

Where:

  • T = tension in each sling leg.
  • W = total load.
  • θ = angle of sling with horizontal.
Remember: A flatter sling angle means greater sling tension.

4.7 Safe Lifting Precautions

  1. Always check the weight of the load.
  2. Confirm the SWL or WLL of the lifting equipment and accessories.
  3. Inspect lifting slings, hooks, shackles and chains before use.
  4. Never use damaged or uncertified lifting accessories.
  5. Ensure the load is properly balanced.
  6. Identify the centre of gravity.
  7. Use the correct sling arrangement.
  8. Avoid shock loading and sudden lifting movements.
  9. Never stand or walk underneath a suspended load.
  10. Never lift people using equipment not designed for personnel lifting.
  11. Use tag lines where appropriate to control load rotation or movement.
  12. Maintain clear communication between the crane operator and the signal person.
  13. Use standard hand signals or approved communication systems.
  14. Ensure the lifting area is clear of unnecessary personnel.
  15. Never exceed the rated capacity of the lifting equipment.
  16. Lower the load onto a stable support before releasing the lifting accessories.

4.8 Lifting Signals and Communication

  • A designated signal person communicates with the crane operator during lifting operations.
  • Standard hand signals reduce misunderstanding.
  • Radio communication may be used when the operator cannot see the signal person.
  • One designated signal person should normally control the lift, except that anyone must be able to give an emergency stop signal.
  • Communication must be clear, consistent and understood by all involved.

4.9 Marine Engineering Applications

Safe lifting and handling are especially important aboard ships because:

  1. Engine components such as cylinder heads, pistons and liners are heavy.
  2. Machinery spaces have restricted access and limited working clearances.
  3. Vessel movement can cause suspended loads to swing.
  4. Heavy spare parts may need to be transported through narrow passages.
  5. Improper lifting can damage expensive machinery.
  6. Personnel may be injured by falling or swinging loads.
Important marine precaution: Before lifting heavy machinery onboard, assess vessel movement, sea conditions, lifting arrangements, load stability and the approved lifting procedure.

5.1 Definition of Hot Work

  • Hot work refers to any activity that generates sufficient heat, sparks or flames to ignite combustible materials.
  • Hot work is commonly performed during maintenance, fabrication, repair and construction.
  • It presents a serious fire and explosion risk, especially in ships, engine rooms, tanks and enclosed spaces.

Examples of Hot Work

  1. Arc welding.
  2. Gas welding.
  3. Gas cutting.
  4. Brazing.
  5. Soldering involving ignition sources or significant heat.
  6. Grinding.
  7. Flame heating.
  8. Use of equipment that produces sparks in hazardous areas.

5.2 Definition of Hot Work Permit

  • A Hot Work Permit is a formal written authorisation issued by an authorised person before carrying out hot work in a designated controlled area.
  • It confirms that the hazards have been identified and necessary safety precautions have been implemented.
  • It ensures that the work is properly planned, authorised, monitored and completed safely.
Important: A hot work permit is not merely a formality. It is a safety control system.

5.3 Purpose of Hot Work Permit

  1. Prevent fires and explosions.
  2. Identify flammable and combustible materials.
  3. Ensure the work area is safe before work begins.
  4. Confirm that required fire protection equipment is available.
  5. Ensure adequate ventilation.
  6. Verify that nearby operations do not create additional hazards.
  7. Establish responsibilities and emergency arrangements.
  8. Ensure that the area is inspected after completion.

5.4 Information Included in a Hot Work Permit

A typical hot work permit contains:

  1. Name of the person performing the work.
  2. Location where the work will be carried out.
  3. Description of the hot work activity.
  4. Date and time of permit validity.
  5. Identification of hazards.
  6. Required PPE.
  7. Fire prevention measures.
  8. Gas testing requirements, where applicable.
  9. Ventilation arrangements.
  10. Isolation of nearby equipment.
  11. Availability of fire extinguishers.
  12. Fire watch arrangements.
  13. Authorising person's approval.
  14. Completion and permit closure details.

5.5 Hot Work Permit Procedure

5.6 Precautions Before Starting Hot Work

  1. Obtain a valid hot work permit.
  2. Conduct a risk assessment.
  3. Inspect the work area.
  4. Remove combustible and flammable materials.
  5. Protect nearby equipment and surfaces from sparks.
  6. Cover drains and openings where flammable vapours may be present.
  7. Isolate connected pipelines, tanks and machinery as required.
  8. Ensure suitable fire extinguishers are available.
  9. Arrange adequate ventilation.
  10. Conduct atmospheric testing where necessary.
  11. Check oxygen, flammable gas and toxic gas concentrations as required by the permit.
  12. Ensure the welding equipment and electrical cables are in good condition.
  13. Assign a trained fire watch where required.
  14. Inform nearby personnel about the hot work activity.
  15. Confirm that emergency escape routes remain clear.

5.7 Fire Watch

  • A fire watch is a trained person assigned to monitor hot work activities for signs of fire.
  • The fire watch is responsible for identifying sparks, flames and smouldering materials.
  • The person must be familiar with the location and use of firefighting equipment.

Responsibilities of Fire Watch

  1. Monitor the hot work area continuously during the activity.
  2. Watch for sparks reaching nearby combustible materials.
  3. Check adjacent spaces and the opposite side of bulkheads or decks when heat can pass through.
  4. Keep firefighting equipment readily accessible.
  5. Stop work if unsafe conditions develop.
  6. Raise the alarm if a fire occurs.
  7. Continue monitoring after the work is completed for the period specified by the permit and risk assessment.
Important: A fire watch must not be assigned other duties that interfere with effective fire monitoring.

5.8 Fire Triangle

The fire triangle explains the three essential elements required for combustion.

Three Elements of Fire

  1. Heat – Provides the energy required to initiate and sustain combustion. Examples: Sparks, flames and hot surfaces.
  2. Fuel – A combustible substance that burns. Examples: Wood, paper, oil, petrol and combustible gases.
  3. Oxygen – Supports the combustion process. Usually supplied by the surrounding air.

Principle: Removing any one of these three elements can extinguish or prevent a fire.

Fire Tetrahedron

  • The fire tetrahedron is an expanded model of the fire triangle.
  • It includes a fourth element called the chemical chain reaction.
  • The four elements are:
    1. Heat.
    2. Fuel.
    3. Oxygen.
    4. Chemical chain reaction.
  • Certain fire extinguishing agents work by interrupting the chemical chain reaction.

5.9 Classes of Fire

Fire classifications differ between national standards. The following table uses the commonly applied Indian/BIS classification.

ClassType of fireExamples
Class AOrdinary combustible solidsWood, paper, cloth, rubber
Class BFlammable liquids and liquefiable solidsPetrol, diesel, paint, oil
Class CFlammable gasesLPG, propane, acetylene
Class DCombustible metalsMagnesium, sodium, aluminium powder
Class FCooking oils and fatsVegetable oils and cooking fats
Electrical fires: In India, fires involving energised electrical equipment are often described separately as electrical fires rather than as a numbered fire class. Once isolated, the burning material determines the appropriate fire classification.

5.10 Types of Fire Extinguishers

1. Water Extinguisher

  • Mainly suitable for Class A fires.
  • Cools burning materials by removing heat.
  • Must not be used on energised electrical equipment or flammable-liquid fires.

2. Foam Extinguisher

  • Commonly suitable for Class A and certain Class B fires.
  • Forms a blanket over the burning liquid.
  • Helps suppress vapour release and cool the burning material.
  • Not suitable for energised electrical equipment unless specifically rated for such use.

3. Carbon Dioxide (CO₂) Extinguisher

  • Commonly used for flammable-liquid and electrical-equipment fires.
  • Displaces oxygen around the fire and helps suppress combustion.
  • Leaves no solid residue.
  • Has limited cooling effect, so fire may reignite.
  • Can cause suffocation in confined spaces.

4. Dry Chemical Powder (DCP) Extinguisher

  • ABC powder is commonly used for Class A, B and C fires and suitable electrical hazards.
  • BC powder is intended for Class B and C fires.
  • Interrupts the chemical chain reaction.
  • May reduce visibility and contaminate sensitive machinery.

5. Wet Chemical Extinguisher

  • Mainly used for Class F cooking oil and fat fires.
  • Cools the burning oil.
  • Produces a soap-like layer through a process called saponification.
  • Helps prevent re-ignition.

6. Special Dry Powder Extinguisher

  • Designed for specific combustible-metal fires.
  • The extinguishing agent must be selected according to the metal involved.
  • Ordinary ABC powder and water must not be assumed suitable for metal fires.

5.11 PASS Method of Fire Extinguisher Operation

PASS is a commonly used method for operating a portable fire extinguisher.

  • P – Pull: Pull the safety pin.
  • A – Aim: Aim the nozzle at the base of the fire.
  • S – Squeeze: Squeeze the operating handle.
  • S – Sweep: Sweep the nozzle from side to side across the base of the fire.
Important precautions:
  1. Use an extinguisher suitable for the fire class.
  2. Maintain a safe escape route behind you.
  3. Do not approach a fire that is spreading rapidly.
  4. Raise the alarm and evacuate if the fire cannot be controlled immediately.
  5. Never use an extinguisher if doing so puts your life at risk.

5.12 General Fire Prevention Precautions in Workshops

  1. Store flammable liquids in approved containers.
  2. Keep combustible materials away from hot work areas.
  3. Avoid accumulation of oily rags and waste.
  4. Maintain good housekeeping.
  5. Ensure electrical installations are properly maintained.
  6. Do not overload electrical sockets.
  7. Prevent oil and grease leakage.
  8. Keep ventilation systems clean and functional.
  9. Ensure fire extinguishers are accessible and inspected.
  10. Keep emergency exits and escape routes unobstructed.
  11. Display emergency contact numbers.
  12. Train workers in fire prevention and emergency response.

Workshop safety becomes even more important in the marine industry because ships operate in confined spaces, often with limited access to emergency assistance.

6.1 Common Hazards in Shipboard Workshops and Engine Rooms

  1. Rotating machinery: Exposed shafts, pumps, fans and engine components.
  2. High temperatures: Steam lines, exhaust pipes, boilers and hot surfaces.
  3. Electrical hazards: Generators, motors, switchboards and electrical panels.
  4. Noise: Main engines, auxiliary engines, compressors and pumps.
  5. Oil leakage: Slippery surfaces and fire hazards.
  6. Confined spaces: Tanks, void spaces, boilers and certain machinery enclosures.
  7. Heavy lifting: Engine parts, cylinder heads, pistons and other machinery components.
  8. Toxic gases: Carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulphide and other hazardous gases depending on the space and work.
  9. Fire and explosion: Fuel oil, lubricating oil, gas and welding activities.
  10. Vessel movement: Rolling and pitching can destabilise workers and suspended loads.

6.2 Additional Shipboard Safety Precautions

  1. Follow the vessel's Safety Management System (SMS).
  2. Obtain the required permits before undertaking controlled work.
  3. Follow the Chief Engineer's and authorised officers' instructions.
  4. Never bypass machinery safety interlocks.
  5. Isolate machinery before carrying out maintenance.
  6. Check for pressure, temperature and stored energy before opening equipment.
  7. Use suitable PPE.
  8. Keep engine-room floors clean and free from oil.
  9. Ensure proper ventilation.
  10. Never enter a confined space without authorised entry procedures.
  11. Check the weather and vessel movement before carrying out lifting operations.
  12. Keep escape routes clear.
  13. Report unsafe conditions immediately.

6.3 Confined Space Safety – Important Marine Concept

  • A confined space is an enclosed or partially enclosed space that may have limited access and inadequate natural ventilation.
  • Such spaces may contain hazardous atmospheres or have other serious safety risks.

Examples

  • Cargo tanks.
  • Ballast tanks.
  • Fuel oil tanks.
  • Freshwater tanks.
  • Double-bottom spaces.
  • Certain enclosed machinery spaces.

Major Hazards

  1. Oxygen deficiency.
  2. Oxygen enrichment.
  3. Flammable gases and vapours.
  4. Toxic gases.
  5. Heat stress.
  6. Restricted access and difficult rescue.

Essential Precautions

  1. Obtain an authorised confined-space entry permit.
  2. Isolate connected systems.
  3. Ventilate the space.
  4. Test the atmosphere using calibrated equipment.
  5. Confirm acceptable oxygen, flammable gas and toxic gas readings.
  6. Maintain continuous or periodic monitoring as required.
  7. Provide a trained attendant outside the space.
  8. Maintain communication.
  9. Keep suitable rescue arrangements ready.
  10. Never enter a suspected hazardous atmosphere to attempt an unprotected rescue.
Important: A hot work permit and a confined-space entry permit are different controls. Welding inside a tank may require both permits and additional safeguards.

Safety signs communicate hazards, mandatory actions and emergency information.

Red – Prohibition / Fire Equipment

  • Indicates prohibited actions.
  • Also identifies fire-fighting equipment.
  • Example: No smoking, fire extinguisher location.

Yellow – Warning

  • Indicates potential hazards or danger.
  • Example: Electrical hazard, hot surface, slippery floor.

Blue – Mandatory Action

  • Indicates actions that must be followed.
  • Example: Wear safety helmet, eye protection or hearing protection.

Green – Safe Condition / Emergency Information

  • Indicates emergency exits, first aid and safe conditions.
  • Example: Emergency exit, assembly point, first-aid station.

9.1 PPE

  • PPE = Personal Protective Equipment.
  • PPE is the last line of defence in the hierarchy of controls.
  • Safety helmet → Head protection.
  • Safety shoes → Foot protection.
  • Goggles → Eye protection.
  • Face shield → Face protection.
  • Welding helmet → UV and IR radiation protection.
  • Earplugs and earmuffs → Hearing protection.
  • Respirator → Protection against harmful airborne contaminants.
  • Leather gloves → Commonly used for welding.
  • Ordinary gloves should not be worn near rotating machinery if they create entanglement risk.

9.2 Machine Guarding

  • Fixed guard → Permanently attached.
  • Interlocked guard → Opening the guard stops or prevents machine operation.
  • Adjustable guard → Manually adjusted according to the work.
  • Self-adjusting guard → Automatically adjusts to the workpiece.
  • Presence-sensing guard → Detects entry into a hazardous zone.
  • E-Stop → Emergency stopping device.
  • LOTO → Prevents accidental starting during maintenance.
  • Nip point → Area where moving parts can trap objects or body parts.
  • Point of operation → Area where the machine performs work.

9.3 Grinding

  • Grinding uses an abrasive wheel to remove material.
  • Grinding wheel must not exceed its rated RPM.
  • Ring test → Used for detecting cracks in suitable vitrified wheels.
  • Wheel guard → Protects against fragments and contact.
  • Bench grinder work-rest clearance → Generally maximum 3 mm.
  • Bench grinder tongue-guard clearance → Generally maximum 6 mm.
  • Never stand in the plane of the wheel during initial startup.
  • Never use a damaged grinding wheel.

9.4 Welding

  • Arc eye = Photokeratitis.
  • UV radiation is a major cause of arc eye.
  • Welding helmet protects the eyes and face.
  • Welding produces fumes and gases.
  • Local Exhaust Ventilation helps remove welding fumes.
  • Arc welding uses an electric arc.
  • TIG welding uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode.
  • MIG/MAG welding uses a continuously fed consumable wire electrode.
  • Oxy-acetylene welding uses oxygen and acetylene.
  • Flashback arrestors help prevent flame propagation into the gas supply system.

9.5 Lifting and Handling

  • Correct lifting technique → Bend knees, maintain a neutral back and keep the load close.
  • SWL = Safe Working Load.
  • WLL = Working Load Limit.
  • Safety Factor = Breaking Load / Working Load.
  • Centre of gravity determines load balance.
  • Flatter sling angle = Higher sling tension.
  • Never stand under a suspended load.
  • Never work under a hydraulic jack-supported load without mechanical support.
  • Inspect slings, hooks, shackles and lifting accessories before use.

9.6 Hot Work and Fire Safety

  • Hot work = Activity generating heat, flames or sparks.
  • Hot work permit = Formal authorisation before controlled hot work.
  • Fire watch = Person monitoring hot work for fire.
  • Fire triangle = Heat + Fuel + Oxygen.
  • Fire tetrahedron = Heat + Fuel + Oxygen + Chemical chain reaction.
  • PASS = Pull, Aim, Squeeze, Sweep.
  • Water extinguisher → Class A fires.
  • Foam extinguisher → Suitable Class A and B fires.
  • CO₂ extinguisher → Commonly used for electrical equipment and Class B fires.
  • ABC powder → Class A, B and C fires and suitable electrical hazards.
  • Wet chemical → Class F fires.
  • Special dry powder → Suitable combustible-metal fires.
  • Never weld on a tank without proper preparation, isolation, testing and authorisation.
  • Confined-space entry and hot work permits are separate requirements.
