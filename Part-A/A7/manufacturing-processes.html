1.1 Introduction to Casting

  • Casting is a manufacturing process in which molten metal is poured into a mould cavity of the desired shape and allowed to solidify.
  • After solidification, the metal takes the shape of the mould cavity.
  • The solidified component is called a casting.
  • The mould is usually made from sand, metal, ceramic or other heat-resistant materials.

Basic principle

  1. Metal is heated above its melting point.
  2. Molten metal is poured into a prepared mould cavity.
  3. The molten metal fills the cavity.
  4. Heat is removed from the molten metal.
  5. The metal solidifies and takes the shape of the cavity.
  6. The casting is removed from the mould.
  7. Finishing operations are performed to obtain the required dimensions and surface quality.

Applications of casting

Casting is particularly useful for manufacturing components that are:

  • Complex in shape.
  • Large in size.
  • Difficult to manufacture by machining.
  • Having internal cavities or complicated passages.
  • Required in large or small production quantities, depending on the casting method.

Examples: Engine blocks, cylinder heads, pump casings, valve bodies, impellers, flywheels, gearbox housings, marine engine components, ship propellers.

1.2 Basic Terminology Used in Casting

1. Pattern

  • A pattern is a replica or model of the component to be cast.
  • It is used to create the mould cavity.
  • It is generally made slightly larger than the final component to compensate for shrinkage and machining.
  • Pattern materials include: wood, metal, plastic, wax.
Important: A pattern is not the actual casting. It is used to produce the mould cavity.

2. Mould

  • A mould is a hollow cavity into which molten metal is poured.
  • It determines the external shape and size of the casting.
  • It can be made from sand, metal or ceramic materials.

3. Core

  • A core is a separate insert placed inside a mould to produce internal cavities or hollow portions.
  • It is generally made of specially prepared sand.
  • Cores are supported by core prints or other supporting arrangements.

Examples: Internal water passages in engine components, hollow pipes, internal cavities in pump casings.

4. Cope and Drag

  • Cope: Upper half of the mould.
  • Drag: Lower half of the mould.
  • The joint between them is called the parting line or parting surface.

5. Flask

  • A flask is the rigid frame that holds the moulding sand.
  • It generally consists of cope and drag sections.

6. Sprue

  • A vertical passage through which molten metal enters the mould from the pouring basin.
  • It directs molten metal towards the runner.

7. Runner

  • A horizontal passage that carries molten metal from the sprue to the mould cavity.

8. Gate

  • A small passage connecting the runner to the mould cavity.
  • It controls the entry of molten metal into the cavity.

9. Riser

  • A reservoir of molten metal provided in the mould.
  • It supplies additional molten metal during solidification to compensate for shrinkage.
  • It helps prevent shrinkage cavities in the casting.
Remember: A riser acts as a reservoir of molten metal.

10. Pouring Basin

  • A funnel-shaped opening at the top of the sprue.
  • It receives molten metal from the ladle and directs it into the sprue.

11. Vent

  • Small passages provided in the mould to allow gases and air to escape.
  • They help prevent gas-related defects.

1.3 Pattern Allowances

A pattern is not made exactly equal to the final casting dimensions because certain dimensional changes occur during casting. These corrections are called pattern allowances.

A. Shrinkage Allowance

  • Metal contracts during cooling and solidification.
  • Therefore, the pattern is made slightly larger than the required casting.
  • This compensates for the reduction in dimensions.
  • It is a positive allowance.

B. Machining Allowance

  • Additional material is provided on the pattern for subsequent machining.
  • It ensures that the final component achieves accurate dimensions and surface finish.
  • It is generally positive.

C. Draft Allowance

  • A small taper is provided on vertical surfaces of the pattern.
  • It helps in easy removal of the pattern from the sand mould without damaging the mould cavity.

D. Distortion Allowance

  • Some castings may warp or bend during cooling due to unequal contraction.
  • The pattern is intentionally made with an opposite curvature to compensate for the expected distortion.

E. Rapping or Shake Allowance

  • The pattern is slightly shaken before removal from the sand mould.
  • This increases the mould cavity dimensions.
  • Therefore, a negative allowance is provided on the pattern to compensate.

1.4 Types of Casting Processes

A. Sand Casting

  • One of the oldest and most widely used casting processes.
  • A mould is prepared using sand mixed with suitable binders and moisture.
  • Molten metal is poured into the sand cavity.
  • After solidification, the sand mould is broken to remove the casting.

Types of sand used:

  1. Green sand: Contains silica sand, clay, water and additives. Used in moist condition. Commonly used for general-purpose casting.
  2. Dry sand: The mould is dried or baked before pouring. Provides better strength and dimensional stability than ordinary green sand moulds.
  3. Loam sand: Contains sand, clay and other materials. Used for certain large castings.

Advantages: Low tooling cost; suitable for large components; can cast complex shapes; suitable for ferrous and non-ferrous metals; can produce small and large quantities.

Disadvantages: Comparatively poor surface finish; lower dimensional accuracy than precision casting methods; sand moulds are generally destroyed after use; may produce sand inclusions and other casting defects.

Applications: Engine blocks, pump bodies, machine beds, valve housings, large marine machinery casings.

B. Die Casting

  • Molten metal is forced into a metallic mould under high pressure.
  • The mould is called a die.
  • Dies are generally made from heat-resistant tool steel.
  • It is mainly used for non-ferrous metals such as aluminium, zinc and magnesium alloys.

Types:

  1. Hot Chamber Die Casting: The injection mechanism is immersed in molten metal. Metal is injected directly into the die cavity. Suitable for metals with relatively low melting temperatures. Commonly used for zinc and certain magnesium alloys.
  2. Cold Chamber Die Casting: Molten metal is poured into a separate injection chamber. A piston forces it into the die cavity. Suitable for aluminium and copper alloys. Used when the molten metal would damage or excessively attack the hot-chamber injection system.

Advantages: High production rate; good surface finish; high dimensional accuracy; suitable for mass production; thin-walled components can be produced.

Disadvantages: High initial die cost; not economical for very small production quantities; generally unsuitable for large, heavy components; gas porosity may occur due to trapped air.

Applications: Automotive housings, aluminium components, electrical equipment housings, small machinery components.

C. Investment Casting (Lost Wax Process)

  • Also known as the lost wax process.
  • A wax pattern of the required component is prepared.
  • The wax pattern is coated with refractory ceramic material.
  • The coating is dried and hardened.
  • Wax is melted and removed, leaving a cavity.
  • Molten metal is poured into the cavity.
  • After solidification, the ceramic shell is broken.

Advantages: Excellent dimensional accuracy; good surface finish; can produce intricate shapes; suitable for complex internal and external features; less machining may be required.

Disadvantages: Expensive process; time-consuming; limited suitability for very large components; wax and ceramic shell preparation require careful control.

Applications: Turbine blades, precision valves, small impellers, aerospace components, complex engineering parts.

Marine engineering connection: Investment casting is useful for producing intricate turbine and machinery components where dimensional precision and complex geometry are important.

D. Centrifugal Casting

  • Molten metal is poured into a rapidly rotating mould.
  • Centrifugal force pushes the molten metal towards the mould wall.
  • The metal solidifies from the outer region towards the inner region, depending on the cooling arrangement.
  • It is particularly suitable for hollow cylindrical components.

Types:

  1. True centrifugal casting: Used to manufacture hollow cylindrical components. Examples: pipes, cylinder liners and sleeves.
  2. Semi-centrifugal casting: Used for solid or partially hollow axisymmetric components. Examples: wheels and pulleys.
  3. Centrifuging: Multiple mould cavities are arranged around a central axis. Centrifugal force helps fill the cavities.

Advantages: Good density in the outer portions; reduced need for cores in true centrifugal casting; suitable for pipes and cylindrical components; can produce sound castings with fewer gas-related defects in many applications.

Disadvantages: Equipment is expensive; limited to suitable shapes; some impurities may concentrate near the inner surface.

Applications: Cast iron pipes, cylinder liners, bushes, sleeves, rings.

E. Permanent Mould Casting

  • Uses a reusable metallic mould.
  • Molten metal is poured into the mould, generally under gravity.
  • The mould is opened after solidification to remove the casting.
  • Commonly used with aluminium and other non-ferrous alloys.

Advantages: Better surface finish than ordinary sand casting; good dimensional accuracy; reusable mould; higher production rate than sand casting in suitable applications.

Disadvantages: High initial mould cost; not ideal for very large components; complex internal cavities may require cores.

F. Shell Moulding

  • A thin shell of sand mixed with thermosetting resin is formed over a heated metal pattern.
  • Heat hardens the resin and binds the sand particles.
  • Two shell halves are joined to form the mould.
  • Molten metal is poured into the cavity.

Advantages: Good surface finish; good dimensional accuracy; suitable for mass production; less moulding sand is required than in conventional sand casting.

Disadvantages: Higher cost than ordinary sand moulding; requires heated metal patterns; resin binders increase material and processing costs.

1.5 Solidification of Metals

Solidification is the transformation of molten metal into a solid state due to heat removal.

Stages of Solidification

  1. Molten metal loses heat after entering the mould.
  2. The metal reaches its solidification temperature or freezing range.
  3. Solid nuclei begin to form.
  4. Crystals grow from these nuclei.
  5. Grains develop until the metal becomes completely solid.

Types of Grain Structures

  • Chill zone: Fine grains near the mould wall due to rapid cooling.
  • Columnar grains: Elongated grains growing towards the hotter region.
  • Equiaxed grains: Approximately equal-sized grains with different orientations.

Factors Affecting Solidification

  • Type of metal.
  • Mould material.
  • Mould temperature.
  • Casting thickness.
  • Cooling rate.
  • Heat transfer conditions.
  • Use of chills and risers.

Chills

  • Chills are metallic inserts placed in a mould to increase the local cooling rate.
  • They promote directional solidification.
  • They help reduce shrinkage defects in selected regions.

Important Defects to Understand

  1. Blowholes – Caused by gas trapped in the molten metal or mould. Usually appear as rounded cavities. Proper venting and moisture control help prevent them.
  2. Shrinkage Cavity – Occurs due to volume reduction during solidification. Usually appears in thicker sections. Proper riser design and directional solidification help prevent it.
  3. Misrun – Occurs when molten metal solidifies before completely filling the mould. Can be caused by low pouring temperature or insufficient fluidity.
  4. Cold Shut – Occurs when two streams of molten metal meet but fail to fuse properly. Produces a visible line or seam.
  5. Hot Tears – Develop when solidifying metal is prevented from contracting freely. Often occur at sharp corners or regions of stress concentration.

1.7 Important Casting Properties

  • Fluidity: Ability of molten metal to flow and fill the mould.
  • Shrinkage: Reduction in volume or dimensions during cooling.
  • Permeability: Ability of moulding sand to allow gases to pass through it.
  • Refractoriness: Ability of moulding material to withstand high temperatures without melting or fusing.
  • Collapsibility: Ability of the mould to break down after solidification, allowing contraction.
  • Strength: Ability of moulding material to maintain its shape during handling and pouring.

2.1 Introduction to Forging

  • Forging is a metal forming process in which metal is shaped by applying compressive forces.
  • The forces are generally applied using a hammer, press or forging machine.
  • The metal undergoes plastic deformation and takes the desired shape.
  • Forging may be performed at hot, warm or cold temperatures.

Basic Principle

  1. A metal billet or workpiece is selected.
  2. The workpiece is heated if hot forging is required.
  3. The workpiece is placed between dies or on an anvil.
  4. Compressive force is applied.
  5. The metal flows and changes shape.
  6. The forged component is removed.
  7. Trimming, heat treatment and machining may be performed.

Why Is Forging Important?

Forging improves the mechanical properties of many metals because:

  • The metal undergoes plastic deformation.
  • Internal voids may be closed under suitable forging conditions.
  • Grain flow follows the shape of the component.
  • Mechanical properties can improve, particularly fatigue strength and toughness in appropriate directions.

Marine engineering applications: Crankshafts, connecting rods, propeller shafts, gear blanks, anchor components, heavy-duty bolts, marine engine parts.

2.2 Types of Forging Based on Temperature

A. Hot Forging

  • Forging is carried out above the recrystallization temperature of the metal.
  • The metal is heated before deformation.
  • Recrystallization occurs during or after deformation, depending on the process conditions.

Advantages: Requires comparatively lower forming forces; large deformation is possible; improved ductility; suitable for large components; grain structure can be refined through controlled processing.

Disadvantages: Oxidation and scaling may occur; lower dimensional accuracy than cold forging; surface finish is comparatively poor; heating equipment is required.

B. Cold Forging

  • Forging is carried out at or near room temperature, below the recrystallization temperature.
  • The metal is shaped without significant external heating.

Advantages: Excellent dimensional accuracy; good surface finish; increased strength and hardness due to strain hardening; less material wastage; no heating scale formation.

Disadvantages: High forming forces are required; ductility decreases due to work hardening; suitable only for materials with sufficient formability; intermediate annealing may be required for large deformation.

C. Warm Forging

  • Forging is performed at a temperature between cold and hot forging conditions.
  • It reduces the forming force compared with cold forging.
  • It can offer better dimensional control than hot forging.

Advantages: Lower forming forces than cold forging; reduced oxidation compared with hot forging; better dimensional accuracy than hot forging in many applications; suitable for certain high-strength materials.

2.3 Types of Forging Based on Method

A. Open-Die Forging

  • The workpiece is compressed between two dies that do not completely enclose it.
  • Metal is free to flow laterally.
  • It is commonly used for large and relatively simple components.

Advantages: Suitable for large components; flexible manufacturing process; lower tooling cost; suitable for low production quantities.

Disadvantages: Requires skilled operators; lower dimensional accuracy; complex shapes are difficult to produce.

Applications: Large shafts, rings, large cylinders, marine propeller shafts, heavy machinery components.

B. Closed-Die Forging (Impression-Die Forging)

  • The heated metal is placed between dies having shaped impressions.
  • The dies close and force the metal to flow into the cavity.
  • Excess material may flow out through a narrow gap, forming flash.
  • Flash is removed by a trimming operation.

Advantages: Can produce complex shapes; good mechanical properties; better dimensional accuracy than open-die forging; suitable for mass production.

Disadvantages: Higher die cost; flash material is wasted unless recycled; requires accurate billet volume and die design.

Applications: Connecting rods, crankshafts, gear blanks, spanners, automotive components.

C. Drop Forging

  • A hammer or ram is raised and dropped onto a heated workpiece.
  • Repeated impact forces deform the workpiece.
  • It is often performed using impression dies.
Important: Drop forging is an impact-based forging process.

D. Press Forging

  • Metal is deformed by slowly applying continuous compressive force.
  • Hydraulic or mechanical presses are commonly used.
  • Deformation generally penetrates more uniformly through the workpiece than in a short-impact hammer blow.

Advantages: Better control over deformation; suitable for large components; lower noise and vibration than hammer forging; good production consistency.

E. Upset Forging

  • The length of a workpiece is reduced while its cross-sectional area increases.
  • It is commonly used to form enlarged heads at the end of rods or bars.

Applications: Bolt heads, rivet heads, fasteners, valve stems.

F. Roll Forging

  • The workpiece passes between specially shaped rotating rolls.
  • The rolls compress and shape the material.
  • It is used to produce tapered and stepped components.

Applications: Axles, leaf springs, tapered shafts, hand tools.

Important Points

  • Excessively low forging temperature can cause cracking.
  • Excessively high temperature can cause overheating or burning.
  • Improper die design can cause laps and underfilling.
  • Improper material handling can cause scale-related defects.

2.5 Grain Flow in Forging

  • During forging, grains deform and elongate in the direction of metal flow.
  • This produces directional grain flow.
  • Properly designed forging can align grain flow with the major stresses experienced by the component.
  • This can improve fatigue resistance and toughness in the favourable direction.
Remember:
  • Forging generally produces better directional grain flow than machining a component from a solid billet.
  • Cast components generally have a different solidification grain structure.
  • Grain flow is an important reason forged components are preferred for many highly stressed machinery parts.

PART A: ROLLING

3.1 Introduction to Rolling

  • Rolling is a metal forming process in which metal is passed between two or more rotating rolls.
  • The rolls apply compressive forces to reduce the thickness or change the cross-section of the workpiece.
  • The material undergoes plastic deformation.
  • The length of the material generally increases as its thickness decreases.

Basic Working Principle

  1. A metal slab, billet or ingot is prepared.
  2. It is passed between rotating rolls.
  3. The rolls grip the material through friction.
  4. Compressive force reduces the thickness.
  5. The material exits with a modified cross-section.
  6. Further rolling passes may be performed to achieve the required dimensions.

Applications

  • Steel plates.
  • Shipbuilding plates.
  • Railway tracks.
  • Structural sections.
  • Sheets and strips.
  • Pipes and tubes.
  • Bars and rods.
Marine engineering connection: Rolling is extremely important in shipbuilding because large steel plates and structural sections used in ship hulls are manufactured through rolling.

3.2 Types of Rolling Based on Temperature

A. Hot Rolling

  • Rolling is performed above the recrystallization temperature of the metal.
  • The material is heated before passing through the rolls.
  • Large thickness reductions can be achieved.

Advantages: Lower deformation resistance; large reductions are possible; suitable for large components; no significant strain hardening during normal hot-working conditions; can refine the grain structure under controlled processing.

Disadvantages: Oxidation and scale formation; lower dimensional accuracy than cold rolling; comparatively poor surface finish; requires heating facilities.

Applications: Shipbuilding plates, structural steel sections, railway rails, large steel sheets, heavy plates.

B. Cold Rolling

  • Rolling is carried out below the recrystallization temperature, generally at room temperature.
  • It produces thinner and more accurate products.

Advantages: Excellent surface finish; better dimensional accuracy; increased strength and hardness due to strain hardening; better thickness control; suitable for thin sheets and strips.

Disadvantages: Higher rolling forces; limited deformation per pass; ductility decreases; intermediate annealing may be necessary.

Applications: Automobile body sheets, electrical steel sheets, precision strips, thin metal sheets.

3.3 Types of Rolling Mills

A. Two-High Rolling Mill

  • Consists of two rolls.
  • The rolls rotate in opposite directions at the contact region.
  • The workpiece passes between them.
  • Types: Reversing two-high mill (direction of rotation can be reversed); Non-reversing two-high mill (rolls rotate in one direction only).

B. Three-High Rolling Mill

  • Consists of three rolls arranged vertically.
  • The workpiece can pass alternately through the upper and lower roll gaps.
  • It reduces the need to reverse roll rotation.

C. Four-High Rolling Mill

  • Consists of two small work rolls and two larger backup rolls.
  • Small work rolls contact the metal.
  • Larger backup rolls support the work rolls.
  • Used for rolling thin sheets and strips.

D. Cluster Rolling Mill

  • Contains multiple backup rolls supporting small work rolls.
  • Used for very thin sheets and strips.
  • Provides high stiffness against roll deflection.

E. Tandem Rolling Mill

  • Consists of several rolling stands arranged in sequence.
  • Material passes continuously from one stand to another.
  • Used for high production rates.

3.4 Important Rolling Terminology

1. Draft

Reduction in thickness during one rolling pass.

d = h0 − hf

Where: h0 = Initial thickness; hf = Final thickness; d = Draft.

2. Percentage Reduction

%R = h0 − hfh0 × 100

3. Neutral Point

  • The point where the speed of the strip equals the peripheral speed of the roll.
  • Before the neutral point, the roll surface moves faster than the strip.
  • After the neutral point, the strip moves faster than the roll surface.

4. Angle of Bite

  • The angle formed between the roll radius and the line joining the roll centre to the point of initial contact.
  • It determines the ability of rolls to grip the workpiece.

5. Roll Gap

  • The distance between the working surfaces of the two rolls.

PART B: EXTRUSION

3.6 Introduction to Extrusion

  • Extrusion is a metal forming process in which a billet is forced through a die opening by applying compressive force.
  • The extruded material takes the cross-sectional shape of the die opening.
  • It is commonly performed using hydraulic or mechanical presses.

Basic Working Principle

  1. A metal billet is prepared.
  2. The billet is placed inside a container.
  3. A ram or punch applies compressive force.
  4. The metal flows through the die opening.
  5. A continuous product with the required cross-section emerges.
  6. The product is cut to the required length.

Applications

  • Aluminium window frames.
  • Heat exchanger sections.
  • Pipes.
  • Tubes.
  • Rods.
  • Structural sections.
  • Marine equipment profiles.

3.7 Types of Extrusion

A. Direct Extrusion (Forward Extrusion)

  • The billet is placed inside a container.
  • The ram pushes the billet towards the die.
  • Metal flows through the die in the same direction as ram movement.
  • There is relative sliding between the billet and container wall.

Advantages: Simple arrangement; widely used; suitable for many cross-sectional shapes.

Disadvantages: Higher friction between billet and container; higher force requirement; greater heat generation due to friction.

B. Indirect Extrusion (Backward Extrusion)

  • The die is mounted on a hollow ram.
  • The ram moves towards the billet.
  • Metal flows through the die in the opposite direction to ram movement.
  • The billet remains relatively stationary against the container wall.

Advantages: Lower friction; lower extrusion force compared with direct extrusion under similar conditions; less frictional heat generation.

Disadvantages: Hollow ram design is more complex; product size and shape may be limited by equipment.

C. Hot Extrusion

  • The billet is heated before extrusion.
  • Lower forces are required because the material has greater ductility and lower deformation resistance.
  • Suitable for large reductions and many difficult-to-form materials.

D. Cold Extrusion

  • Extrusion is carried out at room temperature or near room temperature.
  • It provides better surface finish and dimensional accuracy.
  • It can increase strength due to strain hardening.

Applications: Aluminium cans, small automotive parts, fasteners, precision components.

E. Impact Extrusion

  • A punch strikes a metal slug at high speed.
  • The material flows around or through the punch.
  • Commonly used for thin-walled hollow products.

Applications: Toothpaste tubes, aluminium containers, collapsible metal tubes.

3.8 Extrusion Ratio

Extrusion ratio is the ratio of initial billet cross-sectional area to final extruded product cross-sectional area.

R = A0Af

Where: A0 = Initial billet area; Af = Final extruded area; R = Extrusion ratio.

Remember: Higher extrusion ratio means greater reduction in cross-sectional area. Higher extrusion ratio generally requires greater deformation effort.

4.1 Introduction to Sheet Metal Working

  • Sheet metal working is a manufacturing process in which thin metal sheets are cut, bent, drawn or formed into desired shapes.
  • The operations are generally performed using punches, dies, presses and specialised machines.
  • Sheet metal thickness is generally small compared with its length and width.
  • The process is widely used in automotive, aerospace, construction and marine industries.

Common Materials Used: Mild steel, stainless steel, aluminium, copper, brass, galvanized steel.

Applications in Marine Engineering: Ventilation ducts, electrical enclosures, control panel housings, equipment covers, fuel and oil tank components, ship accommodation panels, protective guards.

4.2 Classification of Sheet Metal Operations

Sheet metal operations can be broadly divided into three categories:

  1. Cutting operations.
  2. Bending operations.
  3. Drawing and forming operations.

4.3 Cutting Operations

Cutting operations separate a portion of sheet metal using a punch and die or other cutting equipment.

A. Blanking

  • Blanking is a cutting operation in which a flat piece of metal is cut from a sheet.
  • The cut-out portion is the required product.
  • The remaining sheet is scrap or skeleton.
Important: In blanking, the removed piece is the useful component.

Applications: Washers, metal discs, flat blanks for further forming, small components.

B. Punching (Piercing)

  • Punching is a cutting operation used to produce holes in a metal sheet.
  • The removed material is called a slug.
  • The sheet containing the hole is the useful component.
Important: In punching, the removed material is generally scrap.

Applications: Bolt holes, ventilation openings, mounting holes, perforated sheets.

Blanking vs Punching

BlankingPunching
Produces a required blankProduces a hole
Removed piece is usefulRemaining sheet is useful
Remaining sheet is generally scrapRemoved slug is generally scrap
Die size determines blank sizePunch size determines hole size
This distinction is extremely important.

C. Notching

  • A portion of material is removed from the edge of a sheet.
  • Used to create slots or cut-outs along the edge.

D. Slitting

  • A sheet is cut into narrower strips.
  • Circular rotary cutters are commonly used.

E. Trimming

  • Excess material is removed from the edge of a previously formed component.
  • Commonly used after deep drawing and forging.

F. Shearing

  • A cutting operation in which sheet metal is separated by applying opposing forces through blades.
  • The material is cut without necessarily producing chips.

Applications: Cutting large metal sheets, preparing sheet blanks, cutting strips to required dimensions.

G. Lancing

  • A portion of sheet metal is partially cut and bent without completely separating it.
  • The material remains attached to the sheet.

H. Nibbling

  • A series of overlapping punches are used to produce a required profile.
  • Useful for cutting irregular shapes.

4.4 Bending Operations

  • Bending is a sheet metal forming process in which the metal is deformed around a straight axis.
  • The sheet undergoes tensile stress on the outer surface and compressive stress on the inner surface.
  • The metal thickness may change slightly depending on the bending conditions.

Important Terms in Bending

  1. Bend Radius: Radius of the curved portion of the bent sheet.
  2. Neutral Axis: The layer within the bent sheet that experiences approximately zero longitudinal strain. The outer layers are stretched. The inner layers are compressed.
  3. Springback: The elastic recovery of sheet metal after the bending force is removed. It causes the final bend angle to differ from the angle imposed by the tooling.
  4. Bend Allowance: The length of the neutral-axis arc in the bend region. It is used to calculate the flat sheet length required before bending.

Types of Bending

  • V-Bending: Sheet metal is bent using a V-shaped punch and die. Commonly used for producing angular bends.
  • U-Bending: Sheet metal is formed into a U-shaped profile. Used for channels and brackets.
  • Edge Bending: The edge of a sheet is bent using a suitable die arrangement.
  • Roll Bending: Sheet metal passes through rollers to produce curved or cylindrical shapes. Used for tanks, ducts and cylindrical shells.

Springback

  • After the load is removed, elastic deformation recovers.
  • The component partially returns towards its original shape.
  • This is known as springback.

Springback is influenced by: Material properties; sheet thickness; bend radius; bend angle; yield strength and elastic modulus.

Prevention or compensation: Overbending; bottoming; coining; proper selection of punch and die geometry.

4.5 Drawing Operations

A. Deep Drawing

  • Deep drawing is a sheet metal forming operation in which a flat blank is converted into a hollow component using a punch and die.
  • The punch pushes the sheet into the die cavity.
  • The blank holder controls the flow of sheet metal.
  • The material flows from the flange region into the die cavity.

Applications: Metal cups, fuel tanks, cylindrical containers, kitchen utensils, automotive components.

Important Components:

  1. Punch: Pushes the sheet into the die.
  2. Die: Provides the required cavity.
  3. Blank holder: Prevents uncontrolled wrinkling of the flange.
  4. Blank: Initial flat sheet metal.

Defects in Deep Drawing

  1. Wrinkling: Folds develop in the flange or wall. Usually caused by insufficient blank-holder force.
  2. Tearing: The sheet develops cracks or fractures. May occur due to excessive drawing force or insufficient material ductility.
  3. Earing: Uneven height develops around the rim of a drawn cup. Caused mainly by planar anisotropy in the sheet metal.
  4. Surface scratches: Caused by poor lubrication or rough tool surfaces.

B. Stretch Forming

  • Sheet metal is stretched under tensile force and formed over a die.
  • The material is held at its edges.
  • It is useful for producing large curved sheet metal components.

C. Roll Forming

  • A continuous metal strip passes through a series of rotating rollers.
  • Each set of rolls gradually changes the cross-sectional shape.
  • It is used for producing long components with constant cross-sections.

Applications: Roofing sheets, channels, structural sections, metal frames.

D. Spinning

  • A circular sheet metal blank rotates on a mandrel.
  • A forming tool presses the metal against the mandrel.
  • It produces axisymmetric hollow components.

Applications: Conical vessels, cylindrical containers, reflectors, domed components.

4.6 Clearance in Sheet Metal Cutting

  • Clearance is the gap between the punch and die cutting edges.
  • Proper clearance is essential for good cutting quality.
  • Too little clearance increases cutting force and tool wear.
  • Too much clearance produces excessive burrs and poor cut quality.

Stages of Sheet Metal Shearing

  1. Rollover: The sheet edge deforms near the punch entry.
  2. Burnishing: A smooth region develops due to plastic deformation.
  3. Fracture: Cracks develop and propagate.
  4. Burr: A sharp projection forms at the exit edge.
Important: Burr formation is a normal characteristic of many shearing operations, but excessive burrs indicate poor cutting conditions or tooling.

5.1 Introduction to Powder Metallurgy

  • Powder metallurgy is a manufacturing process in which metal powders are converted into solid components by compacting and sintering.
  • It is particularly useful for producing small, complex components with controlled composition and porosity.
  • The process reduces material wastage compared with many conventional machining operations.

Basic Steps

  1. Powder production.
  2. Powder mixing and blending.
  3. Compaction.
  4. Sintering.
  5. Secondary operations, if required.

5.2 Powder Production Methods

A. Atomization

  • Molten metal is broken into tiny droplets using high-pressure gas or water jets.
  • The droplets cool and solidify into powder particles.
  • One of the most widely used industrial powder production methods.

Types:

  • Gas atomization: Uses high-pressure gas to break molten metal into droplets. Often produces more spherical powders. Commonly used for applications requiring good flowability.
  • Water atomization: Uses high-pressure water jets. Often produces irregularly shaped particles. Commonly used for iron and steel powders.

B. Mechanical Comminution

  • Metal is broken down into smaller particles by mechanical methods.
  • Ball milling is a common example.
  • It can produce irregular-shaped powders.

C. Electrolysis

  • Metal powder is deposited through an electrolytic process.
  • The deposited material is removed, washed and processed into powder.
  • Used for certain metals such as copper and iron.

D. Chemical Reduction

  • Metal oxides are chemically reduced to obtain metallic powders.
  • Hydrogen and carbon monoxide may be used as reducing agents depending on the metal and process.

5.3 Mixing and Blending

  • Different powders are mixed to obtain a uniform composition.
  • Lubricants may be added to reduce friction between the powder and die walls.
  • Alloying elements may be added to obtain required mechanical properties.

Objectives: Uniform composition; consistent material properties; improved powder flow; reduced friction during compaction.

5.4 Compaction

  • Metal powder is placed inside a die cavity.
  • A punch applies pressure to compress the powder.
  • The powder particles rearrange and deform.
  • A compacted component is produced, commonly called a green compact.

Green Compact

  • The compacted but unsintered component is called a green compact.
  • It has enough strength for careful handling.
  • It has not yet developed its final bonding and strength through sintering.

Important Compaction Factors: Applied pressure; powder particle size; particle shape; powder composition; lubricant content; die design; powder distribution.

5.5 Sintering

  • Sintering is the process of heating the green compact to a temperature below the melting point of the main metal constituent.
  • The heating causes bonding between neighbouring powder particles.
  • Atomic diffusion occurs.
  • Particle contacts develop into bonded regions called necks.
  • Strength and density generally increase.
Important: Sintering is normally performed below the melting point of the main metal constituent. Some specialised processes may involve a liquid phase.

What Happens During Sintering?

  1. The compact is heated in a controlled atmosphere.
  2. Powder particles develop stronger bonds.
  3. Diffusion occurs between neighbouring particles.
  4. Pores may reduce in size.
  5. Strength and hardness generally increase.
  6. The component is cooled under controlled conditions.

Sintering Atmospheres

  • Vacuum.
  • Hydrogen.
  • Nitrogen-based controlled atmospheres.
  • Other protective or reducing atmospheres, depending on material requirements.

Purpose of controlled atmosphere: Prevent oxidation; protect the metal surface; support proper sintering; improve the final component quality.

5.6 Secondary Operations

After sintering, additional operations may be performed.

A. Sizing

  • The sintered component is pressed in a sizing die.
  • It improves dimensional accuracy.

B. Coining

  • Additional pressure is applied to improve shape and surface quality.
  • It can increase density in selected regions.

C. Machining

  • Used to produce accurate holes, threads and complex features.

D. Impregnation

  • Oil or another suitable substance is introduced into the interconnected pores.
  • Commonly used for self-lubricating bearings.

E. Infiltration

  • A lower-melting-point metal is introduced into the pores of a porous sintered component.
  • It increases density and may improve mechanical properties.
Difference: Impregnation commonly uses oil or another fluid. Infiltration uses a molten metal.

5.7 Types of Powder Metallurgy Products

A. Porous Bearings

  • Manufactured from porous metal powders.
  • Oil is impregnated into interconnected pores.
  • During operation, oil is released gradually.
  • They can provide self-lubricating characteristics.

Applications: Electric motors, fans, small rotating machinery, certain pumps and auxiliary equipment.

B. Cemented Carbides

  • Manufactured by powder metallurgy techniques.
  • Commonly contain tungsten carbide particles bonded using cobalt.
  • Very hard and wear-resistant.
  • Used for cutting tools and wear-resistant components.

C. Powder-Metallurgy Gears

  • Suitable for mass production of small and medium-sized gears.
  • Can produce complex shapes with relatively little machining.
  • Used in automotive and machinery applications.

5.8 Advantages of Powder Metallurgy

  • High material utilisation.
  • Low material wastage.
  • Good dimensional consistency.
  • Suitable for mass production.
  • Complex shapes can be manufactured.
  • Controlled porosity is possible.
  • Different metal powders can be combined to produce specialised materials.
  • Useful for materials that are difficult to manufacture through conventional melting and casting.

5.9 Disadvantages of Powder Metallurgy

  • Metal powder can be expensive.
  • Initial die and tooling costs can be high.
  • Component size is limited by available presses and equipment.
  • Some components may have lower ductility than wrought products.
  • Residual porosity may reduce strength and fatigue resistance.
  • Powder handling requires care because fine metal powders can present dust and fire hazards.

5.10 Applications of Powder Metallurgy

  • Self-lubricating bearings.
  • Small gears.
  • Cutting tool inserts.
  • Electrical contacts.
  • Filters.
  • Friction materials.
  • Porous components.
  • Wear-resistant parts.
Marine engineering connection: Powder metallurgy is useful for specialised bearings, filtration elements, wear-resistant components and certain machinery parts.

9. Final Revision: Most Important Concepts

Casting

  • Molten metal is poured into a mould.
  • Pattern creates the mould cavity.
  • Core produces internal cavities.
  • Riser compensates for solidification shrinkage.
  • Sprue is vertical; runner is horizontal.
  • Investment casting is also called the lost wax process.
  • Centrifugal casting is commonly used for pipes and cylinder liners.

Forging

  • Metal is shaped using compressive force.
  • Hot forging is above recrystallization temperature.
  • Cold forging improves strength through strain hardening.
  • Open-die forging is suitable for large components.
  • Closed-die forging produces more complex shapes.
  • Upsetting increases cross-sectional area and reduces length.
  • Forging develops directional grain flow.

Rolling

  • Metal passes between rotating rolls.
  • Hot rolling is suitable for large reductions.
  • Cold rolling provides better surface finish and accuracy.
  • Four-high mills use backup rolls.
  • Alligatoring is a characteristic rolling defect.

Extrusion

  • Metal is forced through a die.
  • Direct extrusion has metal flow in the direction of ram movement.
  • Indirect extrusion has metal flow opposite to ram movement.
  • Extrusion produces constant cross-sectional profiles.
  • Extrusion ratio = Initial area / Final area.

Sheet Metal Operations

  • Blanking produces a useful cut-out.
  • Punching produces a hole.
  • Springback is elastic recovery after bending.
  • Deep drawing produces hollow components.
  • Blank holder prevents excessive wrinkling.
  • Earing is associated with planar anisotropy.

Powder Metallurgy

  • Powder production → Mixing → Compaction → Sintering.
  • Green compact is the unsintered compacted component.
  • Sintering develops bonding between powder particles.
  • Sintering is generally below the melting point of the main metal.
  • Impregnation introduces oil or another fluid into pores.
  • Infiltration introduces molten metal into pores.
  • Porous self-lubricating bearings are an important application.
