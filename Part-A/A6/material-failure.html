Subject: Engineering Materials & Metallurgy Topic: Material Failures

Material failure is one of the most important topics in marine engineering because ship machinery, pipelines, propellers, boilers, pumps, shafts and structural components operate under demanding conditions. Understanding material failures helps marine engineers identify why components fail, how failures can be prevented, and how to maintain machinery safely and reliably.

1.1 What is Corrosion?

  • Corrosion is the gradual deterioration of a metal due to chemical or electrochemical reactions with its surrounding environment.
  • During corrosion, metals generally tend to return to a more stable chemical state, such as oxides, hydroxides or sulphides.
  • Corrosion reduces the thickness, strength and reliability of metal components.
  • It can eventually cause leakage, cracking or complete structural failure.

Example

When iron is exposed to moisture and oxygen, it forms rust.

4Fe + 3O₂ + 6H₂O → 4Fe(OH)₃

Rust is mainly composed of hydrated iron oxides.

1.2 Electrochemical Theory of Corrosion

Electrochemical corrosion occurs when small electrochemical cells form on the metal surface in the presence of an electrolyte. An electrolyte is a liquid or solution that allows the movement of ions.

Examples: Seawater, freshwater containing dissolved salts, moist soil, acidic solutions.

Four elements of an electrochemical corrosion cell

  • Anode: Region where oxidation occurs and metal dissolves.
  • Cathode: Region where reduction reaction occurs.
  • Electrolyte: Medium that allows ionic movement.
  • Electrical connection: Metallic path through which electrons flow.

Anodic reaction

At the anode, metal atoms lose electrons. For iron:

Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻

Metal is gradually removed from the surface. Therefore, corrosion takes place at the anode.

Cathodic reaction

In neutral or alkaline water containing oxygen, the common cathodic reaction is:

O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻

The cathode is protected from metal dissolution in this reaction.

Remember: Anode = Oxidation = Corrosion. Cathode = Reduction = Protected region (in a galvanic cell).

1.3 Types of Corrosion

A. Uniform Corrosion

  • Corrosion occurs relatively uniformly over the exposed metal surface.
  • Metal thickness gradually decreases.
  • It is one of the easiest forms of corrosion to identify and monitor.
  • It can be controlled by coatings, corrosion inhibitors and cathodic protection.

Marine applications

  • Ship hull plates.
  • Steel tanks.
  • Exposed steel structures.

B. Pitting Corrosion

  • Pitting corrosion is a localized form of corrosion that produces small holes or cavities on a metal surface.
  • It may penetrate deeply into the material even when the surrounding surface appears relatively unaffected.
  • It commonly occurs in chloride-containing environments.
  • Stainless steel is particularly vulnerable when its protective passive film breaks down.

Causes

  • Chloride ions.
  • Deposits on metal surfaces.
  • Stagnant water.
  • Damage to protective oxide films.

Marine applications

  • Seawater pipelines.
  • Heat exchangers.
  • Stainless steel components.
  • Condensers.

C. Crevice Corrosion

  • Occurs in narrow gaps or confined spaces where stagnant liquid accumulates.
  • Oxygen concentration inside the crevice becomes lower than outside.
  • This creates a differential aeration cell.
  • The oxygen-deficient region generally becomes anodic and corrodes.

Common locations

  • Under bolts and washers.
  • Between overlapping plates.
  • Under gaskets.
  • Between flanges.
  • Under deposits.

Prevention

  • Avoid unnecessary crevices.
  • Use suitable sealing arrangements.
  • Maintain clean surfaces.
  • Select corrosion-resistant materials.

D. Intergranular Corrosion

  • Occurs preferentially along the grain boundaries of a metal.
  • It is commonly associated with certain stainless steels subjected to improper temperature exposure.
  • In susceptible stainless steel, chromium carbides may precipitate along grain boundaries.
  • This reduces chromium concentration near the grain boundaries.
  • The depleted regions become less resistant to corrosion.
Important term: Sensitization – the process in which chromium carbides precipitate at stainless steel grain boundaries, creating chromium-depleted zones. It can make stainless steel susceptible to intergranular corrosion.

Prevention

  • Use low-carbon stainless steel grades such as 304L and 316L where appropriate.
  • Use stabilized grades containing titanium or niobium.
  • Apply suitable solution heat treatment where applicable.

E. Galvanic Corrosion

This is particularly important in marine engineering and is explained in detail below.

F. Erosion Corrosion

  • Occurs when corrosion and mechanical erosion act together.
  • High-velocity fluids remove protective oxide films from metal surfaces.
  • Fresh metal is exposed to the corrosive environment.
  • Repeated removal of protective films accelerates material loss.

Examples

Pump impellers, pipe bends, valves, seawater circulation systems.

G. Stress Corrosion Cracking (SCC)

  • Occurs due to the combined action of tensile stress and a specific corrosive environment.
  • It produces cracks that can propagate through the material.
  • A component may fail suddenly even when the applied stress is below its normal yield strength.
  • Detailed explanation is provided in Section 4.

1.4 Galvanic Corrosion

Definition: Galvanic corrosion is an electrochemical corrosion process that occurs when two dissimilar metals are electrically connected in the presence of an electrolyte. The less noble (more active) metal becomes the anode and corrodes faster, while the more noble metal becomes the cathode and is protected.

How Galvanic Corrosion Occurs

Consider a steel plate connected to a copper plate in seawater:

  1. Steel and copper have different electrode potentials.
  2. They are electrically connected.
  3. Seawater acts as an electrolyte.
  4. Steel becomes the anode.
  5. Copper becomes the cathode.
  6. Steel loses electrons and dissolves.
  7. Corrosion of steel is accelerated.

Galvanic Series

The galvanic series arranges metals and alloys according to their corrosion potential in a particular environment, such as seawater. A simplified illustrative order from more active to more noble is:

More Active (Anodic)More Noble (Cathodic)
MagnesiumCopper alloys
ZincNickel alloys
Aluminium alloysTitanium
Carbon steelGraphite
Cast iron—
Important: The exact order depends on the environment, alloy composition and surface condition. The galvanic series is more useful than assuming that every metal always follows a fixed universal order.

Factors Affecting Galvanic Corrosion

  1. Difference in electrode potential: A larger potential difference generally increases the driving force for galvanic corrosion.
  2. Area ratio: A small anode connected to a large cathode is particularly dangerous. A small anodic area has to supply corrosion current to a large cathodic area. This causes rapid corrosion of the anode.
  3. Electrolyte conductivity: Highly conductive electrolytes allow greater galvanic current. Seawater is an effective electrolyte because of dissolved salts.
  4. Temperature: Higher temperature often increases corrosion reaction rates.
  5. Distance between metals: Galvanic effects are generally strongest near the metallic connection, although electrolyte conditions affect their extent.

Examples in Ships

  • Steel hull connected to copper-alloy components.
  • Aluminium structures connected to stainless steel fasteners.
  • Different metals used in seawater pipelines.
  • Ship propellers and associated metallic fittings.

Prevention of Galvanic Corrosion

  1. Electrical insulation: Separate dissimilar metals using insulating gaskets, sleeves and washers. Prevent direct electrical contact.
  2. Sacrificial anode protection: Attach a more active metal such as zinc or aluminium alloy. The sacrificial metal corrodes instead of the protected structure.
  3. Protective coatings: Apply suitable paint or protective coatings. Coating systems should be properly maintained. Avoid coating only the anodic metal while leaving the cathodic metal exposed, as this can concentrate corrosion at coating defects.
  4. Proper material selection: Select metals with similar corrosion potentials wherever possible.
  5. Correct area ratio: Avoid small anodic components connected to large cathodic surfaces.

Sacrificial Anode Cathodic Protection

  • A sacrificial anode is a more reactive metal attached to the structure that needs protection.
  • The sacrificial anode becomes the anode and corrodes.
  • The protected structure becomes the cathode.
  • The sacrificial anode gradually gets consumed and must be inspected and replaced.

Common sacrificial anode materials

  • Zinc.
  • Aluminium alloys.
  • Magnesium alloys.

Marine applications

  • Ship hulls.
  • Seawater tanks.
  • Heat exchangers.
  • Offshore structures.
  • Marine pipelines.
Galvanic corrosionSacrificial anode protection

Less noble metal corrodesSacrificial anode corrodes

1.5 Methods of Corrosion Prevention

  1. Painting and protective coatings: Create a barrier between metal and the environment. Commonly used on ship hulls and machinery.
  2. Cathodic protection: Makes the protected structure act as a cathode. Can be achieved using sacrificial anodes or impressed current systems.
  3. Corrosion inhibitors: Chemicals added to a fluid to reduce corrosion reactions. Used in cooling water and boiler water treatment.
  4. Material selection: Select corrosion-resistant materials according to operating conditions.
  5. Proper design: Avoid stagnant water pockets, minimize crevices, provide proper drainage.
  6. Regular maintenance: Inspect surfaces, remove corrosion products, repair damaged coatings, replace consumed sacrificial anodes.

2.1 What is Cavitation?

  • Cavitation is the formation of vapour bubbles in a liquid when the local pressure falls below the liquid's vapour pressure.
  • These bubbles are carried into regions of higher pressure.
  • They collapse suddenly, producing intense localized pressure pulses and micro-jets.
  • Repeated bubble collapse near a solid surface causes material damage known as cavitation erosion.
Important: Cavitation is not simply the presence of air bubbles. It involves the formation and collapse of vapour cavities.

2.2 Principle of Cavitation

Cavitation is mainly related to pressure and temperature. Every liquid has a vapour pressure at a particular temperature.

  • When local pressure falls below the vapour pressure, the liquid starts forming vapour cavities.
  • When pressure increases again, the vapour bubbles collapse.

Step-by-Step Cavitation Process

  1. Liquid flows through a pump or other hydraulic equipment.
  2. At certain locations, the local pressure decreases.
  3. When pressure drops below the liquid's vapour pressure, vapour bubbles form.
  4. These bubbles travel with the liquid.
  5. They enter a region of higher pressure.
  6. The bubbles collapse rapidly.
  7. Collapse produces high-pressure shock waves and micro-jets.
  8. Repeated impacts damage the metal surface.
  9. Small pits and rough surfaces develop.
  10. Continuous damage results in material loss.

2.3 Causes of Cavitation

  1. Low suction pressure: Insufficient pressure at the pump inlet promotes vapour formation.
  2. High liquid temperature: Higher temperature increases vapour pressure. This makes cavitation more likely.
  3. Excessive pump speed: Higher speed can produce lower local pressures depending on operating conditions.
  4. Improper pump design: Poor hydraulic design can create local low-pressure zones.
  5. Blocked suction strainer: Restricts liquid flow. Increases pressure loss on the suction side.
  6. Insufficient Net Positive Suction Head (NPSH): The available suction head is insufficient compared with the required head.
  7. Excessive suction pipe losses: Long pipes, small diameters, sharp bends and unnecessary fittings increase pressure losses.

2.4 Types of Cavitation

A. Travelling Cavitation

  • Vapour bubbles form in the flowing liquid.
  • They travel through the system.
  • They collapse when they enter a higher-pressure region.

B. Attached Cavitation

  • Vapour cavities remain attached to a surface.
  • Commonly observed on propeller blades and hydraulic components.

C. Vortex Cavitation

  • Occurs in low-pressure vortex cores.
  • Can develop near pump impellers and propellers.

D. Sheet Cavitation

  • A continuous sheet-like vapour cavity forms over a surface.
  • Common in marine propellers and hydraulic machinery.

2.5 Effects of Cavitation

  1. Pitting: Small holes form on the metal surface due to repeated bubble collapse.
  2. Surface erosion: Material is gradually removed.
  3. Noise: Cavitation produces crackling or rattling sounds.
  4. Vibration: Irregular pressure fluctuations cause vibration.
  5. Reduced efficiency: Vapour cavities interfere with normal liquid flow.
  6. Damage to impellers: Pump blades become rough and lose material.
  7. Reduced component life: Continuous cavitation can cause serious mechanical damage.

2.6 Cavitation in Marine Engineering

Common locations: Centrifugal pump impellers, ship propellers, seawater circulation pumps, cooling water pumps, hydraulic turbines, rudder and other hydrodynamic surfaces.

Example: Ship Propeller

  • Propeller blades rotate at high speed.
  • Pressure on certain regions of the blade decreases.
  • Vapour bubbles form.
  • These bubbles collapse as they move into higher-pressure regions.
  • Repeated collapse damages the propeller surface.

2.7 Net Positive Suction Head (NPSH)

NPSH is an important concept in pump operation and cavitation prevention.

Definition: Net Positive Suction Head is the amount by which the absolute pressure head at the pump suction exceeds the liquid's vapour pressure head.

A. NPSH Available (NPSHa)

  • It represents the actual suction head available in the pumping system.
  • It depends on suction tank pressure, liquid level, temperature and suction pipe losses.

B. NPSH Required (NPSHr)

  • It is the minimum suction head required by the pump to operate under specified conditions without unacceptable cavitation.
  • It is generally provided by the pump manufacturer.
Important condition: NPSHa > NPSHr
NPSHa should exceed NPSHr. An adequate safety margin should be maintained according to the pump manufacturer's recommendations and applicable design requirements.

2.8 Prevention of Cavitation

  1. Maintain adequate suction pressure.
  2. Ensure proper liquid level in the suction tank.
  3. Avoid excessive pump speed.
  4. Use correctly sized suction pipes.
  5. Clean suction strainers regularly.
  6. Avoid unnecessary bends and restrictions.
  7. Maintain suitable operating temperature.
  8. Select a pump with appropriate NPSH characteristics.
  9. Avoid operating pumps far outside their recommended operating range.
  10. Maintain proper pump alignment and operating conditions.
Remember: Cavitation = Low local pressure → Vapour bubbles → Bubble collapse → Shock waves → Pitting and erosion.

3.1 What is Fatigue Failure?

  • Fatigue failure is the progressive failure of a material due to repeated or fluctuating stresses.
  • It can occur even when the maximum applied stress is below the material's yield strength.
  • Small cracks initiate at stress concentration points.
  • These cracks gradually propagate with repeated loading.
  • Eventually, the remaining material becomes insufficient to carry the load and sudden fracture occurs.
Important: Fatigue is one of the most dangerous types of mechanical failure because it can occur without significant visible deformation before final fracture.

3.2 Causes of Fatigue Failure

  1. Repeated loading and unloading.
  2. Fluctuating tensile and compressive stresses.
  3. Rotating shafts.
  4. Excessive vibration.
  5. Poor surface finish.
  6. Sharp corners and notches.
  7. Manufacturing defects.
  8. Improper alignment.
  9. Corrosion-assisted crack initiation.
  10. Excessive stress concentration.

3.3 Types of Fatigue Loading

A. Completely Reversed Loading

  • Stress changes from maximum tension to maximum compression.
  • Tensile and compressive stresses have equal magnitude.
  • Mean stress is zero.
σm = 0
σa = σmax

B. Repeated or Pulsating Loading

  • Stress varies between zero and a maximum value of the same sign.
  • For example, stress varies between zero and maximum tension.

C. Fluctuating Loading

  • Stress varies continuously between minimum and maximum values.
  • The maximum and minimum stresses may be different.

3.4 Important Fatigue Terms

  1. Maximum Stress (σmax): Highest stress experienced during a loading cycle.
  2. Minimum Stress (σmin): Lowest stress experienced during a loading cycle.
  3. Mean Stress (σm): Average stress during one complete cycle.
    σm = (σmax + σmin) / 2
  4. Stress Amplitude (σa): Half the difference between maximum and minimum stresses.
    σa = (σmax − σmin) / 2
  5. Stress Range:
    Δσ = σmax − σmin
  6. Stress Ratio (R):
    R = σmin / σmax

3.5 Stages of Fatigue Failure

Fatigue failure generally occurs in three stages.

Stage 1: Crack Initiation

  • Small cracks develop at the surface or internal defects.
  • Cracks commonly start at stress concentration points.
  • Surface scratches, notches, inclusions and machining marks can initiate cracks.

Stage 2: Crack Propagation

  • Cracks gradually grow with repeated loading.
  • Each stress cycle contributes to crack growth.
  • The crack may produce characteristic fatigue markings called beach marks or progression marks.
  • Microscopic fatigue striations may also be present.

Stage 3: Final Fracture

  • The remaining uncracked cross-section becomes too small to withstand the applied load.
  • Sudden fracture occurs.
  • This final region often shows a rougher appearance compared with the fatigue propagation region.

3.6 S-N Curve (Wöhler Curve)

The S-N curve represents the relationship between cyclic stress amplitude (S) and the number of cycles to failure (N).

  • Vertical axis: Stress amplitude.
  • Horizontal axis: Number of cycles to failure, usually on a logarithmic scale.
  • Higher stress amplitude generally results in fewer cycles before failure.
  • Lower stress amplitude generally allows more cycles before failure.

Important Terms

  1. Fatigue Life: Number of loading cycles a component can withstand before fatigue failure.
  2. Endurance Limit: A stress level below which some materials, particularly certain steels, can withstand a very large number of cycles without fatigue failure under specified conditions. Not all materials have a clearly defined endurance limit. Aluminium alloys generally do not show a distinct horizontal endurance limit.
  3. Fatigue Strength: The stress amplitude a material can withstand for a specified number of cycles.
  4. Low-Cycle Fatigue: Failure occurs after a relatively small number of cycles. Usually associated with higher cyclic strain and often plastic deformation.
  5. High-Cycle Fatigue: Failure occurs after a large number of cycles. Usually associated with predominantly elastic deformation.

3.7 Factors Affecting Fatigue Strength

  1. Surface finish: Rough surfaces reduce fatigue strength. Scratches and grooves act as stress concentration points.
  2. Stress concentration: Holes, sharp corners and notches increase local stress.
  3. Material properties: Strength, ductility, microstructure and inclusions affect fatigue performance.
  4. Size of component: Larger components may have a greater probability of containing critical defects.
  5. Temperature: Elevated temperatures can influence fatigue strength and crack growth.
  6. Corrosion: Corrosive environments accelerate fatigue damage.
  7. Residual stresses: Tensile residual stresses can reduce fatigue life. Compressive surface residual stresses can improve fatigue resistance.

3.8 Fatigue Failure in Marine Engineering

Common examples: Crankshafts, connecting rods, propeller shafts, engine components, turbine shafts, gear teeth, welded ship structures, engine mounting arrangements.

Example: Crankshaft

  • A crankshaft experiences repeated bending and torsional loading.
  • Every engine cycle contributes to cyclic stresses.
  • Small cracks may initiate near fillets and oil holes.
  • Repeated operation increases crack length.
  • Eventually, the crankshaft may fracture.

3.9 Prevention of Fatigue Failure

  1. Avoid sharp corners and sudden changes in cross-section.
  2. Provide proper fillet radii.
  3. Maintain good surface finish.
  4. Avoid excessive vibration.
  5. Ensure proper shaft alignment.
  6. Avoid overloading.
  7. Use suitable materials.
  8. Apply shot peening where appropriate.
  9. Inspect components periodically.
  10. Detect cracks before they become critical.

Shot Peening

  • A surface treatment in which small spherical particles strike the material surface.
  • Produces beneficial compressive residual stresses.
  • Helps delay fatigue crack initiation.
Remember: Fatigue = Repeated loading → Crack initiation → Crack propagation → Sudden final fracture.

4.1 What is Stress Corrosion Cracking?

  • Stress Corrosion Cracking (SCC) is a type of material failure caused by the combined action of tensile stress and a specific corrosive environment.
  • It produces cracks that may propagate through the material.
  • The applied stress may be below the normal yield strength of the material.
  • Failure may occur suddenly with little visible corrosion or deformation.
Important: SCC requires three essential conditions:
  1. A susceptible material.
  2. A tensile stress.
  3. A specific corrosive environment.
If any one of these conditions is removed, SCC can generally be prevented or significantly reduced.

4.2 Mechanism of SCC

  1. A susceptible metal is exposed to a particular corrosive environment.
  2. Tensile stress acts on the material.
  3. A protective surface film breaks down locally or a crack initiates through an environment-assisted mechanism.
  4. Corrosion and mechanical effects interact at the crack tip.
  5. The crack gradually propagates.
  6. The component eventually fractures.

4.3 Types of Stress Corrosion Cracking

A. Transgranular SCC

  • Cracks propagate through the grains of the material.
  • The crack path passes through the grain interiors.
  • Also called transgranular cracking.

B. Intergranular SCC

  • Cracks propagate along grain boundaries.
  • Grain boundaries become preferential paths for crack growth.
  • It can be associated with sensitized stainless steels in certain environments.

4.4 Common Materials and Environments Associated with SCC

MaterialEnvironment
Austenitic stainless steelChloride-containing solutions
BrassAmmonia-containing environments
Carbon steelConcentrated caustic solutions
High-strength steelsCertain hydrogen-containing or corrosive environments
Aluminium alloysCertain chloride-containing environments

Important examples

  • Stainless steel exposed to hot chloride-containing seawater.
  • Brass components exposed to ammonia.
  • Carbon steel exposed to concentrated caustic environments.

Not every material will crack in every corrosive environment. SCC is strongly dependent on the material-environment combination.

4.5 Factors Affecting SCC

  1. Tensile stress magnitude.
  2. Type of material.
  3. Chemical composition.
  4. Temperature.
  5. Concentration of corrosive substances.
  6. Surface condition.
  7. Residual stresses from welding and manufacturing.
  8. Exposure duration.

4.6 SCC in Marine Engineering

Possible locations: Stainless steel seawater pipelines, heat exchanger components, welded structures, high-strength fasteners, pressure-containing equipment, marine fittings exposed to chloride environments.

Example: Stainless Steel Seawater Pipeline

  • A stainless steel pipeline is exposed to warm seawater.
  • Chloride ions can damage the passive protective film.
  • Tensile stresses may be present due to pressure, welding or thermal expansion.
  • Cracks initiate and propagate.
  • The pipe may eventually leak or fracture.

4.7 Prevention of SCC

  1. Material selection: Select alloys suitable for the operating environment. Use appropriate chloride-resistant materials.
  2. Stress reduction: Avoid excessive operating stress. Reduce residual tensile stresses where practical.
  3. Stress-relieving treatment: Suitable heat treatment may reduce residual stresses for compatible materials.
  4. Environmental control: Reduce harmful chemical concentrations. Control operating temperature where possible. Prevent chemical contamination.
  5. Protective coatings: Use appropriate protective systems where suitable.
  6. Regular inspection: Examine critical areas, welds and stressed components. Use suitable non-destructive testing methods.
  7. Design improvements: Avoid sharp geometrical transitions. Reduce stress concentration.

5.1 What is Wear?

  • Wear is the gradual removal or deformation of material from a surface due to relative motion between two contacting surfaces or interaction with moving particles.
  • It changes the dimensions and surface finish of components.
  • Wear can increase clearance, vibration, leakage and friction.
  • Excessive wear can lead to machinery failure.

Examples

Bearing surfaces, cylinder liners, piston rings, gear teeth, pump seals, valve seats.

5.2 What is Abrasion?

  • Abrasion is a type of wear caused by hard particles or hard surface irregularities cutting, scratching or ploughing a softer surface.
  • It removes material through mechanical action.
  • Abrasive wear commonly occurs when dust, sand or metallic particles enter lubricated machinery.

Example

If sand particles enter a pump, they can scratch and remove material from the impeller and internal surfaces.

5.3 Types of Wear

A. Adhesive Wear

  • Occurs when two surfaces slide against each other under load.
  • Microscopic contact points may form strong adhesive junctions.
  • Material transfers from one surface to another.
  • In severe cases, surfaces may seize.

Causes

  • Poor lubrication.
  • High contact pressure.
  • Excessive temperature.
  • Similar material combinations in certain conditions.

Marine examples

  • Sliding bearings.
  • Piston and cylinder contact.
  • Gear contacts.

Prevention

  • Proper lubrication.
  • Suitable surface finish.
  • Correct material pairing.
  • Maintain operating temperature and load.

B. Abrasive Wear

  • Occurs due to hard particles or asperities scratching and removing material.

There are two main types:

1. Two-body Abrasive Wear

  • A hard rough surface directly scratches a softer surface.
  • Example: A rough rotating surface rubbing against another component.

2. Three-body Abrasive Wear

  • Loose hard particles are present between two moving surfaces.
  • Particles roll, slide or cut into the surfaces.
  • Example: Sand or metallic particles circulating in lubricating oil.

Prevention

  • Use proper filters.
  • Maintain clean lubrication systems.
  • Prevent dust and sand entry.
  • Use wear-resistant materials.

C. Corrosive Wear

  • Occurs when chemical or electrochemical corrosion combines with mechanical wear.
  • Mechanical movement removes protective corrosion films.
  • Fresh material becomes exposed.
  • The cycle repeats, accelerating material loss.

Examples

Pump components handling corrosive liquids, valves, marine pipelines.

D. Erosive Wear

  • Occurs when moving fluids or solid particles strike a material surface.
  • High-velocity particles remove material through repeated impact.
  • It is particularly common at bends and changes in flow direction.

Examples

Pipe bends, pump impellers, slurry pipelines, exhaust gas systems.

E. Surface Fatigue Wear

  • Occurs due to repeated contact stresses between two surfaces.
  • Repeated loading causes subsurface cracks.
  • Small fragments of material break away.
  • This produces pitting or spalling.

Examples

Rolling element bearings, gear teeth, rolling contact surfaces.

F. Fretting Wear

  • Occurs due to very small oscillatory movements between two contacting surfaces.
  • Repeated small movements damage the surface.
  • Oxidized wear debris may accumulate.
  • Fretting corrosion occurs when fretting and oxidation act together.

Examples

Shaft and bearing fits, bolted joints, couplings, press-fitted components.

  1. Contact pressure.
  2. Relative sliding speed.

Example: Cylinder Liner Wear

  • Piston rings move repeatedly inside the cylinder liner.
  • They experience sliding friction.
  • Inadequate lubrication increases metal-to-metal contact.
  • Abrasive particles can scratch the liner.
  • Combustion products can contribute to corrosive wear.
  • Excessive wear increases clearance and can cause increased oil consumption and reduced engine efficiency.

5.7 Prevention of Wear

  1. Provide proper lubrication.
  2. Use suitable lubricant viscosity.
  3. Maintain oil cleanliness.
  4. Install filters and strainers.
  5. Maintain proper alignment.
  6. Avoid excessive loads.
  7. Select suitable surface hardness.
  8. Apply surface treatments such as nitriding or hard chromium plating where appropriate.
  9. Maintain correct operating temperature.
  10. Inspect clearances periodically.

Importance of Lubrication

Lubrication is one of the most effective methods of reducing wear.

Functions of lubricating oil:

  • Reduces friction.
  • Reduces wear.
  • Removes heat.
  • Prevents direct metal-to-metal contact.
  • Carries away contaminants.
  • Provides protection against corrosion.
  • Helps seal clearances in certain machinery.
Remember: Wear = General surface deterioration. Abrasion = Wear due to hard particles or rough surfaces.

Material testing and inspection are essential for ensuring that materials and components meet the required mechanical, chemical and structural properties. They help engineers identify defects before a component fails during operation.

There are two major categories:

  1. Destructive Testing (DT).
  2. Non-Destructive Testing (NDT).

6.1 Destructive Testing (DT)

Definition: Destructive testing involves testing a material or specimen by applying loads or conditions that permanently deform, damage or destroy it. The test specimen generally cannot be used again for its original purpose. It is used to determine mechanical properties and material behaviour.

A. Tensile Test

The tensile test determines how a material behaves when subjected to a gradually increasing tensile load.

Procedure

  1. A standard specimen is prepared.
  2. The specimen is placed in a Universal Testing Machine (UTM).
  3. The specimen is gripped at both ends.
  4. Tensile force is gradually applied.
  5. Elongation is measured.
  6. The load is increased until the specimen fractures.

Properties determined: Yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, percentage elongation, percentage reduction in area, Young's modulus.

Important formulas

Engineering stress:

σ = P / A₀

Where P = applied tensile load, A₀ = original cross-sectional area.

Engineering strain:

ε = ΔL / L₀

Where ΔL = change in length, L₀ = original gauge length.

Young's modulus:

E = Stress / Strain

Within the linear elastic region.

Important: Ultimate tensile strength (UTS) is the maximum engineering stress reached during the tensile test.

B. Compression Test

  • Determines the behaviour of materials under compressive loading.
  • The specimen is compressed between two plates.
  • Deformation and load are recorded.
  • It helps determine compressive strength and deformation behaviour.

Applications

  • Cast iron.
  • Concrete.
  • Refractory materials.
  • Structural components.

C. Hardness Test

Definition: Hardness is the resistance of a material to localized plastic deformation, such as indentation, scratching or penetration.

TestDescription
Brinell Hardness TestA hardened ball or suitable carbide ball is pressed into the material. The diameter of the indentation is measured. Suitable for many metals, including castings and forgings.
Rockwell Hardness TestA specified indenter is pressed into the material. Hardness is determined from the depth of penetration. Common indenters include diamond cones and steel or carbide balls. It is widely used because it is relatively quick.
Vickers Hardness TestUses a diamond-shaped square-based pyramidal indenter. The indentation diagonals are measured. Suitable for a wide range of materials and hardness levels.
Knoop Hardness TestUses an elongated diamond-shaped indenter. Suitable for microhardness testing of thin layers and small regions.

D. Impact Test

The impact test determines the ability of a material to absorb energy during sudden loading.

Common methods

  • Charpy Impact Test: The specimen is supported horizontally at both ends. It contains a notch. A pendulum strikes the specimen opposite the notch. The energy absorbed during fracture is measured.
  • Izod Impact Test: The specimen is held vertically as a cantilever. It contains a notch. A pendulum strikes the specimen. Energy absorbed during fracture is measured.

Important terms: Impact strength, toughness, ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT).

DBTT: Some materials become significantly less ductile and more brittle below a certain temperature range. This behaviour is especially important in certain ferritic and low-alloy steels. It is important when selecting materials for cold environments.

E. Fatigue Test

  • Determines the resistance of a material to repeated or fluctuating loading.
  • The specimen is subjected to cyclic stress.
  • The number of cycles until failure is recorded.
  • Results are used to develop S-N curves.

Applications

  • Crankshafts.
  • Shafts.
  • Gears.
  • Connecting rods.
  • Rotating machinery.

F. Creep Test

Definition: Creep is the slow and permanent deformation of a material under constant stress over a prolonged period, usually at elevated temperature.

Procedure

  1. A specimen is subjected to a constant load.
  2. The specimen is maintained at a specified temperature.
  3. Deformation is measured over time.
  4. Creep behaviour is studied.

Three stages of creep

  1. Primary creep: Creep rate gradually decreases.
  2. Secondary creep: Creep rate becomes approximately constant. This is generally the longest stage.
  3. Tertiary creep: Creep rate increases rapidly. Necking, void formation and damage develop. Final rupture occurs.

Marine applications

  • Boiler components.
  • Steam pipes.
  • Turbine components.
  • High-temperature engine parts.

G. Bend Test

  • Determines the ability of a material to withstand bending deformation.
  • The specimen is bent through a specified angle or around a specified former.
  • It helps evaluate ductility and detect surface cracking.
  • Commonly used for weld and fabrication quality assessment.

H. Torsion Test

  • Determines the behaviour of a material under twisting forces.
  • One end of the specimen is held fixed.
  • Torque is applied to the other end.
  • The angle of twist is measured.
  • It helps determine shear properties and torsional strength.

Marine applications

  • Propeller shafts.
  • Drive shafts.
  • Transmission components.

6.2 Non-Destructive Testing (NDT)

Definition: Non-Destructive Testing is the examination of materials or components to detect defects without causing significant damage or making them unusable.

NDT is extremely important in marine engineering because machinery and ship structures must be inspected without dismantling or destroying critical components unnecessarily.

Main Objectives of NDT

  1. Detect cracks.
  2. Identify internal defects.
  3. Find surface defects.
  4. Check welding quality.
  5. Detect corrosion and material loss.
  6. Monitor structural integrity.
  7. Prevent unexpected failures.

A. Visual Testing (VT)

  • Principle: Inspection is performed by directly observing the surface of a component.
  • Equipment: Naked eye, magnifying glass, inspection mirror, borescope, cameras, measuring gauges.
  • Detects: Surface cracks visible to the eye, corrosion, surface deformation, poor welding appearance, leakage, misalignment.
  • Advantages: Simple, inexpensive, quick, no complex equipment required.
  • Limitations: Cannot reliably detect internal defects. Some surface cracks may be too small to see without additional methods.

B. Dye Penetrant Testing (DPT / LPT)

  • Principle: A liquid penetrant enters surface-breaking defects through capillary action. A developer draws the penetrant back out, making defects visible.

Procedure

  1. Clean the component surface.
  2. Apply the penetrant liquid.
  3. Allow sufficient dwell time.
  4. Remove excess penetrant from the surface.
  5. Apply developer.
  6. Inspect the surface for indications.
  • Detects: Surface cracks, surface porosity, surface-breaking defects, weld surface defects.
  • Advantages: Relatively inexpensive, simple procedure, suitable for many non-porous materials, can detect very fine surface-breaking cracks.
  • Limitations: Detects only defects open to the surface. Requires proper surface cleaning. Not suitable for porous or heavily contaminated surfaces.
Important: Dye penetrant testing does not detect internal cracks that do not reach the surface.

C. Magnetic Particle Testing (MPT / MPI)

  • Principle: The ferromagnetic component is magnetized. A surface or near-surface discontinuity causes magnetic flux leakage. Magnetic particles accumulate at the leakage field. This makes the defect visible.

Procedure

  1. Clean the component.
  2. Magnetize the component.
  3. Apply magnetic particles.
  4. Observe particle accumulation.
  5. Evaluate the indications.
  • Detects: Surface cracks, near-surface cracks, welding defects, fatigue cracks.
  • Advantages: Fast, sensitive to surface and near-surface defects, useful for steel components.
  • Limitations: Only suitable for ferromagnetic materials. Not suitable for aluminium, copper and austenitic stainless steel in their usual non-ferromagnetic condition. Magnetization direction and technique influence defect detection.

Marine applications

  • Crankshafts.
  • Steel shafts.
  • Welded steel structures.
  • Ferromagnetic engine components.

D. Ultrasonic Testing (UT)

  • Principle: High-frequency sound waves are transmitted into a material. The waves travel through the material. When they encounter an interface or defect, some sound energy is reflected. The reflected signals are received and analysed.

Procedure

  1. Place a suitable probe on the component.
  2. Apply couplant to ensure sound transmission.
  3. Transmit ultrasonic waves.
  4. Receive reflected signals.
  5. Analyse signal timing and amplitude.
  6. Determine the location and characteristics of defects.
  • Detects: Internal cracks, voids, inclusions, laminations, weld defects, material thickness.
  • Advantages: Can detect internal defects. Can measure material thickness. Often requires access from only one side. No radiation hazard.
  • Limitations: Requires trained operators. Surface condition and geometry can affect results. Some complex geometries are difficult to inspect. Certain defects may be difficult to detect depending on orientation.

Marine applications

  • Ship hull plates.
  • Pressure vessels.
  • Boiler tubes.
  • Welded joints.
  • Propeller shafts.

E. Radiographic Testing (RT)

  • Principle: X-rays or gamma rays are passed through a component. Different thicknesses and internal defects absorb radiation differently. A detector or radiographic film records the transmitted radiation. Internal defects appear as variations in the image.
  • Radiation sources: X-ray equipment, gamma-ray sources such as Iridium-192 or Cobalt-60, depending on the application.
  • Detects: Internal porosity, slag inclusions, lack of fusion in suitable orientations, internal voids, certain cracks.
  • Advantages: Produces a permanent radiographic record. Useful for weld inspection. Can reveal internal volumetric defects.
  • Limitations: Radiation safety precautions are essential. Requires controlled inspection areas. May require access to both sides of the component. Defect orientation affects detection sensitivity.

Marine applications

  • Pressure vessel welds.
  • Boiler welds.
  • Pipeline welds.
  • Structural fabrication.

F. Eddy Current Testing (ECT)

  • Principle: An alternating current flows through a coil. This produces an alternating magnetic field. When the probe approaches an electrically conductive material, eddy currents are induced in it. Cracks, corrosion or thickness variations disturb the eddy currents. These changes are detected by the instrument.
  • Detects: Surface cracks, near-surface defects, corrosion, wall thickness variations, tube defects.
  • Advantages: Does not require direct electrical contact. Can inspect conductive materials. Useful for heat exchanger tube inspection. Fast for suitable applications.
  • Limitations: Suitable only for electrically conductive materials. Inspection depth is limited. Results are influenced by material properties and geometry.

Marine applications

  • Condenser tubes.
  • Heat exchanger tubes.
  • Conductive metallic components.
  • Surface crack inspection.

G. Leak Testing (LT)

  • Principle: Determines whether gas or liquid escapes from a component or system.
  • Methods: Bubble leak testing, pressure decay testing, vacuum box testing, helium leak testing.
  • Applications: Pipelines, fuel systems, pressure vessels, refrigeration systems, heat exchangers.

6.5 Material Inspection in Marine Engineering

Material inspection is essential throughout the life of a ship. It includes:

  1. Inspection during material procurement.
  2. Inspection during manufacturing.
  3. Inspection during installation.
  4. Periodic inspection during operation.
  5. Inspection during repairs.
  6. Inspection after abnormal operating conditions.

Inspection of Important Marine Components

ComponentCommon inspection methodsMain purpose

Ship hullVisual, UT thickness measurementDetect corrosion and thickness loss

Common Defects Found During Inspection

  1. Cracks: Fractures or separations within a material. May develop due to fatigue, SCC or thermal stresses.
  2. Porosity: Small gas cavities within a material or weld. Commonly associated with welding defects.
  3. Inclusions: Foreign particles trapped within a metal. Can reduce material strength and act as crack initiation sites.
  4. Lack of Fusion: Occurs when deposited weld metal does not properly fuse with the base metal or previous weld pass.
  5. Lack of Penetration: Occurs when the weld does not penetrate through the required joint thickness.
  6. Corrosion Pitting: Localized cavities caused by corrosion.
  7. Laminations: Internal planar separations, commonly found in rolled steel products.
  8. Excessive Wear: Material loss beyond the permissible limit.
  9. Deformation: Permanent change in the original shape or dimensions.

Thickness Measurement

Thickness measurement is especially important for ship hulls, pipelines, tanks and pressure vessels.

  • Ultrasonic thickness gauges are commonly used.
  • A probe sends ultrasonic waves into the material.
  • The reflected signal is used to calculate the material thickness.
  • Repeated measurements help determine corrosion rate and remaining wall thickness.

For approximately uniform corrosion:

Corrosion Rate = Thickness Loss / Time

Example

Original thickness = 12 mm. Current thickness = 10 mm. Time elapsed = 4 years.

Corrosion Rate = (12 − 10) / 4 = 0.5 mm/year

This is a simplified average corrosion rate, assuming comparable measurements and approximately uniform material loss.

Importance of Regular Inspection

  1. Prevents unexpected machinery breakdown.
  2. Improves ship safety.
  3. Reduces repair and maintenance costs.
  4. Helps identify defects at an early stage.
  5. Improves equipment reliability.
  6. Helps maintain compliance with applicable classification and statutory requirements.
  7. Prevents environmental pollution caused by leakage or structural failure.
