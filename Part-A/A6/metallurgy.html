1.1 Introduction to the Iron–Carbon System

  • The iron–carbon equilibrium diagram is a graphical representation showing the phases and microstructures formed in iron–carbon alloys at different temperatures and carbon percentages under equilibrium or near-equilibrium conditions.
  • It is one of the most important diagrams in metallurgy because it explains:
    • How steel and cast iron are classified.
    • How carbon content affects mechanical properties.
    • How different phases form during heating and cooling.
    • Why steels have different hardness, strength and ductility.
    • How heat treatment changes the microstructure of steel.

Composition range

  • Pure iron contains approximately 0% carbon.
  • Steel contains approximately 0.02% to 2.11% carbon in the conventional metastable iron–iron carbide diagram.
  • Cast iron contains approximately 2.11% to 6.67% carbon.
  • The diagram generally extends up to 6.67% carbon because this is the carbon content of cementite (Fe₃C).

Two important forms of the iron–carbon diagram

  1. Iron–Iron Carbide Diagram (Fe–Fe₃C):
    • Metastable equilibrium diagram.
    • Shows carbon combined with iron in the form of cementite.
    • Most commonly used in engineering and heat treatment studies.
  2. Iron–Graphite Diagram:
    • Stable equilibrium diagram.
    • Shows carbon in the form of graphite.
    • Particularly important for understanding grey cast iron and other graphitic cast irons.
Remember: The Fe–Fe₃C diagram is the standard diagram used for understanding steel heat treatment.

1.2 Important Phases in the Iron–Carbon Diagram

A phase is a physically and chemically uniform portion of a material having definite properties and structure. There are five important phases or microstructural constituents to understand.

A. Ferrite (α-Iron)

  • Ferrite is a solid solution of carbon in alpha iron.
  • It has a Body-Centred Cubic (BCC) crystal structure.
  • It is stable at relatively low temperatures.
  • It can dissolve only a very small quantity of carbon.
  • Maximum carbon solubility is approximately 0.022% at 727°C.
  • At room temperature, carbon solubility is approximately 0.008%.

Properties: Soft and ductile; low hardness; relatively low tensile strength; good machinability and formability; magnetic below its Curie temperature (approximately 770°C).

Applications: Low-carbon steel components; structural steel; sheet metal; mild steel plates and sections.

Remember: Ferrite = Soft, ductile and low carbon solubility.

B. Austenite (γ-Iron)

  • Austenite is a solid solution of carbon in gamma iron.
  • It has a Face-Centred Cubic (FCC) crystal structure.
  • It exists at elevated temperatures in ordinary plain-carbon steels.
  • It can dissolve much more carbon than ferrite.
  • Maximum carbon solubility is approximately 2.11% at 1147°C.
  • Austenite is generally non-magnetic.

Properties: Good ductility at elevated temperatures; high carbon solubility; important phase during heat treatment; can transform into pearlite, bainite or martensite depending on cooling conditions.

Applications: Intermediate phase during steel heat treatment; austenitic stainless steels; certain wear-resistant and corrosion-resistant components.

Remember: Austenite = FCC structure and high carbon solubility.

C. Cementite (Fe₃C)

  • Cementite is an iron carbide compound.
  • Its chemical formula is Fe₃C.
  • It contains approximately 6.67% carbon by weight.
  • It is a hard and brittle constituent.
  • It is a metastable phase in the iron–iron carbide diagram.

Properties: Very high hardness; high brittleness; low ductility; high wear resistance; increases hardness and strength when present in steel microstructures.

Applications: Important constituent in hardened steels; wear-resistant components; high-carbon steel applications.

Remember: Cementite = Hard and brittle.

D. Pearlite

  • Pearlite is a microstructural constituent consisting of alternating layers of ferrite and cementite.
  • It forms when austenite containing the eutectoid composition decomposes during slow cooling.
  • It is not an individual phase; it is a mixture of two phases.
  • It has a characteristic layered or lamellar structure.

Properties: Harder and stronger than pure ferrite; less ductile than ferrite; mechanical properties depend on the spacing between ferrite and cementite layers.

Applications: Rails; wires; springs; structural steel components; wear-resistant steel parts.

Remember: Pearlite = Ferrite + Cementite.

E. Ledeburite

  • Ledeburite is a eutectic mixture of austenite and cementite.
  • It forms when liquid iron–carbon alloy containing approximately 4.3% carbon solidifies at 1147°C.
  • On further cooling below the eutectoid temperature, its austenite transforms into pearlite.
  • Therefore, at room temperature, ledeburite consists of pearlite and cementite.

Properties: Very hard; brittle; high wear resistance.

Applications: High-carbon cast irons; wear-resistant cast components.

Remember: Ledeburite = Eutectic constituent.

1.3 Important Critical Points in the Iron–Carbon Diagram

There are three major invariant reactions.

A. Peritectic Reaction

  • Occurs at approximately 1493°C.
  • Carbon content is approximately 0.16% (composition of the resulting austenite is approximately 0.17%).
  • Reaction: L + δ → γ
  • Where L = Liquid, δ = Delta ferrite, γ = Austenite.
  • It occurs at high temperatures.
  • It is less important for ordinary steel heat treatment but important in steel solidification.

B. Eutectic Reaction

  • Occurs at approximately 1147°C.
  • Carbon content is approximately 4.3%.
  • Reaction: L → γ + Fe₃C
  • Liquid transforms into a mixture of austenite and cementite.
  • The resulting microstructure is called ledeburite.

Importance: Explains the formation of cast iron structures; important in understanding solidification and cast iron properties.

C. Eutectoid Reaction (Very Important)

  • Occurs at approximately 727°C.
  • Carbon content is approximately 0.76–0.77%.
  • Reaction: γ → α + Fe₃C
  • Austenite transforms into ferrite and cementite.
  • The resulting microstructure is pearlite.

Importance: Most important reaction for understanding steel heat treatment; determines the transformation of eutectoid steel during slow cooling; helps explain annealing, normalizing and hardening.

1.4 Classification of Steel Based on Carbon Content

Steel can be classified according to its carbon percentage.

Type of steelCarbon content (approx.)Characteristics
Low-carbon steel0.05–0.25%Soft, ductile, easily welded
Medium-carbon steel0.25–0.60%Higher strength and hardness
High-carbon steel0.60–1.0%Hard, strong, less ductile
Very high-carbon steel1.0–2.11%Very hard, difficult to machine

These are approximate engineering classifications; boundaries vary between references.

Low-Carbon Steel

  • Contains a relatively low percentage of carbon.
  • Mainly consists of ferrite with some pearlite.
  • Has good ductility and weldability.
  • Can be easily formed and machined.
  • Cannot achieve very high hardness through conventional through-hardening.

Applications: Ship structural plates; hull structures; general fabrication; low-pressure piping and structural supports.

Medium-Carbon Steel

  • Contains more carbon than low-carbon steel.
  • Offers a good balance between strength, hardness and toughness.
  • Can be hardened and tempered.
  • Has lower weldability than low-carbon steel.

Applications: Shafts; gears; crankshafts; railway wheels; mechanical components.

High-Carbon Steel

  • Contains a relatively high carbon percentage.
  • Has higher hardness and wear resistance.
  • Has lower ductility.
  • Requires careful heat treatment.
  • Has comparatively poor weldability.

Applications: Cutting tools; springs; dies; high-strength wires; wear-resistant components.

1.5 Classification of Steel Based on Eutectoid Composition

This classification is particularly important in the iron–carbon equilibrium diagram.

A. Hypoeutectoid Steel

  • Carbon content is below approximately 0.76–0.77%.
  • During slow cooling, proeutectoid ferrite forms before the remaining austenite transforms into pearlite.
  • Final microstructure consists of ferrite and pearlite.

Characteristics: Comparatively soft; good ductility; lower hardness than eutectoid and hypereutectoid steels of similar processing conditions.

B. Eutectoid Steel

  • Carbon content is approximately 0.76–0.77%.
  • Entire austenite transforms into pearlite during slow cooling through the eutectoid temperature.
  • Final microstructure is approximately 100% pearlite.

Characteristics: Higher hardness than low-carbon hypoeutectoid steel; good combination of strength and ductility.

C. Hypereutectoid Steel

  • Carbon content is above approximately 0.76–0.77% and below 2.11%.
  • Proeutectoid cementite forms before the remaining austenite transforms into pearlite.
  • Final microstructure consists of pearlite and proeutectoid cementite.

Characteristics: High hardness; high wear resistance; lower ductility; greater brittleness if excessive cementite is present.

1.6 Important Transformation Temperatures

  • A₁ temperature: Lower critical temperature, approximately 727°C in plain-carbon steel.
  • A₃ temperature: Upper critical temperature for hypoeutectoid steels, above which the steel becomes fully austenitic.
  • Acm temperature: Upper critical boundary for hypereutectoid steels, above which cementite dissolves into austenite.
  • A₂ temperature: Curie temperature of ferrite, approximately 770°C, where ferrite loses ferromagnetism. It is not a phase transformation boundary.
  • A₄ temperature: Transformation between delta ferrite and austenite in pure iron, approximately 1394°C.

The actual heating and cooling transformation temperatures can differ due to thermal lag and transformation kinetics. This is why symbols such as Ac and Ar are also used.

1.7 Importance of the Iron–Carbon Diagram in Marine Engineering

  • Helps in selecting materials for marine machinery.
  • Explains why different steels are used for shafts, gears, bolts and structural plates.
  • Helps determine suitable heat treatment temperatures.
  • Explains the effects of carbon content on weldability.
  • Helps understand hardness and toughness requirements of engine components.
  • Useful for understanding cast iron components such as pump casings and machinery housings.

2.1 Introduction to Crystal Structure

  • Metals are generally crystalline materials.
  • Their atoms are arranged in an orderly and repeating three-dimensional pattern.
  • This regular arrangement is called a crystal structure.
  • The smallest repeating unit representing the crystal arrangement is called a unit cell.

Importance: Determines mechanical properties; influences density; affects ductility and strength; influences diffusion and deformation; determines how metals respond to heat treatment.

2.2 Important Terms Related to Crystal Structures

A. Crystal Lattice

  • A three-dimensional arrangement of points representing the periodic positions of atoms in a crystal.
  • Each lattice point represents an equivalent position in the crystal.

B. Unit Cell

  • The smallest repeating portion of a crystal lattice.
  • Repetition of the unit cell in three dimensions forms the complete crystal structure.

C. Coordination Number

  • Number of nearest neighbouring atoms surrounding a particular atom.
  • Higher coordination numbers generally indicate closely packed structures.

D. Atomic Packing Factor (APF)

  • Ratio of the total volume occupied by atoms in a unit cell to the total volume of the unit cell.
APF = Volume of atoms in unit cellTotal volume of unit cell

2.3 Three Important Crystal Structures in Metals

A. Body-Centred Cubic (BCC)

  • BCC structure contains atoms at all eight corners of a cube and one atom at its centre.
  • Each corner atom contributes one-eighth to the unit cell.
  • The centre atom contributes one complete atom.

Number of atoms per unit cell:

8 × 18 + 1 = 2
  • Coordination number = 8.
  • Atomic packing factor = 0.68.
  • Less closely packed than FCC.
  • Generally has fewer easily activated slip systems at room temperature.

Examples: Alpha iron (ferrite); delta iron; chromium; tungsten; molybdenum.

Important engineering points: Ferrite has a BCC structure; BCC iron has lower carbon solubility than FCC austenite; BCC metals may show a ductile-to-brittle transition depending on temperature, composition and microstructure.

B. Face-Centred Cubic (FCC)

  • FCC structure contains atoms at all eight corners and at the centre of all six faces of a cube.
  • Each corner atom contributes one-eighth.
  • Each face-centred atom contributes one-half.

Number of atoms per unit cell:

8 × 18 + 6 × 12 = 4
  • Coordination number = 12.
  • Atomic packing factor = 0.74.
  • Closely packed structure.
  • High ductility due to numerous easily activated slip systems.

Examples: Austenite (gamma iron); aluminium; copper; nickel; silver; gold.

Important engineering points: Austenite has an FCC structure; FCC iron can dissolve significantly more carbon than ferrite; FCC metals generally exhibit good formability.

C. Hexagonal Close-Packed (HCP)

  • HCP structure has atoms arranged in a hexagonal pattern.
  • It consists of closely packed atomic layers.
  • The common stacking sequence is ABAB.
  • Coordination number = 12.
  • Atomic packing factor = 0.74.
  • Closely packed structure.
  • Room-temperature ductility may be limited by the number of readily activated slip systems.

Examples: Magnesium; zinc; titanium (alpha phase); cobalt (at ordinary temperatures).

Important engineering points: Titanium alloys are used in marine applications where high strength-to-weight ratio and corrosion resistance are important; magnesium alloys are lightweight but require careful corrosion protection.

  • Iron is an allotropic metal.

2.6 Crystal Defects

  • Real metals are not perfectly arranged crystals.
  • They contain imperfections called crystal defects.
  • Defects influence mechanical, electrical, thermal and chemical properties.
  • They are important in understanding strengthening mechanisms.

Crystal defects are classified into four major types.

A. Point Defects

These are defects involving individual atomic positions.

  • Vacancy defect: An atom is missing from its normal lattice position. Vacancies help atomic diffusion. Their number increases with temperature.
  • Interstitial defect: An additional atom occupies a space between normal lattice positions. Small atoms such as carbon and nitrogen can occupy interstitial spaces in iron.
  • Substitutional defect: A foreign atom replaces a normal atom in the lattice. Common in alloy steels. Examples include chromium, nickel and manganese atoms in iron.

Importance: Alloying elements modify the properties of steel; carbon in iron changes strength and hardness.

B. Line Defects (Dislocations)

  • Dislocations are one-dimensional defects in a crystal lattice.
  • They play a major role in plastic deformation.
  • Movement of dislocations allows metals to deform without breaking all atomic bonds simultaneously.

Two major types:

  • Edge Dislocation: Occurs when an extra half-plane of atoms is inserted into the crystal. The dislocation line is perpendicular to the Burgers vector.
  • Screw Dislocation: Occurs when atomic planes are displaced in a spiral-like arrangement. The dislocation line is parallel to the Burgers vector.

Importance: Strengthening methods often work by making dislocation movement more difficult; work hardening increases dislocation density.

C. Surface or Planar Defects

  • These defects occur over a two-dimensional region.
  • Examples include grain boundaries, twin boundaries, stacking faults, phase boundaries.

D. Volume Defects

  • These are three-dimensional defects.
  • Examples: voids, pores, cracks, inclusions, large precipitate clusters.

These defects can reduce fatigue strength and toughness.

2.7 Grain Structure and Grain Boundaries

  • Most engineering metals consist of numerous small crystals called grains.
  • Each grain has a particular crystal orientation.
  • The boundary separating two adjacent grains is called a grain boundary.
  • Grain boundaries are regions where the crystal orientation changes.

Formation of Grains

  1. During solidification, small nuclei form in the liquid metal.
  2. These nuclei grow into crystals.
  3. Different crystals grow in different directions.
  4. When growing crystals meet, grain boundaries are formed.
  5. The final solid metal consists of multiple grains.

Properties of Grain Boundaries

  • Grain boundaries have higher energy than the interior of grains.
  • They obstruct dislocation movement.
  • They can act as barriers to crack propagation.
  • They can provide faster diffusion paths.
  • They may become preferred locations for corrosion or precipitation.

Grain Size and Mechanical Properties

  • Fine grains: Higher strength; higher yield strength; generally improved toughness; better resistance to crack propagation in many structural steels.
  • Coarse grains: Generally lower yield strength; may reduce toughness; can increase susceptibility to brittle fracture.

Hall–Petch Relationship

The relationship between grain size and yield strength is given by:

σy = σ0 + k d−1/2
  • σy = Yield strength.
  • σ0 = Friction stress or lattice resistance.
  • k = Hall–Petch constant.
  • d = Average grain diameter.

Conclusion: As grain size decreases, yield strength generally increases.

2.8 Grain Growth and Recrystallization

Grain Growth

  • Grain growth occurs when metal is heated to a sufficiently high temperature.
  • Smaller grains tend to disappear while larger grains grow.
  • This reduces total grain boundary area and lowers the energy of the material.
  • Excessive grain growth can reduce toughness.

Recrystallization

  • Recrystallization occurs when a cold-worked metal is heated to a suitable temperature.
  • New, strain-free grains form.
  • The dislocation density decreases.
  • Hardness and strength generally decrease.
  • Ductility increases.
Remember: Cold working increases dislocations; recrystallization reduces dislocations; grain growth increases average grain size.

2.9 Importance in Marine Engineering

  • Grain structure affects the strength of crankshafts, connecting rods and propeller shafts.
  • Grain boundaries influence fatigue failure.
  • Grain refinement improves the toughness of structural steels.
  • Crystal defects influence corrosion and mechanical properties.
  • Understanding metallurgy helps in material selection and failure analysis.

3.1 Introduction to Heat Treatment

  • Heat treatment is a controlled process of heating and cooling metals to change their mechanical properties and microstructure.
  • It is widely used in steel manufacturing and maintenance.
  • The main purpose is to obtain the required combination of: hardness, strength, toughness, ductility, wear resistance, machinability, dimensional stability.

Basic Stages of Heat Treatment

  1. Heating the metal to a specified temperature.
  2. Holding the metal at that temperature for a specified time (soaking).
  3. Cooling the metal at a controlled rate or in a selected medium.
Important: The cooling rate and temperature determine the final microstructure and mechanical properties.

3.2 Annealing

Definition

  • Annealing is a heat treatment process in which a metal is heated to a suitable temperature, held for sufficient time and cooled slowly, generally inside the furnace.
  • It is mainly used to soften steel, improve ductility and relieve internal stresses.

Objectives of Annealing

  • Reduce hardness.
  • Increase ductility.
  • Improve machinability.
  • Relieve internal stresses.
  • Improve dimensional stability.
  • Refine or modify the microstructure.
  • Improve formability.
  • Reduce brittleness.

Process of Annealing

  1. Heat the steel to the required temperature.
  2. Maintain the temperature for sufficient time.
  3. Allow the steel to cool slowly, usually inside the furnace.
  4. Obtain a relatively soft and ductile microstructure.

Types of Annealing

A. Full Annealing

  • Steel is heated above its upper critical temperature.
  • Hypoeutectoid steel is heated above A₃.
  • Hypereutectoid steel is generally heated above A₁ but below Acm for conventional full annealing.
  • It is soaked and slowly cooled in the furnace.
  • Produces relatively soft structures, generally coarse pearlite with ferrite or cementite depending on composition.

Applications: Forged components; cast steel components; components requiring improved machinability.

B. Process Annealing

  • Mainly used for low-carbon steel that has undergone cold working.
  • Steel is heated below the lower critical temperature.
  • It restores ductility by reducing the effects of work hardening.
  • It allows further cold forming.

Applications: Sheet metal; wire manufacturing; cold-formed steel products.

C. Stress-Relief Annealing

  • Used to remove or reduce residual stresses.
  • Steel is heated below the lower critical temperature.
  • It is held for sufficient time and cooled under controlled conditions.
  • It generally does not involve complete austenitization.

Applications: Welded structures; castings; machined components; large fabricated structures.

D. Spheroidizing Annealing

  • Used mainly for high-carbon and tool steels.
  • Steel is heated near or slightly below A₁ for a prolonged period, or subjected to controlled thermal cycling around A₁.
  • Cementite forms into rounded or spheroidal particles in a ferrite matrix.
  • Improves machinability and cold-forming behaviour.

Applications: High-carbon steel; bearing steels; tool steels before machining.

Advantages of Annealing

  • Improves ductility.
  • Reduces hardness.
  • Relieves internal stresses.
  • Improves machinability.
  • Improves dimensional stability.

Disadvantages

  • Requires considerable time.
  • Furnace cooling increases processing time.
  • Produces lower hardness and strength compared with hardened conditions.
  • May cause oxidation and scaling.

3.3 Normalizing

Definition

  • Normalizing is a heat treatment process in which steel is heated above its critical temperature, held for sufficient time and cooled in still air.
  • It is mainly used to refine grain structure and produce uniform mechanical properties.

Objectives of Normalizing

  • Refine grain size.
  • Improve strength.
  • Improve toughness.
  • Remove structural irregularities.
  • Improve uniformity.
  • Reduce residual stresses to some extent.
  • Improve mechanical properties after forging and casting.

Process

  1. Heat steel above its upper critical temperature.
  2. Hold at the required temperature.
  3. Remove the steel from the furnace.
  4. Allow it to cool in still air.
  5. Obtain a finer microstructure than conventional full annealing in many cases.

Microstructure

  • Hypoeutectoid steel: Ferrite + fine pearlite.
  • Eutectoid steel: Fine pearlite.
  • Hypereutectoid steel: Pearlite + proeutectoid cementite, with appropriate normalizing practice.

Applications

  • Forged crankshafts.
  • Connecting rods.
  • Structural steel components.
  • Gears and shafts.
  • Cast steel components.

Advantages

  • Grain refinement.
  • Better strength than annealed steel in many cases.
  • Improved toughness.
  • More uniform structure.
  • Usually faster than full annealing.

Disadvantages

  • May produce more distortion than slow furnace cooling.
  • Less effective for reducing hardness than full annealing.
  • Air cooling can be unsuitable for some highly hardenable steels.

3.4 Hardening

Definition

  • Hardening is a heat treatment process used to increase the hardness and strength of steel.
  • Steel is heated to its austenitizing temperature, held for sufficient time and rapidly cooled (quenched) in a suitable medium.
  • The main objective is to form martensite.

Objectives

  • Increase hardness.
  • Improve wear resistance.
  • Increase strength.
  • Improve resistance to surface damage.
  • Improve load-carrying capacity.

Hardening Process

  1. Heat steel to its required austenitizing temperature.
  2. Hold it to obtain a suitable austenitic structure.
  3. Quench rapidly using a suitable cooling medium.
  4. Austenite transforms into martensite, depending on composition and cooling conditions.
  5. The hardened steel is generally tempered afterward to improve toughness.

Martensite

  • Martensite is a hard, metastable microstructure formed by rapid, diffusionless transformation of austenite.
  • In carbon steel, it has a body-centred tetragonal (BCT) structure.
  • Carbon becomes trapped in the iron lattice.
  • This produces high internal stresses and lattice distortion.

Properties: Very high hardness; high strength; low ductility; high brittleness in the untempered condition; high residual stresses.

Quenching Media

Important: Faster cooling does not always mean better results. Excessive cooling severity can cause cracking and distortion. Quenching medium must be selected according to steel composition, component geometry and hardenability.

Hardenability

  • Hardenability is the ability of a steel to harden to a certain depth when quenched.
  • It is different from hardness.
  • Hardness refers to resistance to indentation or scratching.
  • Hardenability refers to the depth and distribution of hardness achieved.

Factors Affecting Hardenability

  • Carbon content.
  • Alloying elements.
  • Austenite grain size.
  • Quenching medium.
  • Component dimensions.
  • Cooling conditions.

Jominy End-Quench Test:

  • Used to determine hardenability of steel.
  • A standardized steel specimen is heated to the austenitizing temperature.
  • One end is quenched using a controlled water jet.
  • Hardness is measured at different distances from the quenched end.
  • The hardness profile indicates the hardenability of the steel.

3.5 Tempering

Definition

  • Tempering is a heat treatment process performed after hardening.
  • Hardened steel is reheated to a temperature below its lower critical temperature (A₁).
  • It is held for a specified time and then cooled.
  • It reduces brittleness and improves toughness.

Why Is Tempering Necessary?

  • As-quenched martensite is very hard but brittle.
  • It contains high residual stresses.
  • It may crack under impact or shock loading.
  • Tempering reduces these problems.
  • It improves the balance between strength, hardness and toughness.

Tempering Process

  1. Harden the steel by quenching.
  2. Reheat it below A₁.
  3. Maintain the temperature for a suitable period.
  4. Cool it at an appropriate rate.
  5. Obtain tempered martensite or other tempering products depending on steel composition and temperature.

Types of Tempering Based on Temperature

A. Low-Temperature Tempering

  • Approximately 150–250°C.
  • Retains relatively high hardness.
  • Reduces some residual stresses.
  • Improves toughness slightly.

Applications: Cutting tools; measuring tools; wear-resistant components.

B. Medium-Temperature Tempering

  • Approximately 250–450°C.
  • Produces a useful combination of hardness, strength and elasticity.
  • Used for components requiring spring properties.

Applications: Springs; certain elastic components.

C. High-Temperature Tempering

  • Approximately 450–650°C.
  • Produces a better balance of strength and toughness.
  • Hardness decreases compared with low-temperature tempering.
  • Commonly used for quenched-and-tempered engineering steels.

Applications: Shafts; gears; crankshafts; heavy-duty machinery components.

Temperature ranges are approximate and vary with steel composition and application.

Advantages of Tempering

  • Reduces brittleness.
  • Improves toughness.
  • Relieves residual stresses.
  • Improves dimensional stability.
  • Allows properties to be adjusted according to service requirements.

Disadvantages

  • Hardness decreases as tempering temperature generally increases.
  • Excessive tempering can reduce strength.
  • Certain alloy steels can experience temper embrittlement or secondary hardening effects.

3.7 Other Important Heat Treatment Processes

A. Austempering

  • Steel is heated to the austenitizing temperature.
  • It is rapidly cooled to a temperature above the martensite-start temperature.
  • It is held until the austenite transforms into bainite.
  • Produces good toughness and reduces distortion compared with conventional quench hardening in suitable applications.

B. Martempering

  • Steel is quenched into a bath maintained just above the martensite-start temperature.
  • It is held until temperature becomes more uniform throughout the component.
  • It is then cooled through the martensite transformation range.
  • Reduces thermal gradients, residual stresses and distortion.

C. Solution Heat Treatment

  • Used mainly for certain stainless steels and non-ferrous alloys.
  • Alloying elements are dissolved into a solid solution by heating.
  • The material is rapidly cooled to retain the solution.
  • Often followed by ageing in precipitation-hardening alloys.

D. Age Hardening (Precipitation Hardening)

  • Used to increase the strength and hardness of selected alloys.
  • Fine precipitates form during controlled ageing.
  • These precipitates obstruct dislocation movement.
  • Commonly used in aluminium, nickel and certain stainless steel alloys.

4.1 Introduction to Surface Hardening

  • Surface hardening is a process used to increase the hardness and wear resistance of the outer surface of a metal component while maintaining a relatively tough core.
  • It is particularly useful when a component must withstand surface wear and repeated loading.
  • The core provides strength and toughness, while the hardened surface resists abrasion, friction and contact stresses.

Why Is Surface Hardening Required?

  • To improve wear resistance.
  • To improve fatigue resistance.
  • To increase surface hardness.
  • To resist surface deformation.
  • To maintain core toughness.
  • To improve component service life.

Marine Engineering Applications

  • Gears.
  • Camshafts.
  • Engine valve components.
  • Pump shafts.
  • Pinions.
  • Certain bearing components.
  • Marine transmission components.

4.2 Types of Surface Hardening

There are two major categories.

  1. Case Hardening (Diffusion Methods): The chemical composition of the surface is changed. Carbon, nitrogen or other elements diffuse into the surface. A hardened case is produced after appropriate heat treatment.
  2. Surface Hardening (Transformation Methods): The surface is rapidly heated and quenched. The chemical composition generally remains unchanged. The surface transforms into martensite while the core remains relatively soft and tough.

4.3 Carburizing

Definition

  • Carburizing is a case-hardening process in which carbon is diffused into the surface of low-carbon steel at elevated temperature.
  • The carbon-rich surface is subsequently hardened by quenching.
  • The core remains comparatively low in carbon and retains toughness.

Process

  1. Select low-carbon steel.
  2. Heat the component in a carbon-rich environment.
  3. Carbon diffuses into the surface.
  4. Maintain the temperature for the required time.
  5. Quench the component to obtain a hard martensitic case.
  6. Temper if required.

Carburizing Temperature: Generally around 900–950°C. The exact temperature depends on the steel and process.

Types of Carburizing

  • A. Pack Carburizing: Components are packed in a container with carbon-rich material. The container is heated in a furnace. Carbon enters the surface.
  • B. Gas Carburizing: Components are heated in a controlled carbon-rich gas atmosphere. Provides better process control and uniformity.
  • C. Liquid Carburizing: Components are immersed in a molten carburizing medium. Traditionally uses carbon-bearing salt baths. Environmental and safety considerations limit certain older processes.

Advantages

  • High surface hardness.
  • Improved wear resistance.
  • Tough core.
  • Good contact fatigue resistance.
  • Suitable for gears and transmission components.

Disadvantages

  • Requires high temperatures and long processing times.
  • Can cause distortion.
  • Requires additional heat treatment.
  • Process control is important.

4.4 Nitriding

Definition

  • Nitriding is a surface-hardening process in which nitrogen is diffused into the surface of steel.
  • It produces hard nitrides, especially with alloying elements such as aluminium, chromium, molybdenum and vanadium.

Process

  1. Select suitable steel, usually containing nitride-forming alloying elements.
  2. Clean the component.
  3. Heat it in a nitrogen-containing atmosphere.
  4. Nitrogen diffuses into the surface.
  5. Maintain the required temperature and time.
  6. Cool under controlled conditions.

Nitriding Temperature: Generally around 500–550°C.

Types of Nitriding

  • Gas nitriding.
  • Plasma or ion nitriding.
  • Salt-bath nitriding.

Advantages

  • Very high surface hardness.
  • Good wear resistance.
  • Improved fatigue resistance.
  • Low distortion compared with carburizing.
  • Usually no quenching is required.
  • Good dimensional stability.

Disadvantages

  • Requires suitable steel composition for optimum results.
  • Relatively slow process.
  • Case depth is generally lower than that obtainable by some carburizing processes.
  • Requires careful control of the nitriding atmosphere.

Applications

  • Crankshafts.
  • Camshafts.
  • Gears.
  • Dies.
  • Engine components.
  • Precision machinery parts.

4.5 Carbonitriding

Definition

  • Carbonitriding is a case-hardening process in which both carbon and nitrogen are diffused into the surface of steel.
  • It is generally performed in a gaseous atmosphere.
  • The component is usually quenched after treatment.

Process

  1. Heat low-carbon steel in a carbon- and nitrogen-rich atmosphere.
  2. Carbon and nitrogen diffuse into the surface.
  3. Maintain the required temperature.
  4. Quench to form a hard case.
  5. Temper if necessary.

Temperature: Generally around 700–900°C.

Advantages

  • Improves surface hardness.
  • Improves wear resistance.
  • Can improve hardenability of the surface.
  • Useful for small and medium-sized components.

Applications

  • Small gears.
  • Pins.
  • Fasteners.
  • Automotive and machinery components.

4.6 Cyaniding

Definition

  • Cyaniding is a surface-hardening process in which steel is heated in a molten cyanide salt bath.
  • Both carbon and nitrogen diffuse into the surface.
  • The component is subsequently quenched.

Characteristics

  • Produces a relatively shallow hardened case.
  • Provides high surface hardness.
  • Usually used for small components.
  • Cyanide compounds are extremely toxic.
Important: Cyaniding has significant environmental and occupational safety concerns, so alternative processes are preferred in many modern industries.

4.7 Flame Hardening

Definition

  • Flame hardening is a surface-hardening process in which the surface of a steel component is heated rapidly using an oxy-fuel flame.
  • It is then quenched rapidly.
  • The heated surface transforms into martensite.

Process

  1. Clean the component.
  2. Heat the surface using an oxy-fuel flame.
  3. Raise the surface temperature above the austenitizing temperature.
  4. Quench using water or another suitable medium.
  5. Obtain a hardened surface with a relatively tough core.

Advantages

  • Fast process.
  • Suitable for large components.
  • No change in chemical composition.
  • Can selectively harden specific areas.
  • Economical for certain large components.

Disadvantages

  • Requires skilled operation.
  • Risk of distortion.
  • Uneven heating can cause cracks.
  • Not suitable for all steel compositions.

Applications

  • Large gears.
  • Machine guideways.
  • Shafts.
  • Railway components.
  • Heavy machinery parts.

4.8 Induction Hardening

Definition

  • Induction hardening is a surface-hardening process in which the surface of a steel component is heated by electromagnetic induction.
  • High-frequency alternating current flows through an induction coil.
  • It produces an alternating magnetic field.
  • Eddy currents are induced in the component, generating heat.
  • The heated surface is subsequently quenched to form martensite.

Process

  1. Place the component inside or near the induction coil.
  2. Pass alternating current through the coil.
  3. Generate an alternating magnetic field.
  4. Induce eddy currents in the steel.
  5. Heat the surface rapidly.
  6. Quench the heated region.
  7. Obtain a hardened martensitic surface.

Advantages

  • Rapid heating.
  • Localized surface hardening.
  • Good process control.
  • Less oxidation and scaling than many furnace processes.
  • Suitable for mass production.
  • Core toughness is retained.

Disadvantages

  • High initial equipment cost.
  • Requires suitable electrical power and equipment.
  • Component geometry affects heating uniformity.
  • Not suitable for every component.

Applications

  • Gears.
  • Shafts.
  • Crankshafts.
  • Camshafts.
  • Sprockets.
  • Transmission components.

4.10 Other Important Surface Treatments

A. Electroplating

  • A thin metallic coating is deposited on a component using electrolysis.
  • The component is generally the cathode.
  • The coating metal is deposited from an electrolyte.

Examples: Chromium plating; nickel plating; zinc plating.

Applications: Corrosion protection; improved wear resistance; improved surface appearance.

B. Galvanizing

  • Galvanizing is the process of coating steel or iron with zinc.
  • Zinc protects the underlying steel from corrosion.
  • Hot-dip galvanizing involves immersing steel in molten zinc.
  • Zinc provides sacrificial protection even if the coating is locally damaged.

Applications: Structural steel; shipyard structures; outdoor equipment; steel supports and fittings.

C. Anodizing

  • Anodizing is an electrochemical process mainly used for aluminium and its alloys.
  • It forms a thicker protective oxide layer on the surface.
  • Improves corrosion and wear resistance.
  • Can improve paint adhesion.

D. Thermal Spraying

  • A coating material is heated and projected onto a prepared surface.
  • Coatings may be metallic, ceramic or composite.
  • Improves wear resistance, corrosion resistance and thermal protection.

E. Shot Peening

  • Small spherical particles are blasted onto the metal surface.
  • This produces compressive residual stresses.
  • It improves fatigue resistance by reducing the tendency for surface cracks to initiate and propagate.

Applications: Springs; gears; shafts; fatigue-loaded components.

F. Phosphating

  • A chemical conversion coating is formed on steel surfaces.
  • It improves corrosion resistance and paint adhesion.
  • Also used as a base for lubricating coatings.

5.1 Introduction to Welding Metallurgy

  • Welding metallurgy is the study of metallurgical changes that occur in metals during welding.
  • Welding involves localized heating and cooling.
  • This thermal cycle changes the microstructure and mechanical properties of the base metal near the weld.
  • Understanding welding metallurgy is essential to avoid defects, cracks and failures.

Importance in Marine Engineering

Welding is extensively used in:

  • Ship hull fabrication.
  • Marine engine structures.
  • Boiler manufacturing and repair.
  • Pressure vessels.
  • Fuel and lubrication pipelines.
  • Exhaust systems.
  • Structural supports.
  • Propeller and shaft-related repairs where approved procedures permit.

5.2 Basic Principle of Welding

  • Welding is a joining process in which two or more materials are joined by heat, pressure or a combination of both.
  • In fusion welding, the base metal is melted at the joint.
  • Filler material may be added.
  • The molten metal solidifies and forms a joint.

Types of Welding

A. Fusion Welding

  • Base metal is melted.
  • Filler metal may or may not be used.
  • Examples: Arc welding; gas welding; laser welding; electron beam welding.

B. Solid-State Welding

  • Joining occurs without melting the base metal.
  • Heat and/or pressure are applied.
  • Examples: Friction welding; diffusion welding; ultrasonic welding; explosive welding.

5.3 Heat-Affected Zone (HAZ)

Definition

  • The Heat-Affected Zone (HAZ) is the region of the base metal adjacent to the weld that does not melt but experiences changes in microstructure and mechanical properties due to welding heat.

Three Main Regions of a Welded Joint

  1. Fusion Zone (Weld Metal): The region where the base metal has melted. Filler material may mix with the melted base metal. Solidifies after welding.
  2. Heat-Affected Zone: The region that does not melt. Experiences high temperatures. Its microstructure and properties may change.
  3. Base Metal: The unaffected region. Remains outside the significant welding thermal cycle.

Subregions of HAZ in Steel

  • A. Coarse-Grained HAZ: Experiences very high peak temperatures. Austenite grains grow significantly. May have reduced toughness.
  • B. Fine-Grained HAZ: Experiences lower peak temperatures than the coarse-grained region. Austenite grains become finer. Often exhibits better toughness than coarse-grained HAZ.
  • C. Intercritical HAZ: Heated between A₁ and A₃. Partial transformation to austenite occurs. Produces mixed microstructures after cooling.
  • D. Subcritical HAZ: Heated below A₁. Does not undergo austenitization. May experience tempering, recovery or other subcritical changes.

5.4 Welding Thermal Cycle

  • A welding thermal cycle refers to the temperature changes experienced by a particular location during welding.
  • Metal near the weld reaches a high temperature and then cools.
  • Different locations experience different peak temperatures and cooling rates.

Important Factors Affecting the Thermal Cycle

  • Welding current.
  • Welding voltage.
  • Welding speed.
  • Heat input.
  • Thickness of the material.
  • Type of welding process.
  • Preheating temperature.
  • Interpass temperature.
  • Joint geometry.
  • Cooling conditions.

Heat Input

For common arc-welding calculations:

H = η V Iv
  • H = Heat input per unit length.
  • η = Thermal efficiency.
  • V = Welding voltage.
  • I = Welding current.
  • v = Welding travel speed.

For V in volts, I in amperes and v in mm/s, H is obtained in J/mm.

Important: Higher heat input generally increases the width of the HAZ. It can increase grain growth. It may reduce cooling rate. Excessive heat input may reduce toughness in certain steels.

5.5 Cooling Rate and Microstructure

  • The cooling rate after welding determines the microstructure formed in the weld metal and HAZ.
  • Slow cooling may produce ferrite and pearlite.
  • Faster cooling can produce bainite or martensite in hardenable steels.
  • Very rapid cooling can increase hardness and brittleness.
  • The exact transformation depends on steel composition, grain size and cooling conditions.

Carbon Equivalent (CE)

  • Carbon equivalent is an empirical method used to estimate the weldability and hardenability of steel.
  • It accounts for the combined influence of carbon and alloying elements.

A commonly used IIW formula is:

CE = C + Mn6 + Cr + Mo + V5 + Ni + Cu15

Where all elements represent their weight percentages.

Interpretation:

  • Lower CE generally indicates better weldability.
  • Higher CE indicates greater hardenability and increased risk of HAZ hardening and hydrogen cracking.
  • Preheating and other precautions may be necessary for high-CE steels.

Different carbon-equivalent formulas are used for different steel grades and applications.

5.6 Welding Defects Related to Metallurgy

A. Hot Cracking

  • Also called solidification cracking in weld metal when it occurs during solidification.
  • Occurs at high temperatures during or immediately after welding.
  • Often forms along the centreline of a weld.
  • May be associated with low-melting-point segregated constituents.
  • Excessive restraint and unsuitable weld geometry can increase the risk.

Prevention: Use suitable filler material; control weld composition; maintain proper joint design; control welding parameters; avoid excessive restraint.

B. Cold Cracking (Hydrogen-Induced Cracking)

  • Occurs after welding, usually when the metal has cooled.
  • Also known as hydrogen-assisted cracking.
  • Often occurs in hard and brittle HAZ regions.
  • Requires the presence of diffusible hydrogen, a susceptible microstructure and tensile stress.
  • Can occur hours after welding.

Prevention: Use low-hydrogen electrodes; bake and store electrodes according to manufacturer instructions; preheat when required; control cooling rate; reduce residual stresses through suitable procedures.

C. Lamellar Tearing

  • Occurs in rolled steel plates.
  • Usually develops beneath a weld.
  • Caused by through-thickness strain acting on inclusions and weak planes.
  • Often produces a step-like or terrace-like crack appearance.

Prevention: Use steel with improved through-thickness properties; modify joint design; reduce restraint; control welding sequence.

D. Porosity

  • Small cavities form due to trapped gas in the solidifying weld metal.
  • Common causes include moisture, contamination and inadequate shielding.

Prevention: Clean the joint; use dry electrodes; maintain proper shielding gas; use correct welding parameters.

E. Lack of Fusion

  • Occurs when weld metal fails to fuse properly with the base metal or previous weld passes.
  • May result from low heat input, poor technique or contamination.

Prevention: Correct welding current; proper joint preparation; suitable electrode angle; adequate cleaning between passes.

5.7 Residual Stresses in Welding

  • Residual stresses are internal stresses remaining in a material after external loads or thermal gradients are removed.
  • Welding produces localized heating and cooling.
  • The heated region expands.
  • Surrounding colder material restricts expansion.
  • During cooling, contraction is also restricted.
  • This produces residual stresses.

Effects of Residual Stresses

  • Distortion.
  • Warping.
  • Cracking.
  • Reduced fatigue life.
  • Stress corrosion cracking susceptibility in certain materials.
  • Dimensional inaccuracies.

Methods to Reduce Residual Stresses

  • Proper welding sequence.
  • Balanced welding.
  • Preheating.
  • Controlled heat input.
  • Stress-relief heat treatment where permitted.
  • Suitable joint design.
  • Reduced restraint.

5.8 Preheating and Post-Weld Heat Treatment (PWHT)

A. Preheating

  • Preheating involves raising the temperature of the base metal before welding.
  • It reduces the cooling rate.
  • It helps reduce the formation of hard and brittle microstructures.
  • It provides more time for diffusible hydrogen to escape.
  • It reduces the risk of hydrogen-induced cracking.

Factors determining preheating requirements: Carbon equivalent; material thickness; hydrogen level; joint restraint; welding process; welding procedure specifications.

B. Post-Weld Heat Treatment (PWHT)

  • PWHT is a heat treatment applied after welding.
  • It is commonly used for certain pressure vessels, boilers and piping systems.
  • It may reduce residual stresses.
  • It can temper hard microstructures.
  • It can improve toughness and dimensional stability depending on the material and procedure.
Important: PWHT temperature and holding time must be selected according to the material specification and approved welding procedure. Excessive PWHT can negatively affect mechanical properties.

5.9 Common Welding Processes and Their Applications

A. Shielded Metal Arc Welding (SMAW)

  • Also called manual metal arc welding.
  • Uses a flux-coated consumable electrode.
  • Electric arc melts the electrode and base metal.
  • Flux produces shielding gases and slag.
  • Commonly used in shipbuilding and maintenance.

Advantages: Portable equipment; suitable for outdoor work; can weld many steel grades; relatively low equipment cost.

Disadvantages: Requires skilled operators; lower productivity than some automatic processes; electrode replacement is frequent; slag removal is required.

B. Gas Metal Arc Welding (GMAW / MIG-MAG)

  • Uses a continuously fed consumable wire electrode.
  • Shielding gas protects the molten weld pool.
  • MIG generally uses inert shielding gases.
  • MAG uses active shielding gases.

Advantages: High welding speed; continuous wire feeding; high productivity; suitable for automated welding.

Disadvantages: Sensitive to wind; requires gas supply; equipment is more complex than SMAW.

C. Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW / TIG)

  • Uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode.
  • Shielding gas, generally argon or helium, protects the weld pool.
  • Filler metal may be added separately.

Advantages: High-quality welds; excellent control; suitable for thin materials; useful for stainless steel and aluminium.

Disadvantages: Relatively slow; requires high operator skill; equipment cost is higher.

D. Submerged Arc Welding (SAW)

  • Uses a continuously fed consumable electrode.
  • The arc operates beneath a layer of granular flux.
  • Produces high deposition rates.

Advantages: High productivity; deep penetration; good weld quality; suitable for thick plates.

Applications: Ship hull fabrication; heavy structural welding; pressure vessel manufacturing; large steel fabrication.

E. Oxy-Acetylene Welding

  • Uses combustion of acetylene and oxygen.
  • Produces a high-temperature flame.
  • Can be used for welding, brazing and cutting.
  • Suitable for certain repair and maintenance operations.

5.10 Welding Metallurgy in Marine Engineering

  • Ship hull plates are commonly welded using approved welding procedures.
  • Marine boilers and pressure vessels require strict control of weld quality.
  • Engine structures must withstand vibration and cyclic loading.
  • Propulsion machinery components require careful control of residual stresses.
  • Fuel and lubrication systems must have leak-free welded joints.
  • Welding defects can cause fatigue failure, leakage and structural damage.
  • Welding procedures and inspection must comply with applicable classification society and statutory requirements.

7.2 Final Concepts to Remember

  1. Ferrite has a BCC structure, whereas austenite has an FCC structure.
  2. Austenite can dissolve much more carbon than ferrite.
  3. Cementite is hard and brittle.
  4. Pearlite consists of ferrite and cementite.
  5. The eutectoid reaction occurs at approximately 727°C and 0.76–0.77% carbon.
  6. Hypoeutectoid steel contains less carbon than eutectoid steel.
  7. Hypereutectoid steel contains more carbon than eutectoid steel.
  8. Fine grains generally improve strength and toughness.
  9. Dislocations are responsible for plastic deformation in metals.
  10. Annealing generally softens steel and improves ductility.
  11. Normalizing generally produces finer grains than full annealing.
  12. Hardening increases hardness by forming martensite.
  13. Tempering reduces brittleness and improves toughness.
  14. Hardenability is different from hardness.
  15. Carburizing introduces carbon into the steel surface.
  16. Nitriding introduces nitrogen into the steel surface.
  17. Flame and induction hardening do not change the surface chemical composition.
  18. Shot peening introduces compressive residual stresses.
  19. The HAZ is not melted during welding.
  20. High carbon equivalent generally means poorer weldability.
  21. Preheating helps reduce the risk of hydrogen-induced cracking.
  22. PWHT can reduce residual stresses and temper hard microstructures.
  23. Hot cracking occurs at high temperatures, whereas hydrogen-induced cold cracking occurs after cooling.
  24. Welding thermal cycles influence grain size and mechanical properties.
  25. Marine machinery requires an appropriate combination of strength, toughness, fatigue resistance, wear resistance and corrosion resistance.
