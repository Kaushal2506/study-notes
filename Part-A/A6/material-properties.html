Material Properties

Subject: Engineering Materials & Metallurgy Level: Mechanical Engineering Graduate

Material properties describe how a material behaves under forces, temperature, and environmental conditions. They determine suitability for machine components and engineering structures. These notes cover mechanical, physical, and thermal properties, along with key behaviour under loading, fatigue, creep, impact, and wear.

1. Introduction to Material Properties

Material properties are the characteristics that describe how a material behaves when subjected to different types of forces, temperatures, environmental conditions and operating conditions. They determine whether a material is suitable for manufacturing a particular machine component or engineering structure.

Material selection is important because every engineering component experiences different working conditions. For example:

  • A crankshaft requires high strength, toughness and fatigue resistance.
  • A cylinder liner requires wear resistance, hardness and thermal stability.
  • A boiler requires high-temperature strength and resistance to creep.
  • A ship's propeller requires corrosion resistance, strength and fatigue resistance.
  • Bearings require wear resistance, hardness and good frictional properties.

Classification of Material Properties

  1. Mechanical properties
  2. Physical properties
  3. Thermal properties
  4. Chemical properties
  5. Electrical properties
  6. Magnetic properties
  7. Technological or manufacturing properties

2. Mechanical Properties

Mechanical properties describe how a material behaves when subjected to external forces or mechanical loads. They indicate the ability of a material to resist deformation, withstand loads and prevent failure.

Important Mechanical Properties

1. Strength — Ability of a material to withstand an applied load without failure. It indicates how much stress a material can withstand before yielding or breaking.

  • Tensile strength: resist forces that pull or stretch.
  • Compressive strength: resist forces that squeeze or compress.
  • Shear strength: resist forces acting parallel to the cross-sectional area.
  • Bending strength: resist stresses produced by bending loads.
  • Torsional strength: resist twisting forces or torque.

2. Elasticity — Ability of a material to regain its original shape and dimensions after the removal of an applied load. Steel generally exhibits good elasticity.

3. Plasticity — Ability of a material to undergo permanent deformation without breaking. Useful in forging, rolling, extrusion and sheet metal forming.

4. Ductility — Ability to undergo considerable plastic deformation under tensile loading before fracture. Examples: copper, aluminium, mild steel, gold.

5. Malleability — Ability to undergo plastic deformation under compressive forces without cracking. Can be hammered or rolled into thin sheets. Examples: gold, silver, aluminium, lead.

Remember the difference:
Ductility → Ability to be drawn into wires (tensile deformation).
Malleability → Ability to be hammered or rolled into sheets (compressive deformation).

6. Brittleness — Tendency of a material to fracture with little or no plastic deformation. Examples: glass, cast iron, ceramics, hardened steel under certain conditions.

7. Toughness — Ability to absorb energy and plastically deform before fracture. Depends on both strength and ductility. Examples: mild steel, alloy steels, certain nickel steels.

8. Hardness — Ability to resist localized plastic deformation. Resistance to scratching, indentation, abrasion and surface penetration.

BrinellHardened steel or tungsten carbide ball

9. Stiffness — Ability to resist elastic deformation. Related to Young's modulus. Higher Young's modulus means greater resistance to elastic deformation for the same geometry and loading. Steel is stiffer than aluminium because steel has a higher Young's modulus.

10. Resilience — Ability to absorb energy within its elastic limit and release that energy when the load is removed. Energy absorbed per unit volume within the elastic limit is called the modulus of resilience. Applications: springs, shock absorbers, elastic couplings.

11. Fatigue Resistance — Ability to withstand repeated or fluctuating stresses without failure. Fatigue failure can occur even when applied stress is below yield strength. Examples: crankshafts, connecting rods, propeller shafts, rotating machinery.

12. Creep Resistance — Ability to resist slow, permanent deformation under constant stress over an extended period, particularly at elevated temperatures. Applications: steam boilers, steam turbine components, exhaust valves, high-temperature engine components.

3. Physical Properties

Physical properties describe characteristics that can be observed or measured without changing chemical composition.

1. Density — Mass per unit volume. Represented by ρ.

ρ = mV

Where ρ = density (kg/m³), m = mass (kg), V = volume (m³).

MaterialApproximate density
Aluminium2700 kg/m³
Steel7850 kg/m³
Copper8960 kg/m³
Cast iron7200 kg/m³
Lead11340 kg/m³

2. Specific Gravity — Ratio of the density of a substance to the density of a reference substance (water). Dimensionless quantity.

SG = Density of materialDensity of water

SG > 1 → material is denser than water. SG < 1 → material is less dense than water.

3. Porosity — Presence of small voids, pores or empty spaces within a material. Affects strength, density, permeability and durability. May develop during casting, welding and powder metallurgy.

4. Permeability — Ability to allow fluids or gases to pass through interconnected pores. Important in filtration, porous bearings and certain engineering materials.

5. Electrical Conductivity — Ability to allow electric current to pass through. Metals are generally good conductors. Examples: copper, aluminium, silver.

6. Electrical Resistivity — Opposition to the flow of electric current. Inverse of electrical conductivity.

ρe = R AL

Where ρₑ = electrical resistivity (Ω·m), R = resistance (Ω), A = cross-sectional area (m²), L = length (m).

7. Magnetic Properties — How a material responds to an applied magnetic field.

  • Ferromagnetic: strongly attracted, can retain magnetism. Examples: iron, cobalt, nickel.
  • Paramagnetic: weakly attracted.
  • Diamagnetic: weakly repelled.
  • Antiferromagnetic: magnetic moments align in opposite directions and largely cancel.

8. Optical Properties — How a material interacts with light: transparent (most light passes through clearly), translucent (light passes but scatters), opaque (no light passes).

9. Corrosion Resistance — Ability to resist deterioration caused by chemical or electrochemical reactions with the environment. Particularly important in marine environments. Stainless steel, aluminium alloys, copper-nickel alloys and protective coatings are used.

4. Thermal Properties

Thermal properties describe how a material behaves when subjected to changes in temperature or heat energy.

1. Thermal Conductivity — Ability to conduct heat. Represented by k or λ. SI unit: W/m·K. Copper and aluminium have high conductivity; steel lower; wood very low.

2. Specific Heat Capacity — Heat energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K or 1°C. Symbol c. SI unit: J/kg·K.

Q = m c ΔT

Where Q = heat energy (J), m = mass (kg), c = specific heat capacity (J/kg·K), ΔT = change in temperature (K or °C).

3. Thermal Expansion — Tendency of a material to increase in dimensions when temperature rises.

ΔL = α L₀ ΔT
ΔA = β A₀ ΔT
ΔV = γ V₀ ΔT

For isotropic solids: β ≈ 2α and γ ≈ 3α.

4. Thermal Shock Resistance — Ability to withstand sudden temperature changes without cracking or failing. Materials with low thermal expansion and good thermal conductivity often have better thermal shock resistance.

5. Melting Point — Temperature at which a solid changes to liquid at a specified pressure. Aluminium ≈ 660°C; copper ≈ 1085°C; iron ≈ 1538°C.

6. Thermal Stability — Ability to retain properties when exposed to high temperatures or repeated temperature variations.

7. Thermal Diffusivity — How quickly heat spreads through a material.

a = kρ c

High thermal diffusivity means the material responds more quickly to temperature changes.

Marine Engineering Applications

  • Copper and copper alloys in heat exchangers.
  • Insulating materials around boilers and hot pipelines.
  • Expansion joints in pipelines.
  • Adequate clearances in engine components.
  • Boiler and steam pipelines must accommodate thermal expansion.

These four properties are especially important because they describe how materials deform, absorb energy and resist damage.

5.1 Ductility

Ability of a material to undergo plastic deformation under tensile loading before fracture. Measured using a tensile test, expressed as percentage elongation or percentage reduction in area.

%EL = Lf − L0L0 × 100
%RA = A0 − AfA0 × 100

Where L₀ = original gauge length, Lf = final gauge length after fracture, A₀ = original cross-sectional area, Af = final cross-sectional area at fracture.

  • Ductile materials undergo noticeable deformation before fracture.
  • They absorb considerable energy before breaking.
  • They generally provide visible warning before failure.
  • Ductility usually increases with temperature for many metals.
  • Excessive cold working generally reduces ductility.

Examples: mild steel, copper, aluminium, gold.

5.2 Brittleness

Tendency of a material to fracture with very little plastic deformation. Brittle materials generally fail suddenly.

  • Little or no permanent deformation before fracture.
  • Sudden failure may occur.
  • Generally low energy absorption before fracture.
  • Fracture surfaces may appear relatively flat or granular.

Factors affecting brittleness: low temperature in susceptible materials, high strain rate, sharp notches, certain heat treatments, material composition and microstructure.

Examples: glass, ceramics, grey cast iron, some hardened steels.

Marine engineering concept: Brittle fracture in ship structures and pressure vessels is dangerous because failure may occur without significant visible deformation. Suitable material selection, inspection and temperature control help reduce this risk.

5.3 Toughness

Ability to absorb energy and undergo plastic deformation before fracture. Represents energy absorbed per unit volume up to fracture. Related to both strength and ductility.

The area under the complete stress-strain curve up to fracture represents the modulus of toughness.

  • High energy absorption before fracture.
  • Good resistance to crack propagation.
  • Important for components subjected to shock and impact.
  • Toughness can vary significantly with temperature and loading rate.

Examples: mild steel, suitable alloy steels, nickel-containing structural steels.

5.4 Hardness

Resistance of a material to localized plastic deformation. Indicates resistance to indentation, scratching and abrasion. A surface-related mechanical property.

  • High hardness generally improves resistance to indentation and abrasive wear.
  • Hardness is often associated with strength, but does not directly measure toughness.
  • Extremely hard materials can sometimes be brittle.
  • Hardness can be increased by carburizing, nitriding, induction hardening and other surface treatments.

These three properties are extremely important in mechanical engineering because they explain how materials behave under different loading conditions and over time.

6.1 Elasticity

Definition: Ability of a material to return to its original shape and dimensions after the removal of an external load. Elastic deformation is temporary and recoverable.

Important terms

  • Elastic Deformation: Temporary deformation that disappears after removal of load.
  • Elastic Limit: Maximum stress up to which a material can return to original dimensions after unloading.
  • Proportional Limit: Maximum stress up to which stress is directly proportional to strain. Hooke's law applies.

Young's Modulus

E = σε

Where E = Young's modulus (Pa), σ = normal stress (Pa), ε = longitudinal strain (dimensionless).

  • Higher Young's modulus means greater stiffness.
  • Young's modulus is a material property independent of component shape.
  • Steel has a higher Young's modulus than aluminium.
MaterialYoung's modulus
Steel200 GPa
Aluminium70 GPa
Copper110–130 GPa
Cast iron100–170 GPa

Hooke's Law

σ = E ε

Within the proportional limit, stress is directly proportional to strain.

6.2 Fatigue

Definition: Progressive damage and eventual failure of a material due to repeated or fluctuating stresses. Fatigue failure can occur even when maximum applied stress is below yield strength.

Mechanism of fatigue failure

  1. Crack Initiation: Small cracks develop at weak points, often at the surface (keyways, sharp corners, holes, scratches, machining marks).
  2. Crack Propagation: Repeated loading causes the crack to grow gradually with each cycle.
  3. Final Fracture: Remaining material cannot support the load and sudden final fracture occurs.

Types of fatigue loading

  • Completely reversed stress: σmax = −σmin
  • Repeated or pulsating stress: varies between zero and a maximum of the same sign.
  • Fluctuating stress: varies between max and min values that may not be equal and opposite.

Important fatigue terms

  • Fatigue Life: Number of loading cycles before failure at a specified stress level. Represented by N.
  • Fatigue Strength: Maximum stress for a specified number of cycles without failure.
  • Endurance Limit: Stress amplitude below which certain materials can theoretically withstand infinite cycles. Some steels exhibit a distinct endurance limit; aluminium alloys generally do not.
  • S-N Curve: Relationship between stress amplitude (S) and cycles to failure (N). Also called the Wöhler curve.

Factors affecting fatigue strength

  • Surface finish: rough surfaces generally reduce fatigue strength.
  • Stress concentration: sharp corners and notches increase local stress.
  • Material composition: alloying and microstructure influence performance.
  • Heat treatment: appropriate treatment can improve resistance.
  • Corrosion: corrosive environments accelerate fatigue damage.
  • Temperature: high temperatures may reduce performance.
  • Residual stresses: compressive residual stresses at the surface can improve resistance.
Important: A component can fail due to fatigue even if its operating stress is below the material's yield strength.

6.3 Creep

Definition: Slow and progressive permanent deformation of a material under constant stress over a long period. Especially significant at elevated temperatures.

Conditions favouring creep

  • High operating temperature.
  • Constant or sustained loading.
  • Long operating duration.
  • Material susceptible to time-dependent deformation.

For many metals, creep becomes significant at temperatures above approximately 0.4 times their absolute melting temperature:

T ≈ 0.4 Tm

Temperatures must be expressed in Kelvin. This is an approximate guideline, not a universal threshold.

Three stages of creep

  1. Primary Creep (Transient): Creep rate initially high, gradually decreases due to strain hardening.
  2. Secondary Creep (Steady-State): Creep rate approximately constant; generally lasts the longest; strain hardening and recovery approximately balanced.
  3. Tertiary Creep (Accelerating): Creep rate increases rapidly; internal damage develops; necking, void formation and microstructural deterioration may occur; component eventually fractures.

Factors affecting creep

  • Temperature.
  • Applied stress.
  • Operating time.
  • Grain size and microstructure.
  • Material composition.
  • Heat treatment.
  • Oxidation and environmental conditions.

Fatigue vs Creep

FatigueCreep
Caused by repeated or fluctuating stressCaused by sustained stress over time
May occur at relatively low temperaturesParticularly important at high temperatures
Failure depends strongly on loading cyclesDeformation depends strongly on time
Crack initiation and propagation importantTime-dependent deformation and damage important

Memory trick:
Fatigue → Repeated loading.
Creep → Continuous loading over time, particularly at high temperature.

7.1 Impact Strength

Definition: Ability of a material to withstand sudden or rapidly applied loads without fracturing. Indicates the energy absorbed during impact loading.

  • Impact loading is generally associated with high strain rates.
  • Brittle materials usually have poor impact resistance.
  • Tough and ductile materials generally perform better under impact loading.
  • Impact strength depends on temperature, composition, microstructure and loading rate.

Impact testing methods

1. Charpy Impact Test — Standard notched specimen placed horizontally, supported at both ends. Pendulum hammer strikes opposite the notch. Energy absorbed calculated from loss in pendulum's mechanical energy. Notch faces away from striker.

2. Izod Impact Test — Specimen held vertically as a cantilever and struck by a pendulum hammer. Notch generally faces the striker.

Charpy TestIzod Test
Specimen is horizontalSpecimen is vertical
Supported at both endsClamped at one end
Three-point bending arrangementCantilever arrangement

Ductile-to-Brittle Transition Temperature (DBTT)

Some ferritic steels transition from ductile to brittle behaviour as temperature decreases. Below the transition region, the material absorbs less impact energy and becomes more susceptible to brittle fracture.

Marine engineering importance: Ship structures may operate in cold environments. Steel used in low-temperature service must have suitable toughness. Brittle fracture in ship hulls can cause serious structural failure. Material selection must consider the minimum expected operating temperature.

7.2 Wear Resistance

Definition: Ability of a material to resist gradual loss of material from its surface due to contact, friction or relative motion.

Types of Wear

  • Abrasive Wear: Hard particles or rough surfaces remove material from a softer surface. Examples: dust in engine components, abrasive particles in lubricating oil, wear of piston rings and cylinder liners.
  • Adhesive Wear: Contacting surfaces develop localized adhesive junctions; junctions break and material transfers. Examples: poorly lubricated bearings, sliding metal components.
  • Corrosive Wear: Combined effects of chemical/electrochemical reactions and mechanical wear. Examples: marine components exposed to seawater, pumps handling corrosive fluids.
  • Surface Fatigue Wear: Repeated contact stresses produce cracks below or at the surface; material breaks away as small particles. Examples: gear teeth, rolling element bearings.
  • Erosive Wear: Solid particles or fluid droplets strike a surface at high velocity. Examples: pump impellers, pipe bends carrying abrasive fluids, turbine components.

Factors Affecting Wear Resistance

  • Material hardness.
  • Surface finish.
  • Lubrication quality.
  • Contact pressure.
  • Relative speed between surfaces.
  • Temperature.
  • Material combination.
  • Presence of dust and abrasive particles.
  • Corrosive environment.

Methods to Improve Wear Resistance

  1. Hardening: Heat treatment can increase surface hardness.
  2. Carburizing: Adds carbon to surface of suitable steels to produce a hard outer layer.
  3. Nitriding: Introduces nitrogen into the surface to improve hardness and wear resistance.
  4. Induction hardening: Uses electromagnetic induction heating followed by rapid cooling.
  5. Surface coating: Applying protective layers to reduce wear.
  6. Proper lubrication: Reduces direct metal-to-metal contact.
  7. Material selection: Selecting suitable wear-resistant alloys.
  8. Proper alignment: Reduces uneven contact and excessive friction.

Marine Engineering Applications

  • Cylinder liners.
  • Piston rings.
  • Bearings.
  • Gear teeth.
  • Pump shafts.
  • Valve seats.
  • Propeller shaft seals.
  • Fuel injection equipment.

Understanding the relationship between material properties is important because improving one property may sometimes reduce another.

8.1 Hardness vs Toughness

Hardness measures resistance to localized indentation. Toughness measures ability to absorb energy before fracture. A very hard material is not necessarily tough. Some hardened steels have high hardness but lower toughness if not properly tempered.

Example: Gear teeth require high surface hardness to resist wear. The gear core requires sufficient toughness to withstand shock and bending loads.

8.2 Strength vs Ductility

Strength indicates resistance to applied stress. Ductility indicates ability to deform plastically before fracture. Increasing strength through certain heat treatments or cold working may reduce ductility. Proper material selection requires a suitable balance.

8.3 Stiffness vs Strength

Stiffness is resistance to elastic deformation. Strength is resistance to yielding or failure. A material can be stiff but not necessarily strong. Young's modulus measures stiffness, whereas yield strength and tensile strength measure strength.

Example: Steel and aluminium have very different stiffness, but their strength depends on the particular alloy and heat treatment.

8.4 Resilience vs Toughness

Resilience measures energy absorbed within the elastic limit. Toughness measures total energy absorbed up to fracture. Resilience concerns recoverable elastic energy. Toughness includes both elastic and plastic energy absorption.

8.5 Fatigue vs Impact Resistance

Fatigue resistance concerns repeated or fluctuating loading. Impact resistance concerns sudden loading. A material may have good fatigue strength but relatively poor impact toughness, or vice versa. Both properties must be considered when selecting materials for marine machinery.

9.1 Why Material Selection Is Important in Marine Engineering

  • Marine equipment operates under continuous and varying loads.
  • Machinery is exposed to vibration and cyclic stresses.
  • Seawater creates a highly corrosive environment.
  • Engines and boilers operate at elevated temperatures.
  • Some ship components experience sudden impact and shock loading.
  • Equipment failure can affect vessel safety, reliability and operational efficiency.

Therefore, material selection must consider:

  1. Mechanical strength.
  2. Fatigue resistance.
  3. Creep resistance.
  4. Corrosion resistance.
  5. Wear resistance.
  6. Thermal properties.
  7. Operating temperature.
  8. Maintenance requirements.
  9. Weight and cost.
  10. Manufacturing and repair suitability.

12. Key Points to Remember for GME Pre-Sea Training

  1. Strength: Ability to withstand applied loads without failure.
  2. Stiffness: Resistance to elastic deformation.
  3. Elasticity: Ability to regain original dimensions after unloading.
  4. Plasticity: Ability to undergo permanent deformation without fracture.
  5. Ductility: Ability to be drawn into wires.
  6. Malleability: Ability to be hammered into sheets.
  7. Brittleness: Tendency to fracture with little plastic deformation.
  8. Toughness: Ability to absorb energy before fracture.
  9. Hardness: Resistance to indentation and scratching.
  10. Resilience: Ability to absorb and release elastic strain energy.
  11. Fatigue: Failure due to repeated or fluctuating stresses.
  12. Creep: Slow permanent deformation under sustained stress over time.
  13. Impact strength: Resistance to sudden mechanical loading.
  14. Wear resistance: Ability to resist surface material loss.
  15. Density: Mass per unit volume.
  16. Specific gravity: Ratio of material density to reference density.
  17. Thermal conductivity: Ability to conduct heat.
  18. Specific heat: Heat required to raise temperature of unit mass by one degree.
  19. Thermal expansion: Increase in dimensions due to temperature rise.
  20. Thermal diffusivity: Rate at which temperature changes spread through a material.

13. Final Conceptual Summary

Mechanical Properties – How does a material behave under force?

  • Strength — Resists failure.
  • Stiffness — Resists deformation.
  • Ductility — Stretches before breaking.
  • Toughness — Absorbs energy.
  • Hardness — Resists indentation.
  • Fatigue — Resists cyclic failure.

Physical Properties – What are the physical characteristics?

  • Density
  • Specific gravity
  • Porosity
  • Electrical conductivity
  • Magnetic properties
  • Corrosion resistance

Thermal Properties – How does a material behave with heat?

  • Thermal conductivity
  • Specific heat capacity
  • Thermal expansion
  • Thermal stability
  • Thermal shock resistance
  • Thermal diffusivity

Time-dependent and surface-related behaviour

  • Fatigue → Repeated stress
  • Creep → Sustained stress over time
  • Impact → Sudden loading
  • Wear → Surface material loss
Recommendation for GME preparation: Give particular attention to stress-strain behaviour, ductile versus brittle fracture, hardness testing, Charpy and Izod impact tests, S-N curves, the three stages of creep, and the difference between fatigue and creep. These concepts are directly relevant to understanding diesel engines, crankshafts, cylinder liners, boilers, turbines and other shipboard machinery.
