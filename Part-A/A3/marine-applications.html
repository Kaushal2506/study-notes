1.1 Introduction

  • A propeller shaft is an important component of a ship's propulsion system.
  • It transmits rotational power from the main engine to the propeller.
  • The propeller converts the rotational motion of the shaft into thrust, which moves the ship forward or backward.
  • During operation, the shaft is subjected mainly to torsional loading.
  • Torsion is the twisting of a shaft due to an applied torque.

Main functions of a propeller shaft

  1. Transmit engine torque to the propeller.
  2. Transfer rotational power.
  3. Withstand torsional stresses.
  4. Withstand bending loads due to shaft weight and propeller forces.
  5. Maintain alignment between the engine and propeller.

1.2 What is torsion?

  • Torsion occurs when a component is subjected to a twisting moment or torque.
  • When torque is applied to a shaft, one end tends to rotate relative to the other end.
  • This produces shear stress inside the shaft.
  • The shear stress is zero at the centre of a solid circular shaft and maximum at its outer surface.
Think of it this way: rotate a screwdriver while its tip is held fixed. Your hand applies torque, the screwdriver twists, and internal shear stresses develop inside it. The same principle applies to a marine propeller shaft.

1.3 Torque transmitted by a propeller shaft

Torque is the turning effect of a force.

T = F × r

Where

  • T = Torque (N·m)
  • F = Applied tangential force (N)
  • r = Radius (m)

The relationship between power, torque and rotational speed is:

P = 2πNT60

Where

  • P = Power (W)
  • N = Rotational speed (RPM)
  • T = Torque (N·m)

Therefore:

T = 60P2πN

Important points

  • For the same power, reducing rotational speed increases transmitted torque.
  • Increasing transmitted power increases torque if speed remains constant.
  • Large marine diesel engines generally transmit considerable torque because of their high power output.

1.4 Torsional shear stress in a circular shaft

The torsion equation is:

TJ = τr = GθL

Where

  • T = Applied torque (N·m)
  • J = Polar moment of inertia (m⁴)
  • τ = Shear stress (Pa)
  • r = Radial distance from shaft centre (m)
  • G = Modulus of rigidity (Pa)
  • θ = Angle of twist (radians)
  • L = Length of shaft (m)

Solid circular shaft

J = π d⁴32

Maximum shear stress:

τmax = 16Tπ d³

Hollow circular shaft

J = π32(D⁴ − d⁴)

Maximum shear stress:

τmax = 16TDπ(D⁴ − d⁴)

Where

  • D = Outer diameter.
  • d = Inner diameter.

1.5 Angle of twist

  • Angle of twist is the angular deformation produced in a shaft due to torque.
  • It represents how much one end of the shaft rotates relative to the other.
θ = TLJG

Important observations

  • Increasing torque increases the angle of twist.
  • Increasing shaft length increases the angle of twist.
  • Increasing shaft diameter reduces the angle of twist.
  • Increasing the modulus of rigidity reduces the angle of twist.

1.6 Torsional vibration in propeller shafts

  • Torsional vibration is the repeated twisting and untwisting of a rotating shaft.
  • It occurs due to periodic variations in engine torque.
  • Reciprocating marine diesel engines produce fluctuating torque during different stages of the engine cycle.
  • These torque fluctuations create torsional oscillations in the crankshaft and propulsion shafting system.

Causes

  1. Periodic combustion forces.
  2. Uneven torque production in engine cylinders.
  3. Propeller blade interaction with non-uniform water flow.
  4. Sudden changes in engine speed.
  5. Starting and stopping operations.
  6. Resonance between excitation frequency and natural frequency.
What is resonance? Every shafting system has a natural frequency of torsional vibration. When the excitation frequency approaches the natural frequency, vibration amplitude can increase significantly. This condition is called resonance. Excessive torsional vibration can cause shaft failure, coupling damage and crankshaft damage.

Effects of torsional vibration

  • Increased shear stress.
  • Fatigue cracks.
  • Coupling bolt failures.
  • Damage to gears and bearings.
  • Excessive noise and vibration.
  • Shaft fracture in severe cases.

1.7 Marine applications and protection

Common components associated with the propeller shaft system:

  • Intermediate shaft.
  • Thrust shaft.
  • Propeller shaft.
  • Shaft couplings.
  • Stern tube.
  • Propeller.
  • Bearings.

Methods of preventing shaft failure

  1. Correct shaft diameter selection.
  2. Proper alignment of shafting.
  3. Installation of suitable torsional vibration dampers where required.
  4. Avoiding prolonged operation in prohibited critical speed ranges.
  5. Regular inspection of shaft surfaces.
  6. Monitoring vibration levels.
  7. Checking shaft couplings and their bolts.
  8. Proper lubrication of bearings.
Remember: Propeller shaft mainly experiences torsional shear stress. Maximum torsional shear stress occurs at the outer surface. Torsional vibration can cause fatigue failure. Excessive torque can lead to permanent deformation or shaft fracture.

2.1 Introduction

  • A marine engine foundation is the supporting structure on which the main engine is installed.
  • It transfers engine loads to the ship's supporting structure.
  • The foundation must withstand static and dynamic loads.
  • It maintains the correct alignment of the engine and propulsion shafting system.

The foundation is generally connected to the engine bedplate through holding-down bolts and chocks.

Main functions

  1. Support the weight of the engine.
  2. Distribute engine loads to the ship's hull structure.
  3. Maintain engine alignment.
  4. Resist vibration.
  5. Prevent excessive movement of the engine.
  6. Withstand dynamic forces during engine operation.

2.2 Types of loads acting on an engine foundation

A. Static loads

  • Static loads are loads that remain approximately constant with time.
  • The self-weight of the engine is the main static load.
  • The weight of attached equipment and accessories also contributes.

Examples: main engine weight, auxiliary equipment weight, bedplate weight.

B. Dynamic loads

  • Dynamic loads vary with time.
  • They occur due to the movement of engine components and changing combustion forces.

Sources include:

  1. Reciprocating motion of pistons.
  2. Rotating motion of crankshaft and flywheel.
  3. Combustion pressure.
  4. Inertia forces.
  5. Starting and stopping operations.

C. Vibrational loads

  • Engines produce continuous vibration during operation.
  • These vibrations are transmitted to the foundation.
  • If the foundation is insufficiently rigid, excessive vibration and structural damage may occur.

D. Thermal loads

  • Engine components expand when heated.
  • Temperature differences between engine parts and foundation structures can produce thermal stresses.
  • Improper allowance for thermal expansion may affect alignment.

E. Ship motion-related loads

  • Ships experience pitching, rolling, heaving and other motions.
  • These motions generate additional inertial forces on machinery and supporting structures.

2.3 Load transmission through the foundation

  1. Combustion pressure acts on the piston.
  2. Forces are transmitted through the connecting rod.
  3. The crankshaft experiences forces and torque.
  4. Engine reactions are transmitted through the bearings and bedplate.
  5. The bedplate transfers these loads to the engine foundation.
  6. The foundation distributes the loads into the ship's supporting structure.

2.4 Stresses developed in engine foundations

A. Compressive stress

Produced by the weight of the engine and other downward forces. The foundation experiences compression under supporting loads.

σc = PA

Where

  • P = Compressive load.
  • A = Cross-sectional area.

B. Bending stress

Uneven load distribution can cause bending of foundation structures. Bending may occur due to engine weight, hull deformation and dynamic loading.

σb = MyI

Where

  • M = Bending moment.
  • y = Distance from neutral axis.
  • I = Area moment of inertia.

C. Shear stress

Shear forces develop at interfaces between the engine bedplate, chocks and foundation. These forces can cause relative movement between connected components.

τ = FA

D. Fatigue stress

Repeated engine vibration produces fluctuating stresses. Over time, repeated loading can cause fatigue cracks in the foundation or its welded connections.

2.5 Engine bedplate

  • The bedplate is the main supporting structure of a large marine diesel engine.
  • It supports the crankshaft main bearings.
  • It provides a rigid base for the engine.
  • It distributes loads to the foundation.
  • It must possess sufficient stiffness to maintain crankshaft alignment.

Important components

  • Longitudinal girders.
  • Cross girders.
  • Main bearing supports.
  • Holding-down arrangements.
  • Structural connections.

2.6 Holding-down bolts

  • Holding-down bolts secure the engine to its foundation.
  • They prevent unwanted movement.
  • They maintain the correct position of the engine.
  • They may experience tensile and shear forces depending on the loading arrangement.

Improper bolt tightening may result in

  • Engine movement.
  • Misalignment.
  • Vibration.
  • Loosening of connections.
  • Damage to the foundation.

2.7 Chocks

  • Chocks are supporting elements placed between the engine bedplate and foundation.
  • They distribute the engine load.
  • They provide proper contact between the engine and foundation.
  • They help maintain the required engine alignment.

Common arrangements

  1. Metal chocks.
  2. Resin chocks.

2.9 Inspection and maintenance

  • Inspect foundation structures for visible cracks.
  • Check holding-down bolts for tightness and condition.
  • Check for corrosion.
  • Monitor engine vibration.
  • Check engine alignment according to the maintenance schedule.
  • Inspect chocks for damage or deterioration.
  • Investigate abnormal changes in crankshaft deflection readings.
Remember: Engine foundation carries static and dynamic loads. Bedplate supports the crankshaft and distributes engine loads. Holding-down bolts secure the engine. Foundation stiffness is essential for maintaining alignment. Repeated vibration can cause fatigue cracks.

3.1 Connecting rod – introduction

  • A connecting rod is a mechanical component that connects the piston or crosshead assembly to the crankshaft.
  • It transmits forces from the piston to the crankshaft.
  • It converts reciprocating motion into rotary motion through the crank mechanism.
  • In marine diesel engines, connecting rods experience complicated combinations of loading.

Main functions

  1. Transmit combustion forces.
  2. Transmit inertia forces.
  3. Transfer piston forces to the crankshaft.
  4. Withstand compression and tensile loading.
  5. Maintain the mechanical connection between moving components.

3.2 Types of loads acting on connecting rods

A. Compressive loading

  • During the power stroke, high combustion pressure pushes the piston downward.
  • This force is transmitted through the connecting rod.
  • The connecting rod experiences compressive loading.
σc = PA

B. Tensile loading

  • Inertia forces during reciprocating motion can produce tensile loading.
  • Tensile forces are particularly important during rapid changes in piston velocity and acceleration.
  • These forces depend on engine speed and reciprocating mass.
σt = PA

C. Bending loading

  • Connecting rods may experience bending due to eccentric forces.
  • Misalignment and unequal loading can also produce bending stresses.
  • Bending stress is additional to direct axial stress.
σb = MyI

D. Buckling

  • A connecting rod is generally subjected to high compressive forces.
  • If it is sufficiently slender, it may bend or buckle under compression.
  • Buckling is a sudden lateral deformation that may occur before the material reaches its compressive yield strength.

Euler's critical buckling load for an ideal slender column is:

Pcr = π²EILe²

Where

  • Pcr = Critical buckling load.
  • E = Young's modulus.
  • I = Least area moment of inertia.
  • Le = Effective length.

Effective length depends on the end-support conditions.

3.3 Combined stresses in connecting rods

Connecting rods can experience more than one type of stress simultaneously. For example:

  • Axial compression due to combustion pressure.
  • Bending due to eccentric loading.
  • Tensile force due to inertia.
  • Cyclic stress due to repeated engine operation.

For combined axial and bending loading:

σ = PA ± MyI

Where

  • P/A = Direct axial stress.
  • My/I = Bending stress.

One side of the rod may experience higher compression, while the opposite side may experience reduced compression or tension.

3.4 Connecting rod failure

Common failure modes

  1. Buckling due to excessive compression.
  2. Fatigue cracking due to cyclic loading.
  3. Cracking around the big-end and small-end regions.
  4. Bearing failure due to inadequate lubrication.
  5. Deformation due to overload.
  6. Failure due to manufacturing defects.

Causes of failure

  • Excessive combustion pressure.
  • Engine overspeed.
  • Lubrication failure.
  • Misalignment.
  • Excessive vibration.
  • Material defects.
  • Improper maintenance.

3.5 Crankshaft – introduction

  • A crankshaft is a rotating shaft used to convert reciprocating piston motion into rotary motion.
  • It receives forces from the connecting rods.
  • It transmits mechanical power to the flywheel and propulsion system.
  • It is one of the most highly stressed components of a marine diesel engine.

Main parts of a crankshaft

  1. Main journals.
  2. Crankpins.
  3. Crank webs.
  4. Fillets.
  5. Thrust collar, where provided.
  6. Oil passages.

Main journal

  • The portion of the crankshaft supported by the main bearings.
  • It provides support and allows crankshaft rotation.

Crankpin

  • The portion connected to the big end of the connecting rod.
  • It receives the connecting rod force.

Crank web

  • Connects the main journal to the crankpin.
  • Transmits forces between the crankpin and main journal.

Fillet

  • A rounded transition between adjoining crankshaft sections.
  • It reduces stress concentration.

3.6 Stresses acting on a crankshaft

A crankshaft is subjected to several stresses simultaneously.

A. Bending stress

  • Connecting rod forces act on the crankpin.
  • These forces produce bending moments in the crankshaft.
  • The crankshaft bends under the applied loads.
σb = MyI

B. Torsional shear stress

  • The crankshaft transmits engine torque.
  • This produces torsional shear stress.

For a solid circular section:

τmax = 16Tπ d³

C. Shear stress

  • Transverse forces produce shear forces in the crank webs and shaft sections.
  • These forces contribute to the overall stress condition.

D. Fatigue stress

  • The crankshaft experiences repeated loading during every engine cycle.
  • Bending and torsional stresses fluctuate during operation.
  • This makes fatigue resistance extremely important.

E. Stress concentration

  • Crankshaft geometry includes changes in cross-section.
  • Sharp corners and abrupt changes in diameter increase local stresses.
  • Crankpin fillets and journal fillets are particularly important locations.

3.7 Stress concentration in crankshafts

  • Stress concentration occurs when local stress becomes higher than the nominal stress.
  • It is commonly caused by sudden changes in geometry.

Common stress concentration locations

  1. Crankpin fillets.
  2. Main journal fillets.
  3. Oil holes.
  4. Keyways.
  5. Changes in shaft diameter.

Stress concentration factor:

Kt = σmaxσnominal

Where

  • Kt = Theoretical stress concentration factor.
  • σmax = Maximum local stress.
  • σnominal = Nominal stress.

Methods of reducing stress concentration

  • Providing suitable fillet radii.
  • Avoiding sharp corners.
  • Using smooth transitions in diameter.
  • Proper surface finishing.
  • Correctly positioning oil holes.
  • Selecting suitable materials and heat treatments.

3.8 Crankshaft failure

Common failure modes

  • Fatigue cracks at crankpin fillets.
  • Cracks near oil holes.
  • Crankshaft bending or distortion.
  • Torsional fatigue failure.
  • Journal wear due to bearing lubrication problems.
  • Overheating and seizure.

Factors responsible

  1. Excessive combustion pressure.
  2. Incorrect crankshaft alignment.
  3. Bearing damage.
  4. Insufficient lubrication.
  5. Torsional vibration.
  6. Excessive engine speed.
  7. Stress concentration.
  8. Repeated fluctuating loads.

3.9 Crankshaft inspection

  • Check crankshaft deflection.
  • Inspect crankpin and main journal surfaces.
  • Check bearing condition.
  • Inspect fillet regions for cracks.
  • Use suitable non-destructive testing (NDT) methods.
  • Monitor lubrication oil condition.
  • Investigate unusual vibration and knocking sounds.

4.1 Introduction

  • Bolted joints are detachable mechanical connections used to join two or more components.
  • They are widely used in marine machinery.
  • Bolts provide clamping force that holds connected components together.
  • They allow dismantling during inspection, repair and maintenance.

Marine applications

  1. Engine foundation connections.
  2. Propeller shaft couplings.
  3. Cylinder covers.
  4. Machinery mounting.
  5. Flanges and pipe connections.
  6. Pumps and compressors.
  7. Gearbox connections.

4.2 Types of forces acting on bolts

A. Tensile load

  • Tensile load acts along the longitudinal axis of the bolt.
  • It attempts to stretch the bolt.
  • Excessive tensile loading can cause bolt elongation or fracture.
σt = PAt

Where

  • P = Tensile load.
  • At = Tensile stress area of bolt.

B. Shear load

  • Shear force acts perpendicular to the bolt axis.
  • It attempts to slide one connected component relative to another.
  • Excessive shear stress may cause the bolt to shear off.
τ = FA

C. Bending load

  • Improper fitting or uneven contact can cause bending in a bolt.
  • Bending may also develop when connected components separate unevenly or when loads act eccentrically.

D. Combined loading

  • Bolts may experience tensile and shear stresses simultaneously.
  • This is common in machinery connections subjected to vibration and fluctuating forces.

4.3 Bolt preload

  • Preload is the initial tensile force introduced into a bolt when it is tightened.
  • Tightening stretches the bolt slightly.
  • The stretched bolt produces a clamping force that holds the connected components together.

Importance of preload

  1. Prevents separation of connected components.
  2. Reduces relative movement.
  3. Improves joint stiffness.
  4. Reduces the risk of loosening.
  5. Helps distribute external loads.

A properly designed bolted joint is generally tightened to a specified preload.

4.4 Common types of bolted joint failure

1. Tensile failure

  • Occurs when the tensile stress exceeds the bolt's strength.
  • The bolt stretches excessively and may fracture.
  • It may happen due to excessive tightening or external tensile loads.

2. Shear failure

  • Occurs when the applied shear force exceeds the bolt's shear capacity.
  • The bolt may break across its cross-section.

3. Fatigue failure

  • Occurs due to repeated or fluctuating stresses.
  • Small cracks gradually develop and propagate.
  • The bolt may eventually fracture even when the maximum load is below its static strength.

4. Thread stripping

  • Occurs when the threads of the bolt or nut are damaged.
  • Excessive loading or insufficient thread engagement may cause thread failure.
  • The bolt may pull out of the nut without the bolt shank itself breaking.

5. Loosening

  • Occurs when a bolted connection gradually loses its clamping force.
  • Vibration and repeated transverse movement are important causes.
  • Loosening may result in leakage, movement or eventual joint failure.

6. Corrosion failure

  • Marine environments expose bolts to moisture, salt and corrosive conditions.
  • Corrosion reduces the effective cross-sectional area.
  • Pitting corrosion creates local stress concentration.
  • Corrosion can accelerate fatigue failure.

7. Over-tightening failure

  • Excessive tightening introduces excessive tensile stress.
  • The bolt may yield, permanently elongate or fracture.
  • Threads may also be damaged.

4.5 Causes of bolted joint failure

  • Incorrect bolt selection.
  • Improper tightening torque.
  • Insufficient preload.
  • Excessive preload.
  • Vibration.
  • Corrosion.
  • Poor thread engagement.
  • Incorrect installation.
  • Repeated fluctuating loading.
  • Damaged threads.
  • Uneven contact surfaces.

4.6 Stress concentration in bolts

  • Threads create changes in cross-sectional geometry.
  • The thread roots are regions of stress concentration.
  • Fatigue cracks commonly initiate near thread roots.
  • The first engaged threads may carry a relatively large proportion of the applied load.

This is why the thread-root geometry, bolt material and tightening procedure are important.

4.7 Prevention of bolted joint failures

  1. Use bolts of the correct grade and size.
  2. Follow the specified tightening torque or preload.
  3. Use suitable locking arrangements where specified.
  4. Maintain proper thread engagement.
  5. Avoid excessive tightening.
  6. Inspect bolts for corrosion.
  7. Replace damaged bolts.
  8. Follow the manufacturer's maintenance instructions.
  9. Use suitable corrosion-resistant materials or protective coatings.
  10. Check critical bolted connections periodically.
Important marine engineering example: Propeller shaft coupling bolts connect adjacent shaft sections. If coupling bolts become loose: relative movement may occur between flanges, uneven loading develops, vibration increases, bolt fatigue may occur, and in severe cases the connection can fail.
Remember: Tensile failure occurs due to excessive axial loading. Shear failure occurs due to transverse loading. Fatigue failure occurs due to repeated loading. Thread stripping occurs due to thread damage or overload. Vibration and corrosion are major concerns in marine bolted joints.

5.1 Introduction to fatigue

  • Fatigue is the progressive damage of a material caused by repeated or fluctuating loading.
  • It can lead to crack initiation and eventual fracture.
  • Fatigue failure may occur even when the applied stress is below the material's yield strength.
  • It is one of the most important failure mechanisms in rotating marine machinery.

Examples of marine components susceptible to fatigue

  1. Crankshafts.
  2. Propeller shafts.
  3. Connecting rods.
  4. Gear teeth.
  5. Engine foundation structures.
  6. Bolts and fasteners.
  7. Pump shafts.
  8. Compressor components.
  9. Propeller blades.

5.2 Why fatigue occurs in marine machinery

Marine machinery operates under repeated loading conditions. Examples:

  • A crankshaft experiences repeated combustion and inertia forces.
  • A propeller shaft experiences fluctuating torque.
  • A connecting rod experiences repeated compression and tension.
  • Bolts experience cyclic loading and vibration.
  • Propeller blades experience repeated hydrodynamic loading.

These repeated stresses can gradually damage the material.

5.3 Stages of fatigue failure

Fatigue failure generally occurs in three stages.

Stage 1: Crack initiation

  • A small crack begins to form in the material.
  • It often starts at the surface.
  • Surface scratches, machining marks, corrosion pits and stress concentration points can initiate cracks.
  • Repeated loading causes microscopic damage.

Common crack initiation locations:

  • Shaft fillets.
  • Thread roots.
  • Keyways.
  • Oil holes.
  • Weld toes.
  • Corrosion pits.

Stage 2: Crack propagation

  • The crack gradually grows with repeated loading.
  • Each loading cycle may extend the crack slightly.
  • The crack reduces the effective load-bearing cross-section.
  • Stress in the remaining material increases.

This stage may produce visible fatigue markings.

Stage 3: Final fracture

  • Eventually, the remaining cross-section cannot withstand the applied load.
  • Sudden fracture occurs.
  • The final fracture region is often relatively rough compared with the fatigue propagation region.

5.4 Types of fatigue loading

A. Completely reversed loading

  • Stress alternates between equal tensile and compressive values.
  • The mean stress is zero.
σmax = +σ,    σmin = −σ

B. Repeated or pulsating loading

  • Stress varies from zero to a maximum value of the same sign.
  • The component experiences repeated loading and unloading.

C. Fluctuating loading

  • Stress varies between minimum and maximum values.
  • Both values may be tensile or may involve tension and compression.

5.5 Important fatigue terms

Maximum stress

σmax

The highest stress experienced during one loading cycle.

Minimum stress

σmin

The lowest stress experienced during one loading cycle.

Mean stress

σm = σmax + σmin2

Mean stress is the average stress during a loading cycle.

Stress amplitude

σa = σmax − σmin2

Stress amplitude is half the difference between maximum and minimum stress. It represents the magnitude of stress fluctuation.

Stress range

Δσ = σmax − σmin

Stress ratio

R = σminσmax

5.6 S-N curve (Wöhler curve)

  • An S-N curve represents the relationship between cyclic stress amplitude (S) and the number of cycles to failure (N).
  • It is used to understand the fatigue behaviour of materials.
  • It helps engineers estimate fatigue life under specified loading conditions.

Important observations

  1. Higher cyclic stress generally results in fewer cycles to failure.
  2. Lower cyclic stress generally results in longer fatigue life.
  3. Fatigue life depends on material properties and operating conditions.
  4. Surface finish and stress concentration affect fatigue strength.
Endurance limit: The endurance limit is the stress amplitude below which certain materials can withstand a very large number of cycles without fatigue failure under specified conditions. Some steels exhibit an approximately horizontal region in their S-N curve. Aluminium alloys generally do not exhibit a clear endurance limit. For materials without a true endurance limit, fatigue strength is specified at a particular number of cycles.

5.7 Factors affecting fatigue strength

1. Surface finish

  • Rough surfaces contain scratches and irregularities.
  • These irregularities act as stress concentration points.
  • A smoother surface generally improves fatigue strength.

2. Stress concentration

  • Sharp corners and sudden changes in geometry increase local stress.
  • Cracks often initiate at these locations.
  • Fillets and smooth transitions help reduce stress concentration.

3. Corrosion

  • Seawater and moisture cause corrosion.
  • Corrosion pits act as crack initiation points.
  • Combined corrosion and cyclic loading can cause corrosion fatigue.

4. Temperature

  • High operating temperatures may reduce material strength.
  • Temperature variations may produce thermal stresses.
  • These conditions can influence fatigue behaviour.

5. Material properties

  • Material composition and heat treatment influence fatigue resistance.
  • Higher strength alone does not always guarantee better fatigue performance.
  • Ductility, toughness and microstructure are also important.

6. Residual stresses

  • Residual stresses are stresses remaining in a material after manufacturing or processing.
  • Tensile residual stresses near the surface can reduce fatigue resistance.
  • Suitable compressive residual stresses can improve fatigue performance.

7. Size effect

  • Larger components may have a greater probability of containing defects.
  • Stress distribution and surface-to-volume effects influence fatigue strength.

8. Manufacturing defects

  • Inclusions.
  • Porosity.
  • Improper welding.
  • Surface cracks.
  • Machining defects.

These defects may initiate fatigue cracks.

5.8 Corrosion fatigue in marine machinery

  • Corrosion fatigue is the combined effect of cyclic loading and corrosive environmental conditions.
  • Marine components are frequently exposed to seawater, moisture and salt.
  • Corrosion damages the protective surface of materials.
  • Corrosion pits create local stress concentrations.
  • Repeated loading causes cracks to initiate and propagate.

Examples

  • Propeller shafts.
  • Propeller blades.
  • Seawater pump shafts.
  • Marine fasteners.
  • Ship structural components.

Prevention

  1. Use suitable corrosion-resistant materials.
  2. Apply protective coatings where appropriate.
  3. Maintain effective corrosion protection systems.
  4. Inspect seawater-exposed components.
  5. Avoid surface damage.
  6. Monitor cracks and corrosion pits.

5.9 Fatigue fracture appearance

A fatigue fracture surface may show:

A. Beach marks

  • Macroscopic curved markings on the fracture surface.
  • They indicate successive stages of crack growth.
  • They are not necessarily produced by every individual loading cycle.

B. Striations

  • Very fine microscopic markings.
  • They may represent successive stages of fatigue crack advancement.
  • They are generally observed using a scanning electron microscope.

C. Final fracture region

  • The region where the remaining material suddenly breaks.
  • It may show ductile dimples or other overload fracture features, depending on the material and loading conditions.

5.10 Fatigue failure prevention

  • Avoid sharp corners and sudden changes in cross-section.
  • Provide suitable fillet radii.
  • Improve surface finish.
  • Select materials with suitable fatigue resistance.
  • Avoid excessive vibration.
  • Maintain correct shaft alignment.
  • Prevent corrosion.
  • Avoid overloading.
  • Perform periodic inspection.
  • Use non-destructive testing (NDT) where required.

5.11 Non-destructive testing (NDT) for fatigue crack detection

NDT methods help detect defects without destroying the component.

1. Dye Penetrant Testing (DPT)

  • A coloured or fluorescent liquid penetrant is applied to the surface.
  • The penetrant enters surface-breaking cracks.
  • Excess penetrant is removed.
  • A developer helps reveal the cracks.

Application: surface crack detection in non-porous materials.

2. Magnetic Particle Testing (MPT)

  • The component is magnetised.
  • Magnetic particles accumulate around surface and near-surface discontinuities.
  • This reveals cracks.

Application: ferromagnetic materials such as suitable steels.

3. Ultrasonic Testing (UT)

  • High-frequency sound waves are introduced into the component.
  • Reflected waves help identify internal defects.
  • It is useful for detecting internal cracks and discontinuities.

Application: crankshafts and other critical machinery components, using an appropriate inspection procedure.

4. Radiographic Testing (RT)

  • X-rays or gamma rays pass through the material.
  • Internal defects create variations in the resulting image.
  • It is commonly used for weld inspection.

5. Visual Inspection (VT)

  • Direct observation of component surfaces.
  • It can identify visible cracks, corrosion, deformation and damage.
  • Magnification and suitable lighting may improve inspection.
Remember: Fatigue occurs due to repeated or fluctuating loading. Fatigue failure can occur below yield strength. Fatigue failure has three main stages: initiation, propagation and final fracture. Corrosion and stress concentration accelerate fatigue damage. S-N curves are used to understand fatigue life. NDT helps identify cracks before catastrophic failure.

6.3 Important Concepts to Remember for GME

Propeller shaft torsion

  1. Propeller shafts transmit power from the main engine to the propeller.
  2. Torsion produces shear stress.
  3. Maximum shear stress occurs at the outer surface of a solid circular shaft.
  4. At constant power, torque increases when rotational speed decreases.
  5. Torsional vibration results from fluctuating torque.
  6. Resonance can cause excessive vibration and shaft damage.

Engine foundation loading

  1. Engine foundations support the weight of the engine.
  2. Foundations experience static and dynamic loading.
  3. The bedplate distributes loads to the foundation.
  4. Holding-down bolts secure the engine.
  5. Chocks support the engine and help maintain alignment.
  6. Excessive vibration may cause fatigue cracks.

Connecting rod and crankshaft

  1. Connecting rods transmit forces between the piston assembly and crankshaft.
  2. Connecting rods experience compression, tension and bending.
  3. Buckling is an important failure mode of connecting rods.
  4. Crankshafts experience combined bending and torsional stresses.
  5. Crankpin fillets are important stress concentration locations.
  6. Proper lubrication is essential for crankshaft and bearing reliability.

Bolted joint failures

  1. Bolted joints are detachable connections.
  2. Preload creates clamping force.
  3. Bolts may fail in tension, shear or fatigue.
  4. Thread roots are important stress concentration locations.
  5. Vibration can cause joint loosening.
  6. Corrosion reduces the effective bolt cross-section.

Fatigue cracks

  1. Fatigue occurs due to repeated or fluctuating loading.
  2. Failure can occur below yield strength.
  3. Fatigue consists of crack initiation, propagation and final fracture.
  4. Corrosion accelerates fatigue damage.
  5. Stress concentration reduces fatigue life.
  6. NDT methods help identify cracks before failure.

A marine engineer should not only understand the theoretical formulas but also recognise how these failures develop in actual machinery.

Important engineering principle: Never assume that a component is safe simply because it has not reached its yield strength. Repeated loading, vibration, corrosion and stress concentration can cause failure over time. For marine engineering, understanding the relationship between loading, stress, deformation, fatigue and failure is essential for maintaining safe and reliable machinery operation.

6.5 Final Revision – Five Most Important Takeaways

Five takeaways

  1. Propeller shaft = Torsion. Transmits engine power to the propeller and experiences torsional shear stress.
  2. Engine foundation = Static + Dynamic loads. Supports engine weight and withstands vibration, inertia and combustion-related forces.
  3. Connecting rod = Compression + Buckling. Transmits piston forces and is subjected to compression, tension and bending.
  4. Crankshaft = Bending + Torsion. Converts reciprocating motion into rotary motion and experiences combined stresses.
  5. Fatigue = Repeated loading. Causes crack initiation, propagation and eventual fracture, sometimes below yield strength.
My recommendation for your GME preparation: Give special attention to torsion equations, crankshaft combined stresses, connecting rod buckling, bolt preload, fatigue failure stages, S-N curves and NDT methods. These concepts connect fundamental Strength of Materials theory directly to the machinery you will study during marine engineering training.
