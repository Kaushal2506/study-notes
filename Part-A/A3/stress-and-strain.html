Strength of Materials – Stress and Strain

Mechanical Engineering Fundamentals

Subject: Strength of Materials Level: Mechanical / Marine Engineering

These notes cover stress, strain, elastic and plastic deformation, Hooke's law, elastic moduli, Poisson's ratio and stress–strain diagrams – with marine engineering applications and quick-revision formulas.

Stress is the internal resisting force developed per unit area inside a material when an external force is applied. Stress is classified according to the direction in which the force acts relative to the cross-sectional area.

σ = PA

σ = normal stress (N/m² or Pa) · P = axial force (N) · A = cross-sectional area (m²)

Important: 1 MPa = 1 N/mm²  ·  1 GPa = 10³ MPa  ·  1 kPa = 10³ Pa

1.1 Tensile Stress

  • Tensile stress develops when an external pulling force acts on a material.
  • It tends to stretch or elongate the material.
  • It acts perpendicular (normal) to the cross-sectional area.
  • It is also called normal tensile stress.
σt = PA

Characteristics

  • Produces elongation.
  • Acts in the direction of pulling.
  • Associated with positive longitudinal strain.
  • If tensile stress exceeds the material's strength, permanent deformation or fracture may occur.

Practical applications

  • Ship mooring ropes under tension.
  • Crane lifting cables.
  • Tie rods and suspension members.
  • Bolts subjected to pulling forces.

1.2 Compressive Stress

  • Compressive stress develops when an external pushing force acts on a material.
  • It tends to shorten or compress the material.
  • It acts perpendicular to the cross-sectional area.
  • It is also called normal compressive stress.
σc = PA

Characteristics

  • Produces shortening.
  • Acts inward towards the material.
  • Associated with negative longitudinal strain.
  • Slender components under compression may fail due to buckling.

Practical applications

  • Ship structural columns.
  • Engine foundation supports.
  • Compression members in ship structures.
  • Hydraulic press components.
  • Supporting pillars and machine frames.
Buckling: A sudden sideways deflection of a slender structural member subjected to compressive load. It may occur even when the compressive stress is below the material's yield strength. It depends on the member's length, cross-section, material stiffness and end conditions.

1.3 Shear Stress

  • Shear stress develops when forces act parallel or tangential to the cross-sectional area.
  • It tends to make one layer of the material slide relative to another layer.
  • Unlike tensile and compressive stress, shear stress acts parallel to the resisting area.
τ = FA

Characteristics

  • Produces angular deformation.
  • Causes sliding between adjacent layers.
  • Represented by the Greek letter tau (τ).
  • Shear strain is represented by the Greek letter gamma (γ).

Practical applications

  • Riveted joints and bolted connections.
  • Bolts and pins.
  • Ship propeller shaft connections.
  • Punching and shearing operations.
  • Keys and couplings used in machinery.

1.4 Difference Between Tensile, Compressive and Shear Stress

Remember: Pulling = Tensile stress. Pushing = Compressive stress. Sliding = Shear stress.

When a material is subjected to an external force, it changes its shape or dimensions. This change is called deformation, and it is mainly classified into elastic and plastic deformation.

2.1 Elastic Deformation

  • Temporary change in shape or dimensions under an applied load.
  • When the load is removed, the material returns to its original shape, provided the elastic limit is not exceeded.
  • It occurs within the elastic region of the material.
  • It is generally reversible; stress and strain are proportional in the linear elastic region.

Characteristics

  • Temporary deformation.
  • Material returns to original dimensions after unloading.
  • No permanent deformation remains.
  • Governed by elastic properties such as Young's modulus.
  • Important in components designed to operate repeatedly under loads.

Examples

  • A spring stretched within its elastic limit.
  • A steel shaft subjected to a small torque.
  • A metal rod subjected to a small tensile load.
  • A ship's structural member undergoing small recoverable deflection.

2.2 Plastic Deformation

  • Permanent change in shape or dimensions after the applied load is removed.
  • Occurs when stress exceeds the elastic limit (when the material yields).
  • The material does not return completely to its original dimensions after unloading.
  • It is irreversible and involves permanent rearrangement of the internal structure.

Characteristics

  • Permanent deformation.
  • Occurs beyond the elastic limit.
  • Material retains some deformation after unloading.
  • Stress and strain are no longer generally proportional.
  • Excessive plastic deformation may make a component unsuitable for service.

Examples

  • A metal rod permanently stretched by excessive pulling force.
  • A steel plate permanently bent during an overload.
  • A metal component deformed during a forming operation.

2.3 Elastic Limit

  • The maximum stress a material can withstand and still return completely to its original dimensions after removal of the load.
  • Beyond this limit, permanent deformation begins.
  • It separates elastic behaviour from plastic behaviour in an idealized description.

2.4 Yield Point and Yield Strength

  • Yielding is the beginning of significant plastic deformation.
  • Yield strength is the stress at which a material begins to undergo plastic deformation according to a specified test definition.
  • Some materials, particularly mild steel, show a distinct upper and lower yield point.
  • Materials without a clear yield point commonly use the 0.2% proof stress method.
0.2% Proof Stress: The stress that produces a permanent strain of 0.2% after unloading. It is commonly used to define the yield strength of materials without a distinct yield point.

Important for GME: Elastic deformation is desirable during normal operation of ship machinery and structures. Plastic deformation indicates that the material has experienced loading beyond its elastic range. Excessive plastic deformation may lead to component failure.

3.1 Definition

  • Within the proportional limit, stress is directly proportional to strain, provided temperature and other physical conditions remain constant.
  • It explains the linear relationship between stress and strain in the initial elastic region of many engineering materials.
σ ∝ ε
σ = E ε
E = σε

σ = normal stress · E = Young's modulus · ε = longitudinal strain

3.2 Explanation of Hooke's Law

  • When a small tensile force is applied to a metal rod, it elongates.
  • If the applied force increases, the elongation also increases.
  • Within the proportional limit, doubling the force approximately doubles the elongation.
  • When the force is removed, the rod returns to its original length if it remains within the elastic range.

3.3 Hooke's Law for Springs

F = k x

F = applied force (N) · k = spring stiffness / spring constant (N/m) · x = extension or compression (m)

Important: A higher spring constant means a stiffer spring. A lower spring constant means a softer spring. Hooke's law for a spring applies within its linear operating range.

3.4 Proportional Limit

  • The maximum stress up to which stress is directly proportional to strain.
  • Below this limit, the stress–strain graph is a straight line.
  • Beyond this limit, the relationship may become nonlinear.

3.5 Limitations of Hooke's Law

  • It is valid only within the proportional region.
  • It does not accurately describe plastic deformation.
  • It may not apply to nonlinear materials such as rubber over large deformation ranges.
  • It assumes the material's physical conditions remain constant.
  • It is primarily used for small deformations in conventional linear-elastic analysis.
Stress = Young's Modulus × Strain

Elastic moduli are material properties that describe resistance to deformation. A higher modulus generally indicates greater stiffness – more stress is required to produce a given strain.

4.1 Young's Modulus (E)

  • Ratio of normal stress to longitudinal strain within the linear elastic region.
  • Measures the stiffness of a material under tensile or compressive loading.
E = σε
σ = PA,   ε = ΔLL
E = P LA ΔL

P = axial load · L = original length · A = cross-sectional area · ΔL = change in length

Typical Young's Modulus Values

MaterialApproximate Young's modulus
Mild steel200 GPa
Stainless steel190–200 GPa
Cast iron100–170 GPa
Aluminium69 GPa
Copper110–130 GPa
Brass90–110 GPa
RubberMuch lower; strongly dependent on composition
Important points: Young's modulus measures stiffness, not strength. Steel is generally stiffer than aluminium because its Young's modulus is higher. A material with a high Young's modulus undergoes less elastic strain under the same stress. Young's modulus is approximately independent of specimen dimensions for a given material.

4.2 Shear Modulus (G)

  • Ratio of shear stress to shear strain within the linear elastic region.
  • Measures resistance to angular deformation caused by shear forces.
  • Also known as the modulus of rigidity.
G = τγ
γ = tan θ ≈ θ

G = shear modulus · τ = shear stress · γ = shear strain · θ = angular deformation in radians

Practical marine engineering applications

  • Marine engine crankshafts.
  • Propeller shafts.
  • Gearbox transmission components.
  • Torsionally loaded machine components.

4.3 Bulk Modulus (K)

  • Ratio of hydrostatic pressure change to the corresponding volumetric strain.
  • Measures resistance of a material to uniform compression.
  • Indicates how difficult it is to change the volume of a material.
K = − ΔpΔV / V
K = Pressure increaseVolumetric strain magnitude

The negative sign indicates that an increase in pressure produces a decrease in volume.

Marine engineering applications

  • Hydraulic systems.
  • Hydraulic steering systems.
  • Fuel injection systems.
  • Lubrication systems.
  • Pressurized pipelines and fluid systems.

Easy way to remember: Young's modulus → change in length. Shear modulus → change in angle. Bulk modulus → change in volume.

4.5 Relationship Between Elastic Moduli

For a homogeneous, isotropic, linearly elastic material:

E = 2G (1 + μ)
E = 3K (1 − 2μ)
E = 9 K G3K + G
Conditions: These relationships apply to homogeneous, isotropic, linear-elastic materials. They are not universally valid for anisotropic materials or nonlinear material behaviour.

5.1 Definition

  • When a material is stretched in one direction, its length increases and its lateral dimensions generally decrease.
  • When compressed longitudinally, its length decreases and lateral dimensions generally increase.
  • Poisson's ratio describes the relationship between lateral strain and longitudinal strain.
μ = − Lateral strainLongitudinal strain
μ = − εlateralεlongitudinal

5.2 Explanation

  • Consider a cylindrical steel rod under tension: its length increases and its diameter decreases.
  • The increase in length produces longitudinal tensile strain.
  • The decrease in diameter produces lateral compressive strain.
  • The negative sign is used because longitudinal and lateral strains generally have opposite signs.

5.3 Typical Values of Poisson's Ratio

MaterialApproximate Poisson's ratio
Steel0.27–0.30
Aluminium0.32–0.35
Copper0.33–0.35
Brass0.33–0.35
Concrete0.15–0.25
RubberClose to 0.5
Important points: Poisson's ratio is dimensionless. For most common engineering metals it lies between 0.25 and 0.35. For an ideal incompressible isotropic elastic material it is 0.5. A value close to 0.5 indicates very little volume change under uniaxial loading. Some auxetic materials have negative Poisson's ratios.

5.4 Relationship Between Poisson's Ratio and Elastic Moduli

For isotropic, linear-elastic materials:

E = 2G (1 + μ)  →  μ = E2G − 1
E = 3K (1 − 2μ)  →  μ = 12 − E6K
Remember: Poisson's ratio describes how much a material contracts sideways when stretched lengthwise.

6.1 What is a Stress–Strain Diagram?

  • A graphical representation of the relationship between stress and strain in a material.
  • Obtained by conducting a tensile test on a standard specimen using a Universal Testing Machine (UTM).
  • Helps engineers understand mechanical properties and behaviour under loading.
  • Vertical axis: Stress; Horizontal axis: Strain.

6.2 Tensile Test Procedure

  1. A standard test specimen is prepared with a known original cross-sectional area and gauge length.
  2. The specimen is placed between the grips of a Universal Testing Machine.
  3. A gradually increasing tensile load is applied.
  4. The applied load and corresponding elongation are measured.
  5. Stress is calculated using the original cross-sectional area; engineering strain uses the original gauge length.
  6. The values are plotted to obtain the stress–strain curve.
σ = PA0    ε = L − L0L0

P = applied tensile load · A0 = original area · L0 = original gauge length · L = current gauge length

6.3 Regions and Points of the Mild Steel Stress–Strain Diagram

Region 1: Proportional Limit

  • The initial portion of the curve is a straight line; stress is directly proportional to strain.
  • Hooke's law is valid and the slope represents Young's modulus.

Region 2: Elastic Limit

  • The material remains elastic up to this limit and returns to original dimensions after unloading.
  • Beyond this, permanent deformation may occur. Proportional and elastic limits are close but not necessarily identical.

Region 3: Upper Yield Point

  • Mild steel begins to yield; a relatively high stress is required to initiate yielding.
  • The stress reaches the upper yield point and then drops.

Region 4: Lower Yield Point

  • After the upper yield point, stress decreases to the lower yield point.
  • Plastic deformation continues with relatively little change in stress; characteristic of mild steel.

Region 5: Yield Plateau

  • Considerable plastic deformation with strain increasing significantly while stress remains approximately constant.

Region 6: Strain Hardening Region

  • Additional stress is required to continue plastic deformation – the material becomes stronger.
  • Dislocations within the crystal structure interact and impede further deformation.

Region 7: Ultimate Tensile Strength (UTS)

UTS = PmaxA0
  • The maximum engineering stress reached during the tensile test – the highest point on the curve.
  • Beyond this point, localized necking generally begins in a ductile tensile specimen.

Region 8: Necking Region

  • Localized reduction in cross-sectional area – deformation concentrates in a small region.
  • Engineering stress decreases after UTS and the specimen becomes progressively thinner at the neck.

Region 9: Fracture Point

  • Final breaking of the specimen into two or more pieces.
  • The stress at fracture is called fracture stress; no further tensile loading is possible.

6.4 Important Terms

  • Proportional Limit: Maximum stress up to which stress is directly proportional to strain (end of linear portion).
  • Elastic Limit: Maximum stress up to which the material can fully recover its original dimensions after unloading.
  • Yield Strength: Stress at which significant plastic deformation begins (0.2% proof stress if no distinct yield point).
  • Ultimate Tensile Strength (UTS): Maximum engineering stress reached during a tensile test.
  • Breaking Stress: Engineering stress at which the specimen fractures.
  • Modulus of Elasticity: Slope of the initial straight-line portion of the stress–strain curve; indicates stiffness.
  • Resilience: Ability to absorb energy during elastic deformation and release it when unloaded.
  • Toughness: Ability to absorb energy before fracturing (area under curve up to fracture).
  • Ductility: Ability to undergo considerable plastic deformation before fracture.
  • Brittleness: Tendency to fracture with little plastic deformation.
Ur = σy22E

Ur = modulus of resilience · σy = yield stress · E = Young's modulus

7.3 Important Points to Remember for GME

  1. Stress is internal resisting force per unit area.
  2. Strain is the ratio of change in dimension to original dimension.
  3. Tensile stress causes elongation; compressive stress causes shortening.
  4. Shear stress causes sliding between adjacent layers.
  5. Elastic deformation is temporary; plastic deformation is permanent.
  6. Hooke's law states stress is proportional to strain within the proportional limit.
  7. Young's modulus measures resistance to longitudinal deformation.
  8. Shear modulus is also called modulus of rigidity.
  9. Bulk modulus measures resistance to volume change.
  10. Poisson's ratio is the negative ratio of lateral strain to longitudinal strain.
  11. Young's modulus is the slope of the initial linear portion of the stress–strain curve.
  12. Yield strength indicates the beginning of significant plastic deformation.
  13. UTS is the maximum engineering stress.
  14. Necking is the localized reduction in cross-sectional area.
  15. Fracture is the final breaking of the specimen.
  16. Ductile materials undergo significant plastic deformation before fracture.
  17. Brittle materials fracture with little plastic deformation.
  18. Resilience is the ability to absorb energy within the elastic range.
  19. Toughness is the ability to absorb energy up to fracture.
  20. Fatigue failure occurs under repeated or fluctuating loading.
  21. Buckling is a common failure mode of slender compression members.
  22. Stress concentration occurs near holes, notches and sharp corners.
  23. Factor of Safety provides a margin between material strength and allowable working stress.

Practical understanding

  • A marine propeller shaft transmits torque from the engine to the propeller, producing shear stress.
  • A connecting rod experiences alternating tensile and compressive loading during engine operation.
  • A ship's hull experiences bending and shear due to waves, cargo distribution and buoyancy forces.
  • A marine boiler experiences stresses due to internal steam pressure and temperature variations.
  • Mooring ropes experience tensile stress while securing a ship to a berth.
  • Repeated vibration and cyclic loading can produce fatigue cracks in rotating machinery.

7.5 Concept Map — Stress and Strain

STRESS AND STRAIN

Behaviour of materials under external loading

STRESS: Internal resisting force per unit area — Tensile · Compressive · Shear

STRAIN: Change in dimension / Original dimension — Longitudinal · Lateral · Shear · Volumetric

ELASTICITY AND PLASTICITY: Elastic → temporary; Plastic → permanent; Hooke's Law → stress ∝ strain

ELASTIC CONSTANTS: Young's modulus (E) → length; Shear modulus (G) → angle; Bulk modulus (K) → volume; Poisson's ratio (μ) → lateral vs longitudinal strain

STRESS–STRAIN DIAGRAM: Proportional limit → Elastic limit → Yielding → Strain hardening → UTS → Necking → Fracture

My recommendation for your GME preparation: Give special attention to the stress–strain diagram, Hooke's law, elastic constants and Poisson's ratio. These concepts form the foundation for understanding torsion of shafts, bending of beams, pressure vessels, fatigue and failure of marine machinery components.
