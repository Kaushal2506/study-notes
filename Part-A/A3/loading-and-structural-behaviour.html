1.1 Introduction to Beams

  • A beam is a structural member designed primarily to carry loads acting perpendicular to its longitudinal axis.
  • When a beam carries loads, it develops internal resisting forces and moments.
  • These internal forces prevent the beam from collapsing or changing shape excessively.
  • The two important internal actions developed in beams are: Shear force (SF) and Bending moment (BM).

Examples of beams

  • Ship decks and supporting girders.
  • Engine room structural supports.
  • Crane booms.
  • Bridge girders.
  • Machine frames.
  • Structural members supporting heavy equipment.

1.2 Types of Beam Supports

The support conditions determine how a beam behaves under loading.

1. Simply Supported Beam

  • Supported at two ends.
  • One end generally has a hinged or pin support.
  • Other end has a roller support.
  • Allows rotation at supports.
  • Prevents vertical movement at supports.
  • Commonly used in bridges and structural frameworks.

2. Cantilever Beam

  • Fixed at one end and free at the other.
  • Fixed end prevents both translation and rotation.
  • Free end can move and rotate.
  • Maximum bending moment generally occurs at the fixed end for a point load at the free end.
  • Example: crane arm and projecting structural brackets.

3. Fixed Beam

  • Both ends are rigidly fixed.
  • Neither end can rotate or translate.
  • Develops support moments at both ends.
  • Generally has less deflection than a simply supported beam under comparable loading.

4. Overhanging Beam

  • One or both ends extend beyond the supports.
  • The extended portion is called an overhang.
  • May develop negative bending moments near supports.

1.3 Types of Loads Acting on Beams

1. Point Load (Concentrated Load)

  • Acts at a particular point on a beam.
  • Represented by P.
  • Unit: Newton (N) or kilonewton (kN).
  • Example: Weight of a machine mounted at a particular location.

2. Uniformly Distributed Load (UDL)

  • Load is distributed uniformly over a length.
  • Load intensity remains constant throughout the loaded region.
  • Represented by w.
  • Unit: N/m or kN/m.

Total load:

W = wL

Where:

  • w = Load intensity.
  • L = Length over which the load acts.
  • Equivalent resultant load acts at the centre of the distributed length.

3. Uniformly Varying Load (UVL)

  • Load intensity varies linearly along the beam.
  • May be triangular or trapezoidal.
  • A triangular load has zero intensity at one end and maximum intensity at the other.

Resultant load for triangular loading:

W = ½ wmax L
  • Its line of action passes through the centroid of the triangular load diagram.
  • For a triangular load increasing from zero to maximum, the resultant acts at 2L/3 from the zero-load end.

4. Applied Couple or Moment

  • A pure moment causes rotation or bending tendency.
  • It does not produce a net vertical force.
  • Unit: N·m or kN·m.
  • Causes a sudden change in the bending moment diagram.

1.4 Shear Force (SF)

Definition: Shear force at any section of a beam is the algebraic sum of all transverse external forces acting on either side of that section.

  • It is an internal resisting force.
  • It acts parallel to the cross-section.
  • It prevents adjacent portions of a beam from sliding relative to each other.
  • Usually represented by V or Fs.
  • Unit: N or kN.

Understanding shear force

Imagine cutting a loaded beam at a particular location.

  • The external loads on one side of the cut try to move one portion vertically relative to the other.
  • Internal shear force develops at the cut to resist this tendency.
  • The magnitude of internal shear force equals the algebraic sum of external transverse forces on one side of the section.

Important points

  • Shear force is positive or negative depending on the adopted sign convention.
  • A point load produces a sudden jump in the shear force diagram.
  • Under a constant UDL, shear force changes linearly.
  • Under a linearly varying load, shear force changes parabolically.

1.5 Bending Moment (BM)

Definition: Bending moment at any section of a beam is the algebraic sum of the moments of all external forces acting on either side of that section about the section.

  • It represents the internal resistance against bending.
  • Usually represented by M.
  • Unit: N·m or kN·m.

Understanding bending moment

  • External loads try to bend the beam.
  • Internal resisting moments develop inside the beam.
  • These moments prevent excessive rotation and bending.
  • The bending moment at a section depends on the magnitude of forces and their perpendicular distances from that section.

Types of bending moments

1. Sagging Bending Moment:

  • Beam bends like a smile.
  • Centre of the beam moves downward relative to the supports.
  • Top fibres experience compression.
  • Bottom fibres experience tension.
  • Usually considered positive.

2. Hogging Bending Moment:

  • Beam bends like a frown.
  • Top fibres experience tension.
  • Bottom fibres experience compression.
  • Usually considered negative.

1.6 Relationship Between Load, Shear Force and Bending Moment

This relationship is extremely important for solving beam problems.

Consider a beam carrying a distributed load. The fundamental relationships are:

dV / dx = −w
dM / dx = V

Where:

  • V = Shear force.
  • M = Bending moment.
  • w = Distributed load intensity.
  • x = Distance along the beam.

Meaning of these relationships

  • Rate of change of shear force equals negative load intensity.
  • Rate of change of bending moment equals shear force.
  • Area under the load diagram represents the change in shear force, with sign convention considered.
  • Area under the shear force diagram represents the change in bending moment.

Important special cases

1.7 Shear Force and Bending Moment Diagrams

  • A Shear Force Diagram (SFD) represents variation of shear force along the beam.
  • A Bending Moment Diagram (BMD) represents variation of bending moment along the beam.
  • These diagrams help engineers identify critical sections.
  • They are essential for designing beams and selecting suitable cross-sections.

General procedure for drawing SFD and BMD

  1. Identify the type of beam and supports.
  2. Draw the free-body diagram.
  3. Identify all applied loads and their positions.
  4. Calculate support reactions using equilibrium equations.
  5. Divide the beam into different sections wherever loading changes.
  6. Calculate shear force at important sections.
  7. Draw the shear force diagram.
  8. Calculate bending moments at important sections.
  9. Draw the bending moment diagram.
  10. Identify maximum and minimum bending moments.

1.8 Important Points to Remember

  • Point load causes a sudden change in shear force.
  • Point load generally does not cause a sudden change in bending moment.
  • Applied concentrated moment causes a sudden change in bending moment.
  • Maximum bending moment generally occurs where shear force changes sign or becomes zero.
  • At a simply supported end without an applied couple, bending moment is zero.
  • At the free end of a cantilever without an applied couple, bending moment is zero.
  • The area under the SFD gives the change in BM.
  • The slope of the BMD represents shear force.
  • Maximum bending moment is important for beam design.

2.1 Introduction to Bending Stress

  • When a beam is subjected to transverse loading, it bends.
  • Bending causes different fibres of the beam to experience tension and compression.
  • The internal normal stress developed due to bending is called bending stress or flexural stress.
  • It acts perpendicular to the cross-section.
  • It is generally represented by σb.
  • Unit: Pascal (Pa), MPa or N/mm².

Example

When a simply supported beam bends downward:

  • Top fibres shorten.
  • Bottom fibres elongate.
  • Top fibres experience compressive stress.
  • Bottom fibres experience tensile stress.

2.2 Neutral Axis

  • The neutral axis is an imaginary line in the cross-section of a bent beam where longitudinal bending stress is zero.
  • Fibres located above and below the neutral axis experience compression or tension, depending on the bending direction.
  • The neutral axis separates the tensile and compressive regions.
  • For a homogeneous beam under pure bending with linear elastic behaviour, it passes through the centroid of the cross-section.

Important points

  • Stress at the neutral axis is zero in pure bending.
  • Stress increases linearly with distance from the neutral axis.
  • Maximum bending stress occurs at the farthest fibre from the neutral axis.
  • The position of the neutral axis depends on the cross-sectional geometry and material properties.

2.3 Assumptions of Simple Bending Theory

The simple bending theory is based on the following assumptions:

  1. Beam material is homogeneous and isotropic.
  2. Material obeys Hooke's law.
  3. Beam is initially straight and has a uniform cross-section.
  4. Young's modulus is the same in tension and compression.
  5. Plane sections before bending remain plane after bending.
  6. Cross-sections remain perpendicular to the neutral axis after bending.
  7. Radius of curvature is large compared with beam dimensions.
  8. The beam is subjected to pure bending in the region considered.
  9. Deflections and rotations are small.

These assumptions allow the derivation of the flexural formula.

2.4 Flexural Formula

The fundamental bending equation is:

M / I = σ / y = E / R
SymbolMeaningUnit
MBending momentN·mm
ISecond moment of areamm⁴
σBending stressN/mm²
yDistance from neutral axismm
EYoung's modulusN/mm²
RRadius of curvaturemm

This formula connects bending moment, bending stress, material stiffness and beam geometry.

Different forms of the flexural formula

Bending stress at a distance y:

σ = My / I

Maximum bending stress:

σmax = Mc / I

Where c is the maximum distance from the neutral axis.

2.5 Section Modulus

  • Section modulus measures the bending strength of a cross-section.
  • It indicates the ability of a cross-section to resist bending stress.
  • Represented by Z.
  • Unit: mm³ or m³.
Z = I / c

Therefore:

σmax = M / Z

Important points

  • Higher section modulus means lower bending stress for the same bending moment.
  • Section modulus depends on the shape and dimensions of the cross-section.
  • It is an important parameter in beam design.

2.6 Second Moment of Area (Area Moment of Inertia)

  • Second moment of area describes the distribution of cross-sectional area about a particular axis.
  • It measures resistance to bending deformation.
  • Represented by I.
  • Unit: mm⁴ or m⁴.
  • It is a geometrical property, not the mass moment of inertia.

Important formulas

1. Rectangular Section

For breadth b and depth d, about the centroidal horizontal axis:

I = bd³ / 12

2. Solid Circular Section

For diameter D, about a centroidal diametral axis:

I = πD⁴ / 64

3. Hollow Circular Section

For external diameter D and internal diameter d:

I = π(D⁴ − d⁴) / 64

4. Triangular Section

For base b and height h, about its centroidal axis parallel to the base:

I = bh³ / 36

5. I-Section

  • The second moment of area is calculated by dividing the cross-section into rectangular components.
  • The parallel axis theorem is commonly used.
  • I-sections provide high bending resistance for relatively low material weight.

2.7 Parallel Axis Theorem

The parallel axis theorem is used to calculate the second moment of area about an axis parallel to the centroidal axis.

I = IG + Ad²

Where:

  • I = Moment of area about the required axis.
  • IG = Moment of area about centroidal axis.
  • A = Area of the section.
  • d = Distance between the two parallel axes.

Applications

  • Composite cross-sections.
  • I-sections.
  • T-sections.
  • Built-up structural members.

2.8 Bending Stress Distribution

  • Bending stress varies linearly across the depth of a beam under simple bending.
  • It is zero at the neutral axis.
  • Maximum tensile and compressive stresses occur at the extreme fibres.
  • The distribution is triangular for a homogeneous beam with a symmetrical material response.
σ = My / I

Therefore:

σ ∝ y

This means bending stress is directly proportional to the distance from the neutral axis.

2.9 Importance of Bending in Marine Engineering

  • Ship hull structures experience bending due to waves, cargo distribution and buoyancy distribution.
  • The ship hull acts approximately like a long beam.
  • Hogging and sagging create longitudinal bending stresses.
  • Decks and bottom structures experience different tensile and compressive stresses depending on the bending condition.
  • Engine foundations and supporting structures must resist bending caused by machinery loads.
  • Proper structural design prevents excessive deformation and failure.

3.1 Introduction

  • When a beam is subjected to transverse loading, internal shear force develops.
  • This shear force produces shear stress across the cross-section.
  • Shear stress tends to make one layer of material slide relative to an adjacent layer.
  • Represented by τ.
  • Unit: N/mm² or MPa.

Difference between bending stress and shear stress

Caused by bending momentCaused by shear force

Zero at neutral axis in simple bendingOften maximum near neutral axis

3.2 Shear Stress Formula in Beams

The fundamental shear stress equation is:

τ = VQ / Ib

Where:

  • τ = Shear stress at the required location.
  • V = Shear force at the section.
  • Q = First moment of area of the portion above or below the location.
  • I = Second moment of area of the entire cross-section about the neutral axis.
  • b = Width of the cross-section at the location where shear stress is calculated.

First moment of area

Q = A ȳ

Where:

  • A = Area of the selected portion of the cross-section.
  • ȳ = Distance of its centroid from the neutral axis.

Important

  • Q is not the same as the second moment of area I.
  • The width b is the local width at the position where shear stress is calculated.
  • The formula gives the average shear stress across the local width at that level.

3.3 Shear Stress Distribution in Different Cross-Sections

1. Rectangular Cross-Section

  • Shear stress distribution is parabolic.
  • Shear stress is zero at the top and bottom surfaces.
  • Maximum shear stress occurs at the neutral axis.

For a rectangular section:

τmax = 3V / 2A

Where:

  • V = Shear force.
  • A = Cross-sectional area.

Average shear stress:

τavg = V / A

Therefore:

τmax = 1.5 τavg

2. Circular Cross-Section

  • Shear stress distribution is non-uniform.
  • Shear stress is zero at the outermost top and bottom points.
  • Maximum shear stress occurs at the neutral axis.

For a solid circular cross-section:

τmax = 4V / 3A

3. I-Section

  • Most of the shear force is resisted by the web.
  • The web is relatively thin and carries significant shear stress.
  • Shear stress is generally high in the web.
  • Flanges primarily resist bending stresses.
  • Shear stress distribution changes across the flange-web junction.

3.4 Shear Flow

  • Shear flow represents the shear force transferred per unit length along a beam.
  • It is especially useful for thin-walled structural members.
  • Represented by q.
  • Unit: N/mm or N/m.
q = VQ / I

Relationship between shear flow and shear stress:

q = τb

Applications

  • Riveted and bolted joints.
  • Built-up beams.
  • Thin-walled ship structures.
  • Welded structural connections.
  • Composite structural members.

3.5 Importance in Marine Structures

  • Ship decks contain longitudinal and transverse stiffeners.
  • Shear forces develop in bulkheads and hull girders.
  • Engine foundations transfer machinery loads into the ship structure.
  • Structural joints must safely transfer shear forces.
  • Excessive shear stress can cause web failure or shear buckling.
Remember:
Maximum bending stress generally occurs at extreme fibres.
Maximum transverse shear stress in rectangular beams occurs at the neutral axis.
In I-beams, the web carries most of the transverse shear force.

4.1 Introduction to Torsion

  • Torsion is the twisting of a structural member due to an applied torque.
  • Torque is a moment that tends to rotate a body about its longitudinal axis.
  • Shafts are commonly subjected to torsional loading.
  • Torsion produces shear stress in the shaft material.
  • The shaft also experiences angular deformation called the angle of twist.

Examples in Marine Engineering

  • Main propulsion shafts.
  • Intermediate shafts.
  • Crankshafts.
  • Generator shafts.
  • Pump drive shafts.
  • Turbine shafts.

4.2 Torque

  • Torque is the turning effect of a force about an axis.
  • Represented by T.
  • Unit: N·m or N·mm.
T = F × r

Where:

  • F = Applied force.
  • r = Perpendicular distance from the axis of rotation.

4.3 Torsion Equation

For a circular shaft under elastic torsion:

T / J = τ / r = Gθ / L

The equation is applicable under the assumptions of elastic behaviour, uniform circular shaft geometry and suitable torsional loading.

4.4 Polar Moment of Inertia

  • Polar moment of inertia measures resistance of a cross-section to torsion.
  • Represented by J.
  • It is a geometrical property.
  • Unit: mm⁴ or m⁴.

1. Solid Circular Shaft

J = πD⁴ / 32

2. Hollow Circular Shaft

J = π(D⁴ − d⁴) / 32

Where:

  • D = Outer diameter.
  • d = Inner diameter.

4.5 Shear Stress Distribution in a Shaft

  • Shear stress is zero at the centre of the shaft.
  • It increases linearly with radial distance.
  • Maximum shear stress occurs at the outer surface.
  • The stress distribution is linear for elastic torsion.
τ = Tr / J

Maximum shear stress:

τmax = TR / J

Where R is the outer radius.

4.6 Angle of Twist

  • When torque is applied, the shaft twists through an angular displacement.
  • This angular displacement is called the angle of twist.
  • Represented by θ.
  • Unit: Radian or degree.
θ = TL / GJ

Important observations

  • Angle of twist increases with torque.
  • Angle of twist increases with shaft length.
  • Angle of twist decreases with increasing shear modulus.
  • Angle of twist decreases with increasing polar moment of inertia.

4.7 Modulus of Rigidity

  • Modulus of rigidity is also called shear modulus.
  • It represents resistance of a material to shear deformation.
  • Represented by G.
  • Unit: Pa or N/mm².

Relationship with Young's modulus:

E = 2G(1 + ν)

Where:

  • E = Young's modulus.
  • G = Modulus of rigidity.
  • ν = Poisson's ratio.

4.8 Power Transmitted by a Shaft

Rotating shafts transmit power through torque.

P = 2πNT / 60

Where:

  • P = Power in watts.
  • N = Rotational speed in RPM.
  • T = Torque in N·m.

If power is in kW:

P = 2πNT / 60,000

Important relationship:

T = 9550P / N

Where:

  • P = Power in kW.
  • N = Speed in RPM.
  • T = Torque in N·m.

4.9 Solid Shaft vs Hollow Shaft

Solid shaftHollow shaft

Generally simpler to manufactureMore efficient use of material in torsion
Greater mass for a given outer diameterLower mass for the same outer diameter and material

Why are hollow shafts efficient?
  • Shear stress increases from the centre towards the outer surface.
  • Material near the centre contributes relatively little to torsional resistance.
  • Removing some central material can reduce weight while retaining good torsional strength.
  • Hollow shafts are useful where weight reduction is important.

4.10 Important Points to Remember

  • Torsion produces shear stress.
  • Maximum torsional shear stress occurs at the outer surface.
  • Stress is zero at the centre of a solid circular shaft.
  • Hollow shafts can provide a favourable strength-to-weight ratio.
  • Angle of twist is directly proportional to torque and length.
  • Angle of twist is inversely proportional to GJ.
  • Propeller shafts transmit engine power to the propeller through torque.

5.1 Introduction to Combined Stresses

  • A structural component may experience more than one type of stress simultaneously.
  • These are called combined stresses.
  • For example, a shaft may experience torsional shear stress and bending normal stress simultaneously.
  • The resulting stress condition is called a state of stress.

Common combinations

  1. Direct tensile stress and shear stress.
  2. Direct compressive stress and shear stress.
  3. Bending stress and torsional shear stress.
  4. Two mutually perpendicular normal stresses.
  5. Normal stress and shear stress on the same plane.

5.2 Principal Stresses

  • Principal stresses are the maximum and minimum normal stresses acting at a point on planes where shear stress is zero.
  • These planes are called principal planes.
  • Principal stresses are important for predicting failure in structural components.
  • Represented by σ₁ and σ₂ in plane stress.

For a two-dimensional state of stress:

σ₁,₂ = (σx + σy)/2 ± √[((σx − σy)/2)² + τxy²]

Where:

  • σx = Normal stress in the x-direction.
  • σy = Normal stress in the y-direction.
  • τxy = Shear stress.

Important points

  • Principal planes have zero shear stress.
  • Principal stresses are perpendicular to each other in the two-dimensional stress representation.
  • One principal stress is maximum and the other is minimum.
  • Principal stresses may be tensile, compressive or of opposite signs.

5.3 Maximum Shear Stress

Maximum in-plane shear stress is:

τmax = √[((σx − σy)/2)² + τxy²]

The average normal stress on the plane of maximum in-plane shear is:

σavg = (σx + σy) / 2
Important distinction:
  • Maximum in-plane shear stress is not necessarily the absolute maximum three-dimensional shear stress.
  • In three dimensions, absolute maximum shear stress depends on the largest and smallest principal stresses:
τmax,3D = (σmax − σmin) / 2

5.4 Principal Plane Angle

The angle of the principal plane can be determined by:

tan 2θp = 2τxy / (σx − σy)
  • θp is the orientation of a principal plane, subject to the chosen sign convention.
  • The two principal planes are perpendicular to each other.
  • The factor of 2 appears because stress transformation involves double-angle relationships.

5.5 Mohr's Circle

Mohr's circle is a graphical method used to determine:

  • Principal stresses.
  • Maximum shear stress.
  • Average normal stress.
  • Principal plane orientations.
  • Maximum shear plane orientations.
  • Normal and shear stresses on inclined planes.

5.6 Construction of Mohr's Circle

Step 1: Identify the stresses — Determine σx, σy, τxy.

Step 2: Draw coordinate axes — Horizontal axis represents normal stress σ; vertical axis represents shear stress τ.

Step 3: Plot the stress points — For the conventional sign convention:

  • Point A represents (σx, τxy).
  • Point B represents (σy, −τxy).

Step 4: Find the centre

C = (σx + σy) / 2

Step 5: Calculate the radius

R = √[((σx − σy)/2)² + τxy²]

Step 6: Determine principal stresses

σ₁ = C + R
σ₂ = C − R

Step 7: Determine maximum shear stress

τmax = R

Important characteristics of Mohr's Circle

  • Centre lies on the normal stress axis.
  • Radius represents maximum in-plane shear stress.
  • Horizontal intercepts represent principal stresses.
  • The top and bottom points represent maximum positive and negative shear stress.
  • An angle of 2θ on Mohr's circle corresponds to a physical plane rotation of θ.

5.7 Types of Principal Stresses

1. Major Principal Stress — Maximum normal stress at a point; represented by σ₁.

2. Minor Principal Stress — Minimum normal stress at a point; represented by σ₂.

3. Third Principal Stress — In three-dimensional stress analysis, a third principal stress σ₃ exists. It acts perpendicular to the other two principal directions. In plane stress problems, σ₃ = 0 in the out-of-plane direction.

5.8 Importance in Marine Engineering

  • Ship structures experience combined loading due to bending, shear and torsion.
  • Crankshafts experience bending and torsion.
  • Propeller shafts experience torsional stresses and additional bending effects.
  • Pressure vessels and boiler components experience combined stresses.
  • Principal stress analysis helps engineers evaluate the possibility of yielding and structural failure.

6.1 Introduction to Columns

  • A column is a structural member primarily subjected to axial compressive loading.
  • It is generally vertical, although the term can also apply to inclined compression members.
  • Columns transfer compressive loads from upper structures to lower supports.
  • They are widely used in structural frameworks and machinery supports.

Examples

  • Ship engine room supporting columns.
  • Deck support pillars.
  • Structural pillars.
  • Machine frames.
  • Crane supports.

6.2 Short and Long Columns

1. Short Column

  • Has a relatively small slenderness ratio.
  • Mainly fails due to crushing or yielding.
  • Failure occurs when compressive stress exceeds the material's compressive strength.
  • Lateral deformation is generally small before failure.

2. Long Column

  • Has a relatively large slenderness ratio.
  • More likely to fail by buckling.
  • Lateral deflection develops under axial compression.
  • It may fail at a load significantly lower than its crushing load.

3. Intermediate Column

  • Lies between short and long columns.
  • Failure may involve both crushing and buckling effects.
  • Its behaviour is more complicated than that of short or ideal long columns.

6.3 Buckling

  • Buckling is a sudden lateral deflection or instability of a compression member.
  • It occurs when the axial compressive load reaches a critical value.
  • The column bends sideways rather than simply crushing.
  • Buckling can occur even when the average compressive stress is below the material's yield strength.

Important points

  • Long slender columns are more susceptible to buckling.
  • Buckling is an instability failure.
  • Small initial imperfections can significantly affect buckling strength.
  • The load-carrying capacity depends on length, cross-section, material stiffness and end conditions.

6.4 Euler's Theory of Buckling

Euler's theory predicts the critical buckling load of an ideal slender elastic column.

Pcr = π²EI / Le²

Where:

  • Pcr = Critical buckling load.
  • E = Young's modulus.
  • I = Least second moment of area of the cross-section.
  • Le = Effective length of the column.
Important: The column buckles about its weaker axis, meaning the axis with the smaller second moment of area.

6.5 Effective Length of Columns

Effective length depends on the end conditions.

End conditionEffective length
Both ends hinged/pinnedLe = L
Both ends fixedLe = L/2
One end fixed, other freeLe = 2L
One end fixed, other hingedLe ≈ 0.7L
Remember:
Fixed-fixed columns have the shortest effective length among these common cases.
Fixed-free columns have the longest effective length.
Lower effective length means higher critical buckling load.

6.6 Slenderness Ratio

  • Slenderness ratio indicates how slender a column is.
  • It is the ratio of effective length to radius of gyration.
  • Represented by λ.
λ = Le / k

Where:

k = √(I / A)
  • k = Radius of gyration.
  • I = Second moment of area about the relevant buckling axis.
  • A = Cross-sectional area.

Important points

  • High slenderness ratio indicates a slender column.
  • Low slenderness ratio indicates a stockier column.
  • A higher slenderness ratio generally increases susceptibility to buckling.

6.7 Euler Critical Stress

Critical buckling stress is:

σcr = Pcr / A

Therefore:

σcr = π²E / λ²

This equation shows:

  • Critical stress is directly proportional to Young's modulus.
  • Critical stress is inversely proportional to the square of slenderness ratio.
  • Doubling the slenderness ratio reduces the ideal Euler critical stress to one-fourth.

6.8 Limitations of Euler's Theory

Euler's formula is based on ideal assumptions:

  1. Column is initially perfectly straight.
  2. Material is homogeneous and isotropic.
  3. Material remains within the elastic limit.
  4. Load acts perfectly along the centroidal axis.
  5. Column has a uniform cross-section.
  6. End conditions are ideal.
  7. Lateral deflections are small.
Limitations:
  • Not suitable for short columns.
  • Not reliable when material yields before elastic buckling.
  • Real columns contain imperfections and residual stresses.
  • Actual support conditions may differ from ideal assumptions.

6.9 Rankine's Formula

Rankine's formula combines crushing and buckling effects and is useful for intermediate columns.

PR = σc A / (1 + aλ²)

Where:

  • PR = Rankine crippling load.
  • σc = Crushing stress.
  • A = Cross-sectional area.
  • a = Rankine constant for the material and unit convention used.
  • λ = Slenderness ratio.
Important:
Rankine's formula is an empirical relationship.
It accounts for both crushing and buckling behaviour.
It is more suitable than Euler's formula for intermediate columns.

6.10 Marine Engineering Applications

  • Structural pillars support ship decks.
  • Engine room supports carry heavy machinery loads.
  • Bulkhead stiffeners experience compressive forces.
  • Supporting structures must resist buckling under dynamic ship loading.
  • Proper stiffening increases structural stability.

This section is particularly important because marine machinery experiences continuous operation, cyclic loading, vibration, temperature changes and localised stresses.

7.1 Fatigue Failure

Definition: Fatigue is the progressive failure of a material caused by repeated or fluctuating stresses over time.

  • Fatigue failure can occur even when the maximum applied stress is below the material's yield strength.
  • It develops due to repeated loading and unloading.
  • Small cracks initiate at locations of high local stress.
  • These cracks gradually propagate.
  • Finally, the remaining material becomes insufficient to carry the applied load and sudden fracture occurs.

Examples in Marine Engineering

  • Crankshafts subjected to cyclic bending and torsion.
  • Propeller shafts.
  • Connecting rods.
  • Gear teeth.
  • Engine components exposed to repeated vibration.
  • Welded ship structures exposed to wave-induced cyclic stresses.

7.2 Stages of Fatigue Failure

Fatigue failure generally occurs in three stages.

1. Crack Initiation

  • A small crack develops at a local weak point.
  • Often begins at the surface.
  • Common locations include scratches, notches, sharp corners and machining marks.
  • Stress concentration increases the possibility of crack initiation.

2. Crack Propagation

  • Repeated loading causes the crack to grow.
  • The crack gradually moves through the material.
  • The crack growth rate depends on stress range, material properties and operating conditions.
  • Fatigue striations may appear on the fracture surface.

3. Final Fracture

  • The remaining cross-sectional area becomes too small.
  • The material can no longer withstand the applied load.
  • Sudden fracture occurs.

7.3 Types of Fatigue Loading

1. Completely Reversed Stress

  • Stress alternates between equal tensile and compressive values.
  • Mean stress is zero.
σm = 0

2. Repeated Stress

  • Stress varies from zero to a maximum value of the same sign.
  • Example: repeated tensile loading.

3. Fluctuating Stress

  • Stress varies between minimum and maximum values.
  • It may contain both tensile and compressive portions.

7.4 Important Fatigue Parameters

1. Maximum Stress

σmax

Highest stress during a loading cycle.

2. Minimum Stress

σmin

Lowest stress during a loading cycle.

3. Mean Stress

σm = (σmax + σmin) / 2

4. Stress Amplitude

σa = (σmax − σmin) / 2

5. Stress Range

Δσ = σmax − σmin

6. Stress Ratio

R = σmin / σmax

These parameters help describe the severity and nature of cyclic loading.

7.5 S-N Curve

  • An S-N curve is also called a Wöhler curve.
  • It represents the relationship between cyclic stress amplitude (S) and the number of cycles to failure (N).
  • Stress amplitude is generally plotted on the vertical axis.
  • Number of cycles to failure is plotted on a logarithmic horizontal axis.
  • It is used to estimate fatigue life.

Important characteristics

  • Higher cyclic stress generally results in fewer cycles to failure.
  • Lower cyclic stress generally results in longer fatigue life.
  • Some materials, particularly many steels, show an approximate endurance limit.
  • Many non-ferrous alloys do not exhibit a clear endurance limit.
  • Fatigue behaviour depends on surface finish, size, temperature and loading conditions.

7.6 Endurance Limit

  • Endurance limit is the maximum stress amplitude that a material can theoretically withstand for an indefinitely large number of cycles under specified test conditions.
  • It is also called the fatigue limit.
  • Commonly associated with certain ferrous materials.
  • It is an important parameter in fatigue-resistant component design.
Important distinction:
  • Endurance limit is not the same as yield strength.
  • A material can fail by fatigue at stresses below its yield strength.
  • Actual component endurance strength must account for service conditions and design factors.

7.7 Methods to Improve Fatigue Strength

  1. Avoid sharp corners and sudden changes in cross-section.
  2. Use smooth surface finishes.
  3. Provide suitable fillet radii.
  4. Reduce stress concentrations.
  5. Apply shot peening where appropriate.
  6. Use suitable heat treatment.
  7. Avoid excessive vibration.
  8. Ensure correct shaft alignment.
  9. Maintain proper lubrication.
  10. Perform regular inspection and non-destructive testing.

7.8 Creep

Definition: Creep is the slow and permanent deformation of a material under a constant load or stress over a prolonged period, particularly at elevated temperatures.

  • Creep is a time-dependent deformation.
  • It can occur even when the applied stress is constant.
  • Temperature has a significant influence on creep.
  • Creep becomes important at high operating temperatures.
  • It may occur in metals, polymers and other materials.

Examples in Marine Engineering

  • Steam turbine blades.
  • Boiler tubes.
  • Exhaust valves.
  • Exhaust manifolds.
  • High-temperature engine components.
  • Superheated steam pipelines.

7.9 Stages of Creep

Creep is generally divided into three stages.

1. Primary Creep (Transient Creep)

  • Occurs immediately after the load is applied.
  • Creep strain increases with time.
  • Creep rate gradually decreases.
  • Material initially undergoes relatively rapid deformation.
  • Work hardening is important during this stage.

2. Secondary Creep (Steady-State Creep)

  • Creep rate becomes approximately constant.
  • Work hardening and recovery processes reach a balance.
  • This stage often represents a major portion of the component's service life.
  • It is important in engineering design.

3. Tertiary Creep

  • Creep rate increases rapidly.
  • Internal damage develops.
  • Necking, void formation and microstructural deterioration may occur.
  • The component eventually fractures.

7.10 Factors Affecting Creep

  1. Temperature.
  2. Applied stress.
  3. Duration of loading.
  4. Material composition.
  5. Grain size and microstructure.
  6. Heat treatment.
  7. Environmental conditions.

Important relationship

  • Higher temperature generally increases creep deformation.
  • Higher applied stress generally increases creep rate.
  • Longer exposure produces greater total creep strain.

For many metals, creep becomes particularly significant at temperatures around 0.4 times their absolute melting temperature (in Kelvin), although this is only a general guideline.

7.11 Creep Prevention

  • Use materials with suitable high-temperature strength.
  • Maintain operating temperature within specified limits.
  • Avoid excessive stress.
  • Use suitable heat treatment.
  • Inspect high-temperature components regularly.
  • Replace components when creep damage exceeds permissible limits.

7.12 Stress Concentration

Definition: Stress concentration is the local increase in stress around geometric discontinuities or irregularities in a structural component.

  • Actual stress is not uniformly distributed throughout a component.
  • Certain geometrical features cause stress to become concentrated in small regions.
  • These regions may become starting points for fatigue cracks or fractures.

Common causes

  1. Holes.
  2. Notches.
  3. Sharp corners.
  4. Grooves.
  5. Keyways.
  6. Threads.
  7. Sudden changes in cross-section.
  8. Weld defects.
  9. Scratches and surface damage.

7.13 Stress Concentration Factor

  • Stress concentration factor measures the increase in maximum local stress compared with nominal stress.
  • Represented by Kt.
Kt = σmax / σnominal

For shear stress concentration:

Kts = τmax / τnominal

Where:

  • Kt = Theoretical normal stress concentration factor.
  • Kts = Theoretical shear stress concentration factor.

Important points

  • Kt is dimensionless.
  • It is generally greater than or equal to 1.
  • Larger geometric discontinuities or sharper notches can increase stress concentration.
  • Its value depends on geometry and loading conditions.
  • Stress concentration factors are often obtained from charts, handbooks or numerical analysis.

7.14 Methods to Reduce Stress Concentration

  1. Avoid sharp internal corners.
  2. Provide generous fillet radii.
  3. Use gradual changes in cross-section.
  4. Avoid unnecessary grooves and notches.
  5. Improve surface finish.
  6. Provide suitable reinforcement around holes.
  7. Use proper welding procedures.
  8. Avoid machining marks in highly stressed regions.
  9. Design keyways and threads carefully.

7.15 Stress Concentration and Fatigue

  • Stress concentration increases local stress.
  • Higher local stress increases the possibility of fatigue crack initiation.
  • Fatigue cracks commonly begin at holes, notches, keyways and sharp corners.
  • Therefore, reducing stress concentration improves fatigue performance.

Fatigue stress concentration factor:

Kf = 1 + q(Kt − 1)

Where:

  • Kf = Fatigue stress concentration factor.
  • Kt = Theoretical stress concentration factor.
  • q = Notch sensitivity factor.

The notch sensitivity factor generally lies between 0 and 1 for conventional engineering materials.

10. Important Concepts to Memorise for GME

The following are high-value conceptual points. Make sure you can explain each one without looking at your notes.

  1. A point load produces a sudden jump in the shear force diagram.
  2. The maximum bending moment generally occurs where shear force is zero or changes sign.
  3. Sagging produces compression at the top and tension at the bottom.
  4. Hogging produces tension at the top and compression at the bottom.
  5. The neutral axis experiences zero longitudinal bending stress in simple bending.
  6. Bending stress varies linearly with distance from the neutral axis.
  7. Section modulus indicates the bending strength of a cross-section.
  8. Second moment of area is a geometrical property that measures resistance to bending.
  9. Maximum shear stress in a rectangular beam occurs at its neutral axis.
  10. In an I-beam, most transverse shear force is carried by the web.
  11. Torsion produces shear stress in circular shafts.
  12. Maximum torsional shear stress occurs at the outer surface of a circular shaft.
  13. Shear stress at the centre of a solid circular shaft is zero under elastic torsion.
  14. Hollow shafts provide good torsional strength relative to their weight.
  15. Principal planes have zero shear stress.
  16. Mohr's circle is used to determine principal stresses and maximum in-plane shear stress.
  17. Maximum shear stress is equal to the radius of Mohr's circle.
  18. Long slender columns are more susceptible to buckling.
  19. Euler's critical buckling load is inversely proportional to the square of effective length.
  20. A fixed-free column has an effective length twice its actual length under ideal Euler conditions.
  21. Buckling is an instability failure rather than simply a crushing failure.
  22. Fatigue occurs due to repeated or fluctuating stresses.
  23. Fatigue failure can occur below the yield strength.
  24. Fatigue failure involves crack initiation, crack propagation and final fracture.
  25. Creep is time-dependent permanent deformation under sustained loading.
  26. Creep is particularly important at elevated temperatures.
  27. Secondary creep has an approximately constant creep rate.
  28. Tertiary creep involves rapidly increasing deformation before failure.
  29. Stress concentration occurs near holes, notches and sharp corners.
  30. Increasing fillet radius generally reduces stress concentration.
