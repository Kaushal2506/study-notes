1.1 Basic concept

  • Unit conversion means changing a physical quantity from one unit to another without changing its actual value.
  • Dimensional analysis checks whether an equation is physically correct.
  • Always convert quantities into compatible units before calculations.

1.3 Important unit conversions

Length

  • 1 km = 1000 m
  • 1 m = 100 cm
  • 1 inch = 25.4 mm
  • 1 foot = 0.3048 m

Area and volume

  • 1 m² = 10,000 cm²
  • 1 m³ = 1000 litres
  • 1 litre = 0.001 m³
  • 1 cm³ = 1 mL

Mass

  • 1 tonne = 1000 kg
  • 1 kg = 1000 g

Pressure

  • 1 bar = 100,000 Pa
  • 1 kPa = 1000 Pa
  • 1 MPa = 10 bar
  • 1 atmosphere ≈ 1.01325 bar
  • 1 bar ≈ 14.5 psi

Energy and power

  • 1 kW = 1000 W
  • 1 MW = 1000 kW
  • 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ
  • 1 HP ≈ 746 W
  • 1 metric horsepower (PS) ≈ 735.5 W

Temperature

  • K = °C + 273.15
  • °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9
  • °F = (°C × 9/5) + 32

1.4 Dimensional analysis

Dimensions represent the physical nature of a quantity.

QuantityDimensions
Velocity[LT⁻¹]
Acceleration[LT⁻²]
Force[MLT⁻²]
Work / Energy[ML²T⁻²]
Power[ML²T⁻³]
Pressure[ML⁻¹T⁻²]
Density[ML⁻³]
Momentum[MLT⁻¹]

Principle of dimensional homogeneity

  • Both sides of a physically valid equation must have the same dimensions.
  • Only quantities with compatible dimensions can be added or subtracted.

Example

F = ma
[MLT⁻²] = [M][LT⁻²]

Hence, the equation is dimensionally correct.

Engineering applications

  • Converting engine power from kW to HP.
  • Converting boiler pressure from bar to Pa.
  • Converting fuel consumption from litres to cubic metres.
  • Checking dimensional correctness of engineering formulas.
  • Converting temperature readings in marine machinery.
Remember: Always convert units before substituting values into formulas.

2.1 Basic concept

  • Graphs represent the relationship between two or more variables.
  • They help engineers understand machine performance and operating conditions.
  • The horizontal axis is called the X-axis.
  • The vertical axis is called the Y-axis.

2.2 Types of engineering graphs

  • 1. Linear graph – Shows a straight-line relationship. Example: Distance versus time at constant velocity. Slope represents the rate of change.
  • 2. Non-linear graph – Shows a curved relationship. Example: Pump performance and engine characteristics.
  • 3. P-T graph – Shows pressure versus temperature. Used in refrigeration and thermodynamics.
  • 4. Bar graph – Compares different values. Example: Fuel consumption of different engines.

2.3 Important graph formulas

Slope / Gradient

m = y₂ − y₁x₂ − x₁
  • Positive slope: Variables increase together.
  • Negative slope: One increases while the other decreases.
  • Zero slope: Constant Y value.

Percentage change

% Change = New − OriginalOriginal × 100

Percentage efficiency

η = OutputInput × 100

2.5 How to interpret a graph

  1. Read the title.
  2. Identify X-axis and Y-axis.
  3. Check units and scale.
  4. Identify maximum and minimum values.
  5. Observe increasing or decreasing trends.
  6. Calculate slope if required.
  7. Compare data points.
  8. Identify unusual changes or sudden variations.
Remember:
Slope = Rate of change.
Area under a velocity-time graph = Displacement.
Area under a force-displacement graph = Work.
Area under a power-time graph = Energy.

These calculations are extremely important in marine engineering because they are used in pumps, compressors, boilers, diesel engines and propulsion systems.

3.1 Force

Force is a push or pull that changes or tends to change the motion of an object.

F = m × a
  • F = Force (N)
  • m = Mass (kg)
  • a = Acceleration (m/s²)

Weight

W = m × g

Where g = 9.81 m/s².

Example

A body has a mass of 50 kg. Find its weight.

W = 50 × 9.81 = 490.5 N

3.2 Pressure

Pressure is the force acting per unit area.

P = FA

Hydrostatic pressure

P = ρgh

Example

Calculate the pressure at a depth of 5 m in seawater. Take density = 1025 kg/m³.

P = 1025 × 9.81 × 5
P = 50,276.25 Pa
P ≈ 0.503 bar

Types of pressure

  • Atmospheric pressure: Pressure exerted by the atmosphere.
  • Absolute pressure: Pressure measured relative to a perfect vacuum.
  • Gauge pressure: Pressure measured relative to atmospheric pressure.
  • Vacuum pressure: Pressure below atmospheric pressure.
Pabsolute = Patmospheric + Pgauge

3.3 Power

P = Wt

Mechanical power

P = F × v

Rotational power

P = 2πNT60

Power in kW

P (kW) = 2πNT60,000

Hydraulic power

P = Δp × Q

3.4 Efficiency

η = OutputInput × 100

Combined efficiency

ηoverall = η₁ × η₂
Remember:
Force = Mass × Acceleration.
Pressure = Force ÷ Area.
Power = Work ÷ Time.
Efficiency = Output ÷ Input × 100.

4.1 Heat balance

Heat balance is the accounting of heat energy entering, leaving, and being stored in a system.

For a steady-state system with no energy storage:

Heat Input = Heat Output

4.2 Important heat formulas

Sensible heat

Q = mcpΔT

Latent heat

Q = mL

Heat transfer rate

Q̇ = ṁ cp (T₂ − T₁)

4.3 Heat balance example

A boiler transfers heat to 100 kg of water. The water temperature rises from 30°C to 80°C. Take specific heat capacity = 4.18 kJ/kg K.

Q = mcpΔT
Q = 100 × 4.18 × (80 − 30)
Q = 20,900 kJ

4.5 Mass balance

Mass In = Mass Out

Mass flow rate

ṁ = ρQ

4.6 Mass balance example

A tank receives 10 kg/s of water and discharges 7 kg/s.

= 10 − 7 = 3 kg/s
Remember:

Mass balance follows conservation of mass.

5.1 Basic statistics

Statistics is the mathematical study of collecting, organizing, analyzing and interpreting data.

5.2 Mean (Average)

x̄ = Σxn

5.3 Median

Median is the middle value when data is arranged in ascending or descending order.

5.4 Mode

Mode is the value that occurs most frequently.

5.5 Standard deviation

Population standard deviation

σ = √Σ(x − μ)²N

Sample standard deviation

s = √Σ(x − x̄)²n − 1

5.6 Measurement uncertainty

Measurement uncertainty indicates the possible range of doubt associated with a measured value.

Example

80 ± 1 °C
