1.1 Introduction to Crankshaft

  • A crankshaft is a rotating mechanical component that converts the reciprocating motion of the piston into rotary motion.
  • It is one of the most important components of a marine diesel engine.
  • It receives power from the piston through the connecting rod and transmits torque to the flywheel and propulsion system.
  • In a marine main engine, the crankshaft ultimately transmits power towards the propeller through the thrust bearing, shafting arrangement and associated components.

Main functions of a crankshaft

  • Converts reciprocating motion into rotary motion.
  • Transmits engine power in the form of torque.
  • Maintains the correct mechanical relationship between pistons and crank mechanism.
  • Transfers combustion forces from the piston to the engine structure.
  • Drives auxiliary mechanisms through suitable gear arrangements.
  • Helps maintain smooth engine rotation when combined with the flywheel.

1.2 Construction and Parts of a Crankshaft

A crankshaft consists of several important sections.

1. Main journals

  • Cylindrical surfaces supported by the main bearings.
  • Allow the crankshaft to rotate inside the engine structure.
  • Maintain the position and alignment of the crankshaft.
  • Transfer radial loads to the engine bedplate.

2. Crank pins (Crank journals)

  • Offset cylindrical portions of the crankshaft.
  • Connected to the big end of the connecting rod.
  • Convert the connecting rod's reciprocating force into rotational torque.
  • Generally operate with a bearing shell between the crank pin and connecting rod.

3. Crank webs (Crank arms)

  • Connect the main journals and crank pins.
  • Maintain the crank throw or offset.
  • Resist bending and torsional stresses.
  • Transfer forces between different crankshaft sections.

4. Crank throw

  • The perpendicular distance between the crankshaft axis and crank pin axis is called the crank radius.
  • The distance between the two axes is twice the crank radius and is called the crank throw.
  • It determines the piston stroke.
\text{Stroke} = 2r

Where:

  • r = Crank radius.
  • Stroke = Total distance travelled by the piston between TDC and BDC.

5. Counterweights

  • Attached to the crankshaft to balance rotating and, where applicable, reciprocating masses.
  • Reduce unbalanced forces and vibrations.
  • Help reduce bearing loads caused by rotating imbalance.

6. Oil passages

  • Internal drilled passages that supply lubricating oil to crankshaft bearings.
  • Maintain an adequate oil film between rotating and stationary surfaces.
  • Help prevent metal-to-metal contact and overheating.

7. Crankshaft flange

  • A machined connecting surface used to join the crankshaft to another shaft or flywheel.
  • In marine engines, flange couplings may connect crankshaft sections or the crankshaft to the thrust shaft, depending on engine arrangement.

1.3 Working Principle of a Crankshaft

The working process can be understood in the following sequence:

  1. Fuel is injected into the combustion chamber.
  2. Combustion produces high-pressure gases.
  3. Gas pressure pushes the piston downward during the power stroke.
  4. The piston transfers force to the connecting rod.
  5. The connecting rod pushes the crank pin.
  6. The crankshaft rotates due to the moment produced by the connecting rod force.
  7. Rotary motion is transmitted to the propulsion system.

Important Formulae Related to Crankshaft

1. Crank radius

r = L / 2

Where L is the piston stroke.

2. Angular velocity

ω = 2πN / 60

Where:

  • ω = Angular velocity (rad/s).
  • N = Engine speed (RPM).

3. Torque

T = F × r

Where:

  • T = Torque (N·m).
  • F = Tangential force (N).
  • r = Effective crank radius (m).

For an engine delivering power P:

P = 2πNT / 60
T = 9550P / N

Where:

  • P = Power in kW.
  • N = RPM.
  • T = Torque in N·m.

4. Piston displacement volume

Vs = (π/4) D²L

Where:

  • D = Cylinder bore.
  • L = Stroke length.

1.4 Stresses Acting on a Crankshaft

A crankshaft experiences different types of mechanical stresses during engine operation.

1. Bending stress

  • Produced by forces acting on the crankshaft.
  • Occurs due to combustion forces, connecting rod forces and bearing reactions.
  • Causes bending deformation.
  • Particularly important around crank webs and journal transitions.

2. Torsional stress

  • Produced by twisting torque transmitted through the crankshaft.
  • Occurs due to variations in gas pressure and engine torque.
  • Can cause torsional vibration.
  • Excessive torsional stress can lead to fatigue failure.

3. Shear stress

  • Produced by transverse forces and transmitted torque.
  • Acts along the cross-section of the shaft.

4. Fatigue stress

  • Crankshafts experience repeated loading during every engine cycle.
  • Repeated bending and torsional stresses can initiate cracks.
  • Fatigue cracks commonly develop around stress concentration regions.

5. Stress concentration

  • Occurs at locations where geometry changes suddenly.
  • Examples include:
    • Fillets between crank webs and journals.
    • Oil drilling openings.
    • Changes in shaft diameter.
    • Keyways and flange transitions.

Fillet radii are carefully designed to reduce stress concentration.

1.5 Crankshaft Materials and Manufacturing

Common materials:

  • Forged alloy steel.
  • Carbon steel.
  • Special high-strength alloy steel.

Important material properties:

  1. High tensile strength.
  2. High fatigue strength.
  3. Good toughness.
  4. Good resistance to wear.
  5. Good machinability.
  6. Adequate resistance to corrosion.

Manufacturing processes:

  • Forging: Produces a strong component with suitable grain flow.
  • Casting: Used in certain crankshaft designs.
  • Machining: Produces accurate journals, crank pins and fillets.
  • Heat treatment: Improves mechanical properties.
  • Grinding: Provides accurate bearing surfaces and surface finish.
  • Nondestructive testing: Detects internal and surface defects.

Common inspection methods include:

  • Magnetic particle inspection.
  • Ultrasonic testing.
  • Dye penetrant testing.

1.6 Crankshaft Lubrication

  • Crankshaft bearings require continuous lubrication.
  • Oil is supplied through the engine lubricating oil system.
  • Oil passes through drilled passages to the main bearings and crankpin bearings.
  • A thin oil film separates the moving metal surfaces.
  • This lubrication reduces friction, wear and heat generation.

Hydrodynamic lubrication:

  • When a journal rotates inside a bearing, it drags oil into a converging clearance.
  • This creates a pressure-generating oil wedge.
  • The pressure supports the applied load.
  • Under suitable operating conditions, the journal is separated from the bearing surface by an oil film.

Common lubrication problems:

  • Insufficient oil supply.
  • Low oil pressure.
  • Contaminated oil.
  • Excessive bearing clearance.
  • High oil temperature.
  • Incorrect oil viscosity.

These problems can cause bearing overheating, wiping, scoring and seizure.

1.7 Introduction to Connecting Rod

  • A connecting rod is a mechanical link connecting the piston to the crankshaft.
  • It transmits the force generated by combustion from the piston to the crankshaft.
  • It converts the linear motion of the piston into the rotary motion of the crankshaft through the crank mechanism.
  • During operation, it experiences alternating compressive and tensile forces.

Main parts of a connecting rod

1. Small end

  • The upper end of the connecting rod.
  • Connected to the piston through the gudgeon pin or piston pin.
  • Allows relative angular movement between the piston and connecting rod.
  • May contain a bush or bearing depending on design.

2. Big end

  • The lower end of the connecting rod.
  • Connected to the crankshaft crank pin.
  • Generally consists of a bearing housing and bearing shells.
  • Often divided into two parts for assembly around the crank pin.

3. Shank

  • The main body between the small and big ends.
  • Commonly designed with an I-section or H-section.
  • Provides high strength with comparatively low weight.
  • Resists bending, tensile and compressive loads.

4. Big-end cap

  • Removable section of the big end.
  • Allows installation and removal of the connecting rod from the crankshaft.
  • Secured using high-strength connecting rod bolts.

5. Connecting rod bolts

  • Hold the big-end cap securely.
  • Must withstand cyclic loading.
  • Require correct tightening procedures and specified tightening torque or elongation.

6. Bearing shells

  • Installed inside the big-end housing.
  • Provide a replaceable bearing surface.
  • Support the crank pin through an oil film.

1.8 Forces Acting on a Connecting Rod

1. Compressive force

  • During the power stroke, combustion pressure pushes the piston downward.
  • This produces a major compressive force in the connecting rod.

2. Tensile force

  • During certain parts of the engine cycle, inertia forces can produce tensile loading.
  • Particularly important at high engine speeds.

3. Bending force

  • Occurs due to lateral forces, misalignment and deformation.
  • Can cause bending of the connecting rod.

4. Buckling

  • A connecting rod under compression may become unstable and bend sideways.
  • This is called buckling.
  • Long slender connecting rods are more susceptible to buckling.

Euler's Buckling Formula

For an ideal slender column:

Pcr = π²EI / Le²

Where:

  • Pcr = Critical buckling load.
  • E = Young's modulus.
  • I = Least second moment of area.
  • Le = Effective length.

Actual connecting rod design also accounts for dynamic loading, end conditions, non-uniform geometry and material behaviour.

1.9 Connecting Rod Materials and Design Considerations

Common materials:

  • Forged alloy steel.
  • High-strength steel.
  • Special alloy steels.

Important design requirements:

  1. High tensile and compressive strength.
  2. High fatigue resistance.
  3. Low weight without sacrificing strength.
  4. High stiffness.
  5. Good resistance to impact.
  6. Accurate machining of bearing surfaces.
  7. Secure fastening of big-end cap.

2.1 Introduction to Shaft Alignment

  • Shaft alignment is the process of positioning two or more connected rotating shafts so that their rotational axes are correctly aligned.
  • It is extremely important in marine propulsion machinery.
  • Incorrect alignment produces additional forces and bending moments on shafts, bearings and couplings.
  • Proper alignment improves machinery reliability and reduces vibration.

Examples in marine machinery:

  • Main engine crankshaft and intermediate shaft.
  • Intermediate shaft and propeller shaft.
  • Engine and gearbox.
  • Motor and pump.
  • Motor and compressor.

2.2 Types of Shaft Misalignment

There are four important types of misalignment.

1. Angular misalignment

  • Occurs when the axes of two shafts intersect at an angle.
  • The shaft centre lines are not parallel.
  • Produces cyclic forces during rotation.
  • Can cause vibration and coupling wear.

2. Parallel or offset misalignment

  • Occurs when two shaft axes are parallel but displaced from each other.
  • The shafts have no common centre line.
  • Produces additional radial loads on bearings and couplings.

3. Combined misalignment

  • Combination of angular and parallel misalignment.
  • Common in actual machinery installations.
  • Requires careful correction.

4. Axial misalignment

  • Occurs when the shafts are positioned incorrectly along their axial direction.
  • Can cause excessive axial loading.
  • May affect coupling clearances and thrust-bearing loading.

2.3 Causes of Shaft Misalignment

  1. Incorrect machinery installation.
  2. Foundation settlement.
  3. Thermal expansion of machinery.
  4. Improper tightening of foundation bolts.
  5. Wear of bearings.
  6. Shaft deformation.
  7. Structural distortion.
  8. Incorrect coupling assembly.
  9. Changes in ship loading and hull deflection.
  10. Incorrect maintenance practices.
Marine-specific consideration: A ship's hull is not perfectly rigid. Hull deflection and structural deformation can change the relative positions of the main engine, shaft bearings and stern tube. This is one reason marine shaft alignment must consider the operating condition of the vessel.

2.4 Effects of Incorrect Shaft Alignment

  • Excessive vibration.
  • Increased bearing temperature.
  • Premature bearing failure.
  • Coupling damage.
  • Increased power losses.
  • Seal leakage.
  • Increased shaft bending stress.
  • Noise.
  • Fatigue cracks.
  • Damage to connected machinery.

2.5 Shaft Alignment Methods

1. Straightedge and feeler gauge method

  • A straightedge is placed across the coupling rims.
  • A feeler gauge measures gaps.
  • Suitable mainly for preliminary or basic alignment checks.
  • Has limited accuracy compared with modern methods.

2. Dial indicator method

  • Dial indicators measure relative shaft displacement.
  • Can be used to identify parallel and angular misalignment.
  • Requires careful measurement at different shaft positions.

3. Laser alignment method

  • Uses laser transmitters and detectors.
  • Measures shaft position accurately.
  • Calculates horizontal and vertical corrections.
  • Commonly used in modern machinery installation and maintenance.

4. Optical alignment

  • Uses optical instruments to establish shaft centre lines.
  • Particularly useful in long shafting arrangements.

2.6 Important Shaft Alignment Terms

Soft foot

  • Occurs when one or more machinery feet do not sit properly on the foundation.
  • Tightening foundation bolts can distort the machine casing.
  • Causes alignment changes.
  • Corrected by proper shimming and foundation inspection.

Cold alignment

  • Alignment performed when machinery is at ambient or relatively low temperature.
  • Adjustments are made considering expected thermal growth.

Hot alignment

  • Alignment condition when machinery reaches its operating temperature.
  • Thermal expansion changes the relative position of the shaft centre lines.

Shaft runout

  • The amount of deviation of a rotating shaft surface from its true rotational axis.
  • Excessive runout may indicate bending, eccentricity or machining defects.

Bearing offset

  • The vertical or horizontal displacement of a bearing centre relative to the intended shaft centre line.

2.7 Introduction to Coupling Design

  • A coupling is a mechanical device used to connect two shafts together.
  • It transmits torque from one shaft to another.
  • It can also accommodate limited misalignment depending on the coupling design.

Main functions of a coupling

  • Transmit rotary power.
  • Connect two shafts.
  • Accommodate limited angular or parallel misalignment.
  • Reduce shock transmission in suitable designs.
  • Facilitate maintenance and dismantling.
  • Provide a reliable mechanical connection.

2.8 Types of Couplings

A. Rigid couplings

  • Connect two shafts without allowing significant relative movement.
  • Require accurate shaft alignment.
  • Used where shafts must behave as a continuous shaft.

Types:

  1. Sleeve or muff coupling.
  2. Split muff coupling.
  3. Flange coupling.

Flange coupling:

  • Consists of two flanges connected by bolts.
  • Each flange is mounted on a shaft.
  • Torque is transmitted through the flange connection and bolts.
  • Common in marine shafting arrangements.

B. Flexible couplings

  • Allow limited relative movement between connected shafts.
  • Accommodate small amounts of angular, parallel and axial misalignment depending on design.
  • Can reduce transmission of vibration and shock.

Types:

  1. Jaw coupling.
  2. Tyre coupling.
  3. Gear coupling.
  4. Grid coupling.
  5. Disc coupling.
  6. Elastomeric coupling.

C. Fluid coupling

  • Transmits power using a fluid.
  • Consists mainly of an impeller and runner.
  • Power is transferred through circulating fluid.
  • Allows a degree of slip between input and output shafts.
  • Used in certain marine propulsion and industrial drive systems.

2.9 Flange Coupling Design Fundamentals

A flange coupling is especially important in marine shafting.

Main components:

  • Two flanges.
  • Coupling bolts.
  • Shaft ends.
  • Hub sections.
  • Key or interference fit, depending on design.

Important design considerations:

  1. Torque transmission capacity.
  2. Bolt strength.
  3. Flange thickness.
  4. Shaft diameter.
  5. Key strength, if applicable.
  6. Fatigue resistance.
  7. Alignment accuracy.
  8. Corrosion protection.

Torque transmitted by a shaft

For a solid circular shaft:

T = (π/16) τ d³

Where:

  • T = Torque (N·mm).
  • τ = Allowable shear stress (N/mm²).
  • d = Shaft diameter (mm).

Rearranging:

d = ³√(16T / πτ)

Bolt load in flange coupling

If there are n bolts equally spaced on a pitch circle of diameter Dp:

T = n Fb (Dp / 2)

Therefore:

Fb = 2T / (n Dp)

Where:

  • Fb = Tangential load per bolt.
  • n = Number of bolts.
  • Dp = Bolt pitch circle diameter.

For bolts carrying shear:

τb = Fb / A

For a circular bolt:

A = (π db²) / 4

Actual flange coupling design must also consider bolt preload, joint friction, fatigue, flange stresses and applicable classification requirements.

2.10 Marine Propulsion Shafting Arrangement

A typical marine propulsion shafting arrangement includes:

1. Main engine

  • Produces mechanical power.
  • Drives the propulsion shafting system.

2. Thrust shaft / thrust collar arrangement

  • Transfers propeller thrust to the thrust bearing, depending on the shafting arrangement.

3. Thrust bearing

  • Transfers axial propeller thrust to the ship's structure.
  • Prevents excessive axial movement of the shaft.

4. Intermediate shaft

  • Connects the engine-side shafting to the propeller shaft.
  • Transmits torque.

5. Stern tube

  • Provides support and passage for the propeller shaft through the hull.
  • Contains bearing and sealing arrangements.

6. Stern tube bearing

  • Supports the propeller shaft.
  • Helps maintain its position and alignment.

7. Propeller shaft

  • Transmits rotary power to the propeller.

8. Propeller

  • Converts rotational power into thrust by accelerating water.
Important: The exact shafting configuration varies between direct-drive engines, geared propulsion systems and other propulsion arrangements.

3.1 Introduction to Bearings

  • A bearing is a machine element that supports a rotating or moving component.
  • It reduces friction between moving surfaces.
  • It supports radial loads, axial loads or a combination of both.
  • Bearings are essential in marine diesel engines, pumps, turbines, compressors and shafting systems.

Main functions of a bearing

  • Support rotating shafts.
  • Reduce friction.
  • Maintain shaft alignment.
  • Carry mechanical loads.
  • Reduce wear.
  • Maintain smooth rotation.
  • Transfer forces to the machinery structure.

3.2 Types of Bearings

Bearings are broadly divided into two main categories.

A. Sliding contact bearings

  • Also called plain bearings.
  • The shaft slides over a lubricated bearing surface.
  • Relative motion occurs between the journal and bearing.
  • Often used in large marine diesel engines.

Types:

  1. Journal bearing.
  2. Thrust bearing.
  3. Footstep bearing.
  4. Bush bearing.

Journal bearing:

  • Supports radial loads.
  • The shaft rotates inside a bearing.
  • Lubricating oil creates a supporting film.

Thrust bearing:

  • Supports axial loads.
  • Prevents excessive axial movement.
  • Used in marine propulsion systems to transfer propeller thrust to the hull.

B. Rolling contact bearings

  • Also called antifriction bearings.
  • Use rolling elements between inner and outer races.
  • Reduce sliding friction.

Types:

  1. Ball bearing.
  2. Cylindrical roller bearing.
  3. Tapered roller bearing.
  4. Spherical roller bearing.
  5. Needle roller bearing.

3.3 Types of Bearing Loads

1. Radial load

  • Acts perpendicular to the shaft axis.
  • Produces a load in the radial direction.
  • Commonly occurs due to shaft weight, belt tension, gear forces and combustion-related reactions.

2. Axial load

  • Acts parallel to the shaft axis.
  • Also called thrust load.
  • In marine propulsion, the propeller produces axial thrust.
  • The thrust bearing transfers this load to the ship's structure.

3. Combined load

  • Occurs when a bearing carries both radial and axial loads.
  • Common in gearboxes, pumps and other rotating machinery.

3.4 Bearing Load Calculations – Basic Concepts

Bearing load calculations help determine the forces acting on a bearing and whether the bearing can safely support them.

Important parameters:

  • Applied load.
  • Shaft speed.
  • Bearing dimensions.
  • Bearing material.
  • Lubrication.
  • Operating temperature.
  • Expected operating life.
  • Vibration and shock conditions.

A. Radial load on a bearing

If a shaft is supported by two bearings and a load acts between them, bearing reactions can be calculated using static equilibrium.

For vertical equilibrium:

ΣFy = 0

For moment equilibrium:

ΣM = 0

For a simply supported shaft with a central load W:

RA = RB = W / 2

Where:

  • RA = Reaction at bearing A.
  • RB = Reaction at bearing B.
  • W = Applied load.

For an eccentric load at distance a from bearing A and distance b from bearing B:

RA = Wb / (a + b)
RB = Wa / (a + b)

These equations are useful for basic shaft and bearing reaction calculations.

B. Bearing pressure

For a plain journal bearing, projected bearing area is:

A = L d

Where:

  • L = Bearing length.
  • d = Journal diameter.

Average projected bearing pressure:

Pb = W / (L d)

Where:

  • Pb = Bearing pressure (N/mm²).
  • W = Radial load (N).

Importance:

  • Excessive bearing pressure increases the risk of overheating and wear.
  • Bearing material and lubrication must be selected according to operating conditions.
  • Actual pressure distribution is not uniform, so this is an average projected pressure.

C. Bearing surface velocity

For a rotating journal:

V = π d N / 60

Where:

  • V = Surface velocity (m/s).
  • d = Journal diameter (m).
  • N = Rotational speed (RPM).

D. PV value

The PV value is the product of bearing pressure and sliding velocity.

PV = Pb × V

Where:

  • Pb = Bearing pressure.
  • V = Sliding velocity.

Importance:

  • Used as a preliminary bearing selection and performance parameter.
  • Higher PV means greater heat generation potential.
  • The permissible PV value depends on bearing material, lubrication and cooling.

3.5 Rolling Bearing Load Calculations

Rolling bearings are commonly selected using equivalent dynamic bearing load and bearing life.

A. Equivalent dynamic bearing load

P = X Fr + Y Fa

Where:

  • P = Equivalent dynamic bearing load.
  • Fr = Radial load.
  • Fa = Axial load.
  • X = Radial load factor.
  • Y = Axial load factor.

The values of X and Y depend on bearing type and manufacturer specifications.

For a purely radial load, the equivalent load may be simplified to:

P = Fr

This is valid when the bearing's selection conditions allow the axial load to be neglected.

B. Basic dynamic load rating

  • Represented by C.
  • Indicates the bearing's ability to withstand repeated dynamic loading.
  • Obtained from bearing manufacturer catalogues.

C. Basic static load rating

  • Represented by C0.
  • Indicates the bearing's capacity under static or very slow loading.
  • Important when bearings experience shock loads or stationary loading.

D. Bearing life

The basic rating life is expressed as:

L10 = (C / P)p

Where:

  • L10 = Basic rating life in millions of revolutions.
  • C = Basic dynamic load rating.
  • P = Equivalent dynamic bearing load.
  • p = Life exponent.

For ball bearings:

p = 3

For roller bearings:

p = 10/3

Meaning of L10:

  • It is the life that 90% of a sufficiently large group of identical bearings are expected to reach or exceed under specified conditions.
  • It is a statistical rating, not a guarantee that an individual bearing will last for that duration.

E. Bearing life in operating hours

L10h = (106 / (60 N)) (C / P)p

Where:

  • L10h = Basic rating life in hours.
  • N = Bearing speed in RPM.

Important factors affecting bearing life:

  1. Applied load.
  2. Rotational speed.
  3. Lubrication quality.
  4. Contamination.
  5. Operating temperature.
  6. Installation accuracy.
  7. Shaft alignment.
  8. Vibration.
  9. Corrosion.
  10. Maintenance practices.

3.6 Lubrication in Marine Bearings

1. Hydrodynamic lubrication

  • A moving shaft creates an oil wedge.
  • The oil wedge generates pressure.
  • This pressure supports the load.
  • Common in crankshaft and other plain bearings.

2. Hydrostatic lubrication

  • Oil is supplied under external pressure.
  • The oil film can support a load even when the shaft is stationary.
  • Useful in certain heavy-duty applications.

3. Boundary lubrication

  • Occurs when the lubricating film is insufficient to completely separate surfaces.
  • Surface contact occurs at microscopic high points.
  • Friction and wear increase.

4. Elastohydrodynamic lubrication

  • Occurs in heavily loaded rolling contacts.
  • Elastic deformation of surfaces and lubricant viscosity changes help form a supporting film.

4.1 Introduction to Thermal Expansion

  • Thermal expansion is the tendency of a material to change its dimensions when its temperature changes.
  • Most engineering materials expand when heated and contract when cooled.
  • Marine machinery operates under varying temperatures.
  • Therefore, thermal expansion must be considered during equipment design, installation and operation.

Examples in marine machinery:

  • Main engine components.
  • Steam pipes.
  • Exhaust gas pipes.
  • Boilers.
  • Heat exchangers.
  • Fuel oil pipelines.
  • Engine foundations.
  • Shafting systems.

4.2 Types of Thermal Expansion

A. Linear expansion

  • Change in length due to temperature change.
  • Applicable to long components such as pipes, shafts and rods.

Formula:

ΔL = α L ΔT

Where:

  • ΔL = Change in length (m or mm).
  • α = Coefficient of linear thermal expansion.
  • L = Original length.
  • ΔT = Temperature change.

Final length:

Lf = L + ΔL

B. Area expansion

  • Change in surface area due to temperature change.
ΔA = β A ΔT

Where:

  • β = Coefficient of area expansion.
  • A = Original area.

For isotropic materials and small temperature changes:

β ≈ 2α

C. Volumetric expansion

  • Change in volume due to temperature change.
ΔV = γ V ΔT

Where:

  • γ = Coefficient of volumetric expansion.
  • V = Original volume.

For isotropic solids:

γ ≈ 3α

4.3 Coefficient of Thermal Expansion

  • The coefficient of linear thermal expansion indicates the change in length per unit original length per degree temperature change.
  • Different materials have different thermal expansion coefficients.

Approximate values:

MaterialLinear expansion coefficient (per °C)
Carbon steel12 × 10−6
Stainless steel16–17 × 10−6
Aluminium23 × 10−6
Copper17 × 10−6
Cast iron10–12 × 10−6
Brass19 × 10−6

Values vary with material grade and temperature.

Important observation: Aluminium expands more than carbon steel for the same length and temperature rise. Different expansion rates can create additional stresses when dissimilar materials are connected rigidly.

4.4 Free Thermal Expansion

  • Occurs when a component is allowed to expand without restriction.
  • No significant thermal stress is produced under ideal free-expansion conditions.

Formula:

ΔL = α L ΔT

Example applications:

  • Freely expanding metal rods.
  • Unrestrained pipes.
  • Components with suitable sliding supports.

4.5 Restrained Thermal Expansion

  • Occurs when a component is prevented from expanding freely.
  • Thermal expansion produces internal stresses.
  • Excessive thermal stress can cause deformation, cracking or failure.

For a completely restrained bar under uniform temperature rise, assuming linear elastic behaviour:

σ = E α ΔT

Where:

  • σ = Thermal stress.
  • E = Young's modulus.
  • α = Linear expansion coefficient.
  • ΔT = Temperature change.

Thermal force:

F = σ A

Where A is the cross-sectional area.

Important: These equations apply to an idealised fully restrained member with suitable elastic assumptions. Actual piping and machinery systems require consideration of flexibility, supports, restraints and boundary conditions.

4.6 Thermal Expansion in Marine Piping

Marine piping systems carry fluids at different temperatures.

Examples:

  • Main engine exhaust gas.
  • Steam.
  • Hot water.
  • Fuel oil.
  • Lubricating oil.
  • Cooling water.

As temperature increases, the pipe expands.

If expansion is not accommodated:

  1. High thermal stresses develop.
  2. Pipe supports may become overloaded.
  3. Flange connections may leak.
  4. Pipe joints may fail.
  5. Equipment nozzles may experience excessive loads.
  6. Supports and foundations may deform.

4.7 Methods of Accommodating Thermal Expansion

1. Expansion loops

  • A pipe is designed with a U-shaped or looped arrangement.
  • The loop provides flexibility.
  • Thermal expansion is absorbed through bending of the pipe.
  • Common in long piping systems.

2. Expansion bellows

  • Flexible metallic elements installed in piping.
  • Absorb axial, lateral or angular movement depending on design.
  • Common in exhaust and hot piping systems.

3. Sliding supports

  • Allow pipes to move along a specific direction.
  • Prevent unnecessary restraint.
  • Maintain proper support while accommodating expansion.

4. Flexible connections

  • Used between connected equipment and piping.
  • Accommodate limited movement and vibration.
  • Must be suitable for the pressure, temperature and fluid involved.

5. Proper pipe routing

  • Provides sufficient flexibility through suitable bends and offsets.
  • Reduces excessive thermal loading.

4.8 Thermal Expansion in Marine Engines

Thermal expansion is particularly important in marine diesel engines.

1. Cylinder liner

  • Experiences high temperatures due to combustion.
  • Expands during engine operation.
  • Must maintain suitable clearance and sealing arrangements.

2. Piston

  • Expands due to combustion heat.
  • Piston clearance must account for operating temperature.
  • Incorrect clearance can cause piston seizure or excessive leakage.

3. Crankshaft

  • Experiences temperature changes during operation.
  • Thermal deformation can influence bearing clearances and shaft alignment.

4. Exhaust system

  • Exhaust gas temperatures are high.
  • Expansion allowances are essential.
  • Expansion joints help reduce thermal stresses.

5. Engine foundation

  • Temperature changes and structural deformation can affect the relative position of machinery.
  • Alignment must account for expected thermal growth.

4.9 Thermal Expansion and Shaft Alignment

  • Marine engines and shafting systems operate at different temperatures.
  • When an engine heats up, its structure expands.
  • The shaft centre line may move vertically or horizontally.
  • This can change the alignment between the engine and connected shafting.

Therefore:

  • Cold alignment values must account for thermal growth.
  • Alignment should be performed according to manufacturer specifications.
  • Expected operating temperatures must be considered.
  • Incorrect thermal allowance may cause bearing overload and vibration.

4.10 Thermal Insulation

  • Thermal insulation reduces heat transfer between surfaces and surroundings.
  • Used in steam pipes, exhaust systems and hot machinery.
  • Protects personnel from hot surfaces.
  • Reduces heat losses.
  • Helps maintain process temperatures.

Common insulation materials:

  • Mineral wool.
  • Glass wool.
  • Ceramic fibre.
  • Calcium silicate.
  • Other suitable high-temperature insulation materials.
Important point to remember: Thermal expansion allowance is the provision made in a design to accommodate dimensional changes caused by temperature variations without creating excessive stresses or damaging connected equipment.

5.1 Introduction

  • Pressure-containing equipment refers to machinery and vessels designed to hold fluids or gases at pressures different from atmospheric pressure.
  • These components must withstand internal or external pressure without leakage, excessive deformation or failure.
  • They are extremely important in marine engineering.

Examples of marine pressure-containing equipment:

  1. Marine boilers.
  2. Air receivers.
  3. Compressed air cylinders.
  4. Heat exchangers.
  5. Pressure filters.
  6. Fuel oil separators.
  7. Hydraulic accumulators.
  8. Pressure pipelines.
  9. Freshwater generators.
  10. Condensers and associated pressure housings.

5.2 Basic Pressure Terminology

1. Pressure

Pressure is force acting per unit area.

P = F / A

Where:

  • P = Pressure.
  • F = Force.
  • A = Area.

SI unit:

1 Pa = 1 N/m²

Common engineering units:

  • Pascal (Pa).
  • Kilopascal (kPa).
  • Megapascal (MPa).
  • Bar.
1 bar = 100 000 Pa
1 MPa = 10 bar

2. Absolute pressure

  • Measured relative to a perfect vacuum.

3. Gauge pressure

  • Measured relative to atmospheric pressure.

Relationship:

Pabsolute = Pgauge + Patmospheric

4. Vacuum pressure

  • Pressure below atmospheric pressure.
  • Expressed as the difference between atmospheric pressure and absolute pressure.

5. Design pressure

  • The pressure selected for equipment design.
  • It accounts for the intended operating conditions and relevant design requirements.

6. Operating pressure

  • The pressure at which equipment normally operates.

7. Maximum allowable working pressure (MAWP)

  • The maximum pressure permitted at the specified design temperature under the applicable construction code.
  • Must not be exceeded during operation.

5.3 Types of Pressure Vessels

A. Cylindrical pressure vessels

  • Have a cylindrical shell with suitable end closures.
  • Commonly used for air receivers, boilers and storage vessels.
  • Efficient for many pressure applications.

B. Spherical pressure vessels

  • Have a spherical shape.
  • Provide efficient pressure resistance because stress is distributed uniformly.
  • Require more complicated fabrication.
  • Used in certain high-pressure storage applications.

C. Horizontal pressure vessels

  • Installed horizontally.
  • Common in air receivers and process equipment.

D. Vertical pressure vessels

  • Installed vertically.
  • Used where space or process requirements favour vertical orientation.

5.4 Important Parts of a Pressure Vessel

1. Shell

  • Main cylindrical or spherical body.
  • Contains the pressurised fluid.

2. Heads or end closures

  • Close the ends of the vessel.
  • May be hemispherical, ellipsoidal, torispherical or flat, depending on the design.

3. Nozzles

  • Connections for inlet, outlet, drainage, instrumentation and other purposes.

4. Manhole

  • Opening used for inspection, cleaning and maintenance.

5. Flanges

  • Used to connect pipes, valves and equipment.

6. Supports

  • Carry the vessel's weight and operational loads.
  • Common support types include saddles, skirts and legs.

7. Safety valves

  • Protect pressure equipment from excessive pressure.
  • Open at a specified pressure to release fluid or gas.

8. Pressure gauges

  • Indicate internal pressure.

9. Drain and vent connections

  • Allow removal of accumulated liquid and trapped gases.

5.5 Thin Cylindrical Pressure Vessel

  • A vessel is generally treated as thin-walled when its wall thickness is small compared with its internal diameter.
  • A commonly used preliminary condition is:
t / d ≤ 0.1

Where:

  • t = Wall thickness.
  • d = Internal diameter.

In thin-wall analysis, radial stress is small compared with the principal membrane stresses and is commonly neglected for basic calculations.

There are two major stresses.

A. Hoop stress (Circumferential stress)

  • Acts around the circumference of the cylindrical vessel.
  • Tends to split the cylinder along its length.

For a thin cylindrical vessel:

σh = P d / (2 t)

Where:

  • σh = Hoop stress.
  • P = Internal pressure.
  • d = Internal diameter.
  • t = Wall thickness.

B. Longitudinal stress

  • Acts along the length of the cylinder.
  • Tends to separate the end closures from the cylindrical shell.
σl = P d / (4 t)

Important relationship:

σh = 2 σl

Therefore, for a thin cylindrical vessel under internal pressure, hoop stress is twice the longitudinal stress.

5.6 Thin Spherical Pressure Vessel

  • A spherical vessel has uniform membrane stress in all directions under ideal internal pressure conditions.
  • The stress is lower than the hoop stress in a thin cylindrical vessel of the same diameter and thickness.

Formula:

σ = P d / (4 t)

Where:

  • σ = Membrane stress.
  • P = Internal pressure.
  • d = Internal diameter.
  • t = Wall thickness.

Comparison:

Cylindrical vesselSpherical vessel
Has hoop and longitudinal stressesHas equal membrane stresses in all directions
Hoop stress is twice longitudinal stressMembrane stress is uniform
Easier to manufactureMore difficult to manufacture
Common in marine machineryUsed in selected high-pressure applications

5.7 Thick-Walled Pressure Vessels

  • When wall thickness is significant compared with the internal diameter, thin-wall assumptions are no longer appropriate.
  • Stress varies across the wall thickness.
  • Radial stress becomes important.
  • Lame's equations are used for ideal thick-cylinder analysis.

For a thick cylinder:

σr = A − B / r²
σθ = A + B / r²

Where:

  • σr = Radial stress.
  • σθ = Hoop stress.
  • r = Radius.
  • A and B = Constants determined by boundary conditions.
Important:
  • Radial stress is compressive under internal pressure.
  • Hoop stress is generally tensile.
  • Hoop stress is greatest at the inner surface for a simple internally pressurised thick cylinder.

5.8 Design Considerations for Pressure Vessels

Pressure vessel design requires consideration of several factors.

1. Internal pressure

  • Main loading condition in many vessels.
  • Produces circumferential and longitudinal stresses.

2. External pressure

  • Can cause buckling or collapse.
  • Particularly important for vacuum equipment and certain submerged systems.

3. Temperature

  • Affects material strength.
  • Produces thermal expansion.
  • May change allowable stress values.

4. Material selection

Materials should have:

  • Adequate tensile strength.
  • Good toughness.
  • Suitable ductility.
  • Weldability.
  • Resistance to corrosion.
  • Appropriate high- or low-temperature properties.

5. Corrosion allowance

  • Additional material thickness may be specified to account for expected corrosion.
  • Depends on fluid characteristics and service conditions.

6. Weld quality

  • Welded joints must satisfy applicable fabrication and inspection requirements.
  • Defective welds can cause leakage or structural failure.

7. Fatigue

  • Repeated pressure cycles can produce fatigue cracks.
  • Particularly important in equipment experiencing frequent pressure changes.

8. Nozzle reinforcement

  • Nozzles create openings in the pressure boundary.
  • Openings reduce the strength of the shell.
  • Reinforcement may be necessary.

9. Support design

  • Supports must withstand equipment weight, fluid weight, vibration and piping loads.

5.9 Pressure Vessel Failure Modes

1. Burst failure

  • Occurs when internal pressure exceeds the vessel's strength.
  • Can cause catastrophic rupture.

2. Leakage

  • Occurs through defective welds, joints, seals or cracks.

3. Fatigue failure

  • Caused by repeated pressure cycles.
  • Cracks may develop and propagate over time.

4. Corrosion failure

  • Material thickness reduces due to chemical or electrochemical attack.
  • Can eventually cause leakage or rupture.

5. Buckling

  • Occurs when external pressure or compressive loading causes structural instability.

6. Brittle fracture

  • Sudden fracture with little plastic deformation.
  • Can occur in unsuitable materials or under unfavourable temperature and stress conditions.

7. Creep failure

  • Time-dependent deformation under sustained stress at elevated temperature.
  • Particularly important in boilers and high-temperature steam equipment.

5.10 Safety Devices Used in Pressure Equipment

1. Safety relief valve

  • Protects equipment from excessive pressure.
  • Opens automatically when pressure reaches its set pressure.
  • Releases fluid or gas.
  • Closes again when pressure falls sufficiently, depending on valve design.

2. Pressure gauge

  • Displays pressure inside equipment.
  • Helps operators monitor operating conditions.

3. Pressure switch

  • Monitors pressure and activates or deactivates electrical control circuits.
  • Used in compressors and automatic pressure systems.

4. Rupture disc

  • A pressure-sensitive membrane designed to burst at a specified pressure.
  • Provides pressure protection through a one-time opening mechanism.
  • Must be replaced after activation.

5. Low-water-level alarm

  • Important in boiler systems.
  • Warns operators when boiler water level becomes dangerously low.

5.11 Pressure Testing

Pressure testing verifies the strength and leak-tightness of pressure-containing equipment.

A. Hydrostatic test

  • Uses water or another suitable liquid.
  • Equipment is filled with the test liquid.
  • Air is removed as far as practicable.
  • Pressure is raised to the specified test pressure.
  • Equipment is inspected for leakage and unacceptable deformation.

Advantages:

  1. Liquid is relatively incompressible.
  2. Stored energy is generally lower than in a comparable pneumatic test.
  3. Leakage can be identified visually.

B. Pneumatic test

  • Uses compressed gas.
  • Generally performed when hydrostatic testing is unsuitable.
  • Has a higher stored-energy hazard.
  • Requires specific safety precautions and an approved procedure.
Important distinction: Hydrostatic testing uses liquid. Pneumatic testing uses gas. Pneumatic testing is generally more hazardous because compressed gas stores significantly more energy.

5.12 Pressure-Containing Equipment in Marine Engineering

A. Marine boilers

  • Generate steam using heat energy.
  • Contain water and steam under pressure.
  • Require reliable water-level control and pressure protection.
  • Must be inspected and maintained carefully.

B. Starting air receivers

  • Store compressed air for starting marine diesel engines.
  • Supply high-pressure air to the engine starting system.
  • Protected by safety valves and other required protective arrangements.

C. Heat exchangers

  • Transfer heat between two fluids.
  • Depending on design, one or both fluid sides may be pressurised.
  • Require suitable pressure-boundary materials and leak-tight construction.

D. Fuel oil equipment

  • Fuel systems may include pressurised pumps, filters and pipelines.
  • Components must withstand the specified fuel pressure and operating temperature.

E. Hydraulic systems

  • Use pressurised hydraulic oil to transmit power.
  • Components include pumps, accumulators, cylinders, valves and pipelines.
  • Require pressure relief and overload protection.
