1.1 What is Machine Design?

  • Machine Design is the process of designing machine elements and mechanical systems so that they perform their intended functions safely, efficiently and economically.
  • It involves selecting suitable materials, determining dimensions, calculating forces and stresses, and ensuring reliability during operation.
  • The main objective is to develop components that can withstand the loads and operating conditions they will experience throughout their service life.

Examples of machine elements

  • Shafts – transmit rotational motion and torque.
  • Bearings – support rotating shafts and reduce friction.
  • Gears – transmit power and change speed or torque.
  • Springs – store and release mechanical energy.
  • Bolts and nuts – connect machine components.
  • Couplings – connect two shafts for power transmission.
  • Keys – prevent relative rotation between a shaft and hub.

1.2 Objectives of Machine Design

  1. Strength: Components must withstand applied forces without failure.
  2. Rigidity: Components should not deform excessively under working loads.
  3. Safety: Components must operate without causing accidents or damage.
  4. Reliability: Components should perform consistently throughout their intended service life.
  5. Durability: Components must resist wear, corrosion, fatigue and deterioration.
  6. Economy: The design should minimize manufacturing, operating and maintenance costs.
  7. Ease of Manufacturing: Components should be easy to manufacture, assemble and inspect.
  8. Standardization: Standard dimensions and components should be preferred wherever possible.

1.3 General Steps in Machine Design

  1. Identify the function and purpose of the machine.
  2. Determine the forces, loads and operating conditions.
  3. Select a suitable material.
  4. Determine the type of stresses developed.
  5. Calculate the required dimensions.
  6. Select a suitable factor of safety.
  7. Check for possible failure under static and fluctuating loads.
  8. Decide the required fits, limits and tolerances.
  9. Check manufacturing and assembly requirements.
  10. Finalize the design and verify its safety and performance.

2.1 What are Design Loads?

  • A load is an external force or moment acting on a machine component.
  • Design loads are the forces and moments considered while designing a component.
  • A machine component must be designed to withstand the maximum expected loads during its operation.
  • Incorrect estimation of loads can result in excessive deformation, cracks or sudden component failure.

Examples in marine engineering

  • Propeller shaft – torsional load due to transmission of engine power.
  • Crankshaft – bending and torsional loads.
  • Connecting rod – alternating tensile and compressive loads.
  • Ship structures – forces due to waves, cargo and vessel motion.
  • Engine foundation bolts – tensile loads and vibration-induced loading.

2.2 Types of Loads

A. Based on the nature of application

1. Static Load

  • A load that is applied gradually and remains constant or nearly constant with time.
  • The load does not change significantly during operation.
  • It produces a relatively constant stress in the component.

Examples:

  • Weight of a stationary machine.
  • Weight of equipment supported by a bracket.
  • Constant load acting on a structural support.

2. Dynamic Load

  • A load that changes with time.
  • It may vary in magnitude, direction or point of application.
  • Dynamic loads can produce additional stresses due to acceleration, vibration and impact.

Examples:

  • Loads acting on a ship's machinery during vessel movement.
  • Reciprocating engine components.
  • Rotating machinery subjected to vibration.

3. Impact or Shock Load

  • A load applied suddenly over a very short period.
  • It produces a high instantaneous force and stress.
  • Impact loading can cause failure even when the same force applied gradually would be safe.

Examples:

  • Sudden engagement of a clutch.
  • Sudden stopping of rotating machinery.
  • Shock loading on a coupling.

4. Cyclic Load

  • A load that repeatedly increases and decreases during operation.
  • It may vary between minimum and maximum values.
  • Repeated cyclic loading can cause fatigue failure.

Examples:

  • Crankshafts.
  • Connecting rods.
  • Rotating shafts.
  • Engine valve springs.

B. Based on the direction of application

1. Tensile Load

  • A pulling force that tends to stretch a component.
  • It produces tensile stress.
  • The component tends to increase in length.

Example: A connecting rod under tensile force.

2. Compressive Load

  • A pushing force that tends to shorten a component.
  • It produces compressive stress.
  • Slender components may fail by buckling under compression.

Example: A column or engine support.

3. Shear Load

  • A force acting parallel to a cross-section.
  • It tends to cause one part of a component to slide relative to another.

Example: A bolt subjected to transverse force.

4. Bending Load

  • A load that causes a component to bend.
  • It produces tensile stress on one side and compressive stress on the opposite side.
  • The stress is generally maximum at the outermost fibres.

Example: A shaft carrying a pulley or gear.

5. Torsional Load

  • A twisting moment or torque acting on a component.
  • It produces shear stress.
  • It is commonly encountered in rotating shafts.

Example: Marine propulsion shaft transmitting engine power to the propeller.

2.3 Important Load Parameters

For fluctuating or cyclic loads, the following parameters are important.

1. Maximum Load (Fmax)

  • The highest load experienced by a component during a loading cycle.

2. Minimum Load (Fmin)

  • The lowest load experienced during a loading cycle.

3. Mean Load (Fm)

The average of maximum and minimum loads.

Fm = Fmax + Fmin2

4. Alternating Load (Fa)

Half the difference between maximum and minimum loads.

Fa = Fmax − Fmin2

5. Load Ratio (R)

R = FminFmax
  • It indicates the relationship between minimum and maximum loads.

The same concepts are used for fluctuating stress.

σm = σmax + σmin2
σa = σmax − σmin2

Where:

  • σm = Mean stress.
  • σa = Alternating stress.
  • σmax = Maximum stress.
  • σmin = Minimum stress.

2.4 Types of Stress Developed in Machine Components

1. Tensile Stress

  • Developed when a component is subjected to a pulling force.
  • It tends to elongate the material.
σt = PA

2. Compressive Stress

  • Developed when a component is subjected to a compressive force.
  • It tends to shorten the material.
σc = PA

3. Shear Stress

  • Developed when forces act parallel to a cross-section.
τ = FA

4. Bending Stress

  • Developed when a component is subjected to a bending moment.
σb = MyI

Where:

  • M = Bending moment.
  • y = Distance from neutral axis.
  • I = Second moment of area.

5. Torsional Shear Stress

  • Developed when a shaft is subjected to torque.
τ = TrJ

Where:

  • T = Applied torque.
  • r = Radial distance from the centre.
  • J = Polar moment of inertia.

2.5 What is Allowable Stress?

  • Allowable stress is the maximum stress permitted in a component during normal operation.
  • It is also called working stress or design stress in traditional machine design terminology.
  • It is selected below the material's limiting strength to provide a margin of safety.
  • The allowable stress depends on material properties, loading conditions, factor of safety and applicable design standards.

The basic relationship is:

σallow = σfailureFOS

For a ductile material under static loading, the yield strength is commonly used:

σallow = SyFOS

For a brittle material under static loading, the ultimate tensile strength may be used:

σallow = SutFOS

Where:

  • Sy = Yield strength.
  • Sut = Ultimate tensile strength.
  • FOS = Factor of Safety.

Important points

  • Allowable stress is always lower than the selected failure strength.
  • It prevents permanent deformation or fracture under the specified design conditions.
  • Different loading conditions require different allowable stresses.
  • For fatigue loading, fatigue strength and mean-stress effects must also be considered.

2.6 Stress Concentration

  • Stress concentration is the localized increase in stress near geometric discontinuities.
  • These discontinuities interrupt the normal flow of stress through a component.
  • The actual maximum stress near such locations can be considerably higher than the nominal stress.

Common causes

  1. Holes.
  2. Sharp corners.
  3. Keyways.
  4. Grooves.
  5. Threads.
  6. Sudden changes in cross-section.
  7. Notches and scratches.

Stress Concentration Factor (Kt)

Kt = Maximum stressNominal stress
  • Kt is generally greater than or equal to 1.
  • A higher value indicates a greater local increase in stress.
  • It depends on component geometry and loading conditions.

Methods to reduce stress concentration

  • Use rounded corners instead of sharp corners.
  • Provide fillets at changes in cross-section.
  • Avoid sudden changes in diameter.
  • Provide suitable relief grooves.
  • Improve surface finish.
  • Avoid unnecessary holes and notches.
  • Use smooth transitions in shaft design.

3.1 Definition

  • Factor of Safety (FOS) is the ratio of the strength of a material to the actual working stress or applied design stress, depending on the chosen failure criterion.
  • It provides a safety margin against unexpected loads, material defects, manufacturing variations and uncertainties.
  • It ensures that a component does not reach its failure limit during normal service.
FOS = Failure StrengthAllowable Working Stress

For ductile materials under static loading:

FOS = Syσallow

For brittle materials under static loading:

FOS = Sutσallow

3.2 Why is Factor of Safety Required?

  1. Uncertain Loading: Actual operating loads may be higher than calculated loads.
  2. Material Variations: Actual material strength may differ slightly from specified values.
  3. Manufacturing Defects: Components may contain internal cracks, pores or inclusions.
  4. Wear and Corrosion: Material strength may reduce during service.
  5. Unexpected Operating Conditions: Machinery may experience overloads, vibration and shock.
  6. Fatigue: Repeated loading may cause failure even when stress is below yield strength.
  7. Human Errors: Errors may occur during manufacturing, assembly or operation.
  8. Service Life: A safety margin improves the reliability of the component.

3.3 Types of Strength Used in FOS Calculations

1. Yield Strength (Sy)

  • Stress at which a material begins to deform permanently.
  • Mainly important for ductile materials.
  • Used when avoiding permanent deformation is the design objective.

2. Ultimate Tensile Strength (Sut)

  • Maximum engineering tensile stress a material can withstand before necking and eventual fracture.
  • Important in designing brittle materials and in certain failure criteria.

3. Endurance Limit (Se)

  • Stress amplitude that a material can withstand for a very large number of cycles under specified conditions.
  • Used in fatigue design for materials that exhibit a practical endurance limit, particularly many steels.

4. Compressive Strength

  • Maximum compressive stress a material can withstand under specified conditions.
  • Important for components subjected to compression.

3.4 Factors Affecting Selection of FOS

  • Type of material (ductile or brittle).
  • Type of loading (static, dynamic, impact or cyclic).
  • Nature of service conditions.
  • Reliability requirements.
  • Manufacturing quality.
  • Corrosion and environmental conditions.
  • Consequences of component failure.
  • Accuracy of stress calculations.
  • Maintenance and inspection availability.
  • Applicable classification society and engineering standards.

3.5 High and Low Factor of Safety

High FOS:

  • Provides a larger margin against failure.
  • Useful where failure consequences are severe or uncertainties are high.
  • May increase component weight, dimensions and material cost.

Low FOS:

  • Produces a lighter and potentially more economical design.
  • Requires accurate load calculations and reliable material properties.
  • May result in insufficient safety if uncertainties are underestimated.
Important: A higher FOS does not automatically mean a better design. The correct FOS must be selected according to design codes, operating conditions and risk.

3.6 Factor of Safety in Marine Engineering

In marine machinery, FOS is especially important because equipment operates under demanding conditions.

Examples:

  • Propeller shafts must withstand torque, bending and cyclic loading.
  • Crankshafts experience repeated bending and torsional stresses.
  • Pressure vessels and boilers operate under high pressure and temperature.
  • Connecting rods experience fluctuating tensile and compressive loads.
  • Lifting equipment must safely withstand working and occasional overload conditions.
Note: Marine machinery is designed using applicable classification society rules, statutory requirements and recognized engineering standards. A general FOS formula should not replace the specific safety factors prescribed by those rules.

4.1 What is Mechanical Failure?

  • Mechanical failure occurs when a machine component can no longer perform its intended function.
  • Failure does not always mean complete fracture.
  • Excessive deformation, cracking, wear, buckling and loss of functionality are also considered forms of failure.

Common types of mechanical failure

  1. Yielding.
  2. Fracture.
  3. Fatigue failure.
  4. Buckling.
  5. Creep.
  6. Wear.
  7. Corrosion.
  8. Excessive deformation.

4.2 Static Failure

  • Static failure occurs when a component fails under a constant or gradually applied load.
  • The load does not vary significantly with time.
  • Static failure generally occurs due to excessive stress exceeding the material's limiting strength.

A. Ductile Failure

  • Ductile materials undergo considerable plastic deformation before fracture.
  • Permanent deformation occurs after the yield strength is exceeded.
  • Ductile failure is generally associated with yielding.

Examples: Mild steel, aluminium alloys, copper.

Characteristics:

  • Noticeable deformation before failure.
  • Necking may occur during tensile testing.
  • Generally provides warning before complete fracture.
  • Yield strength is an important design parameter.

B. Brittle Failure

  • Brittle materials fracture with little or no plastic deformation.
  • Failure may occur suddenly.
  • Brittle materials generally have limited ability to redistribute local stresses through plastic deformation.

Examples: Cast iron, glass, ceramics.

Characteristics:

  • Very little permanent deformation.
  • Sudden fracture.
  • Crack propagation can be rapid.
  • Ultimate tensile strength is commonly important in static design.

4.3 Static Failure Theories

When a component is subjected to combined stresses, the simple tensile stress formula is insufficient. Failure theories help predict failure under combined loading conditions.

1. Maximum Principal Stress Theory (Rankine Theory)

  • Failure occurs when the maximum principal stress reaches the limiting strength of the material.
  • It is mainly suitable for brittle materials.

Principal stresses are: σ1, σ2, σ3

For a simple tensile condition:

σ1 ≤ SutFOS

Important points:

  • Mainly used for brittle materials.
  • Considers maximum normal stress.
  • Does not directly account for the combined effect of all shear stresses.
  • Useful for materials with low plastic deformation before fracture.

2. Maximum Principal Strain Theory (Saint-Venant Theory)

  • Failure occurs when the maximum principal strain reaches the strain corresponding to the limiting strength in a simple tensile test.
  • It considers the effect of Poisson's ratio.

For a three-dimensional stress condition:

ε1 = 1E [σ1 − ν(σ2 + σ3)]

Where:

  • E = Young's modulus.
  • ν = Poisson's ratio.
  • σ1, σ2, σ3 = Principal stresses.

Important points:

  • Based on maximum principal strain.
  • Takes Poisson's ratio into consideration.
  • Less commonly used in practical machine design.
  • Not as reliable as modern ductile failure criteria for general combined loading.

3. Maximum Shear Stress Theory (Guest-Tresca Theory)

  • Yielding begins when the maximum shear stress reaches the maximum shear stress at yielding in a uniaxial tensile test.
  • Mainly applicable to ductile materials.

For three principal stresses:

τmax = σmax − σmin2

Yielding occurs when:

τmax ≥ Sy2

For safe design:

τmax ≤ Sy2 FOS

Important points:

  • Particularly suitable for ductile materials.
  • Considers maximum shear stress.
  • Commonly used in shaft and mechanical component design.
  • Generally more conservative than the von Mises theory.

4. Maximum Distortion Energy Theory (Von Mises Theory)

  • Yielding occurs when the distortion energy per unit volume reaches the distortion energy at yielding in a uniaxial tensile test.
  • It is widely used for ductile materials.

For plane stress:

σvm = √σx2 + σy2 − σxσy + 3τxy2

Where:

  • σx = Normal stress in the x-direction.
  • σy = Normal stress in the y-direction.
  • τxy = Shear stress.

For safe design:

σvm ≤ SyFOS

Important points:

  • Also known as the Von Mises yield criterion.
  • Widely used for ductile metals.
  • Considers the combined effect of normal and shear stresses.
  • Commonly used in finite element analysis (FEA).
  • Generally gives a less conservative result than Tresca for many combined stress states.

4.5 Fatigue Failure

Definition

  • Fatigue failure is the progressive failure of a material due to repeated or fluctuating stresses.
  • It can occur even when the maximum applied stress is below the yield strength.
  • Repeated loading causes microscopic cracks to develop and gradually grow until sudden fracture occurs.

Examples in marine engineering:

  • Crankshafts.
  • Propeller shafts.
  • Connecting rods.
  • Engine valve springs.
  • Rotating machinery shafts.
  • Ship structural members subjected to repeated wave loading.

Stages of Fatigue Failure

1. Crack Initiation

  • A small crack begins to form at a weak location.
  • Common initiation locations include surface defects, scratches, keyways and sharp corners.
  • Stress concentration significantly increases the possibility of crack initiation.

2. Crack Propagation

  • The crack gradually grows with repeated loading.
  • Each loading cycle can extend the crack.
  • The crack reduces the effective cross-sectional area of the component.

3. Final Fracture

  • The remaining cross-section becomes insufficient to carry the applied load.
  • Sudden fracture occurs.
  • The final fracture may happen without significant warning.

Characteristics of Fatigue Failure

  1. Occurs due to repeated or fluctuating stresses.
  2. Can occur below yield strength.
  3. Usually begins at stress concentration locations.
  4. Crack growth occurs progressively.
  5. Final fracture can be sudden.
  6. Surface finish has a significant influence.
  7. Fatigue strength depends on material, geometry and loading conditions.
  8. Proper design and inspection can reduce fatigue-related failures.

4.6 Types of Fatigue Loading

1. Completely Reversed Loading

  • Stress changes from an equal tensile value to an equal compressive value.
  • Mean stress is zero.
σmax = +σ
σmin = −σ
σm = 0

2. Repeated or Pulsating Loading

  • Stress varies from zero to a maximum value of the same sign.
  • It does not reverse direction.

For a tensile pulsating cycle:

σmin = 0
σm = σa = σmax2

3. Fluctuating Loading

  • Stress varies between unequal minimum and maximum values.
  • Mean stress is generally non-zero.
  • The stress may or may not reverse its sign.

4.7 Fatigue Strength and Endurance Limit

Fatigue Strength:

  • The maximum stress amplitude a material can withstand for a specified number of loading cycles without failure.
  • It is specified for a particular cycle count.

Endurance Limit:

  • The stress amplitude below which a material can theoretically withstand an extremely large number of cycles without fatigue failure.
  • Many steels show a practical endurance limit.
  • Aluminium and many other non-ferrous alloys do not have a distinct endurance limit.
Fatigue StrengthEndurance Limit
Applies to a specified number of cyclesApplies to very high or theoretically infinite cycles
Used for finite-life designUsed for long-life design
Can be specified for different cycle countsA limiting fatigue stress for materials showing an endurance limit

4.8 S-N Curve (Wöhler Curve)

  • The S-N curve represents the relationship between cyclic stress amplitude and the number of cycles to failure.
  • S represents stress amplitude.
  • N represents the number of cycles to failure.
  • It is commonly plotted with stress on the vertical axis and logarithmic cycle count on the horizontal axis.

Important observations

  1. Higher cyclic stress generally results in fewer cycles to failure.
  2. Lower cyclic stress generally increases fatigue life.
  3. For many steels, the curve becomes nearly horizontal at high cycle counts.
  4. That horizontal region represents the practical endurance limit.
  5. Non-ferrous metals generally continue to show decreasing fatigue strength with increasing cycle count.

4.9 Factors Affecting Fatigue Strength

  1. Surface Finish: Rough surfaces have scratches that act as stress concentration points.
  2. Size Effect: Larger components may have lower fatigue strength because of greater highly stressed material volume.
  3. Stress Concentration: Holes, grooves and keyways increase local stresses.
  4. Material Properties: Different materials have different fatigue resistance.
  5. Temperature: High temperatures can reduce material strength and influence fatigue behaviour.
  6. Corrosion: Corrosion pits act as crack initiation points.
  7. Residual Stress: Compressive residual stresses can improve fatigue resistance, while tensile residual stresses can reduce it.
  8. Loading Type: Completely reversed, pulsating and irregular loads produce different fatigue behaviour.
  9. Manufacturing Process: Heat treatment, machining, grinding and shot peening can influence fatigue performance.

4.10 Fatigue Failure Theories and Design Criteria

When a component experiences fluctuating stress, both mean stress and alternating stress must be considered. Two commonly used fatigue design criteria are:

1. Soderberg Criterion

  • A conservative fatigue design criterion.
  • Considers alternating stress and mean stress.
  • Uses endurance limit and yield strength.
σaSe + σmSy ≤ 1FOS

Where:

  • σa = Alternating stress.
  • σm = Mean stress.
  • Se = Corrected endurance limit.
  • Sy = Yield strength.

Important points:

  • Uses yield strength for mean stress.
  • Provides a conservative design.
  • Helps prevent both fatigue failure and yielding.

2. Goodman Criterion

  • A fatigue design criterion that considers the effect of mean stress.
  • Uses endurance limit and ultimate tensile strength.
σaSe + σmSut ≤ 1FOS

Where: Sut = Ultimate tensile strength.

Important points:

  • Commonly used in fatigue design.
  • Generally less conservative than Soderberg.
  • Useful for components subjected to fluctuating tensile stresses.

3. Gerber Criterion

  • Uses a parabolic relationship between alternating stress and mean stress.
  • It is commonly used as an empirical fatigue criterion for ductile metals.
  • It is generally less conservative than Goodman.
σaSe + (σmSut)2 = 1

The above is the idealized failure boundary. For a design with FOS n, a common formulation is:

σaSe + (n σmSut)2 = 1

Important points:

  • Based on a parabolic relationship.
  • Uses ultimate tensile strength.
  • Often provides a closer fit to experimental fatigue data for ductile materials.

5.1 What is Material Selection?

  • Material selection is the process of choosing a suitable material for manufacturing a machine component.
  • The material must satisfy strength, durability, temperature, corrosion and operating requirements.
  • The correct material improves component reliability and reduces maintenance costs.

Examples

ComponentCommonly used materials
CrankshaftForged alloy steel
Connecting rodForged steel
Engine cylinder linerCast iron or alloy cast iron
PistonAluminium alloy or cast iron, depending on engine design
Marine shaftingSuitable forged or manufactured steel
GearsAlloy steel
BearingsBearing steel, bronze or bearing alloys
BoltsCarbon steel or alloy steel
Ship propellerNickel-aluminium bronze or other suitable marine alloys

Actual material selection depends on the machinery design, operating conditions and applicable marine standards.

5.2 Important Mechanical Properties of Materials

1. Strength

  • Ability of a material to withstand applied loads without failure.
  • It may be expressed as tensile, compressive, shear or yield strength.

2. Hardness

  • Ability to resist indentation, scratching and surface wear.
  • Hard materials generally have good resistance to surface deformation.
  • Hardness is important for gears, cutting tools and wear-resistant surfaces.

3. Toughness

  • Ability to absorb energy before fracturing.
  • Tough materials can withstand shock and impact loads.
  • Important for components subjected to sudden loading.

4. Ductility

  • Ability to undergo considerable plastic deformation before fracture.
  • Ductile materials can be drawn into wires.
  • Usually measured by percentage elongation or reduction in area.

5. Brittleness

  • Tendency of a material to fracture with little plastic deformation.
  • Brittle materials have limited ductility.
  • Cast iron and ceramics are common examples.

6. Elasticity

  • Ability of a material to return to its original shape after removal of the load, provided it has not exceeded its elastic limit.

7. Plasticity

  • Ability of a material to undergo permanent deformation without fracture.
  • Important in forming and shaping operations.

8. Stiffness

  • Ability of a material to resist elastic deformation.
  • It is associated with Young's modulus.
E = Normal stressLongitudinal strain

Higher Young's modulus means greater stiffness for the same geometry and loading.

9. Resilience

  • Ability of a material to absorb energy within the elastic range and release it when the load is removed.
  • Important for springs.

10. Creep Resistance

  • Ability to resist slow, permanent deformation under constant stress over a long period.
  • Important at elevated temperatures.

11. Fatigue Resistance

  • Ability to withstand repeated or fluctuating stresses without failure.
  • Important for rotating and reciprocating machine components.

12. Wear Resistance

  • Ability to resist material loss due to friction and contact.
  • Important for bearings, gears and cylinder liners.

13. Corrosion Resistance

  • Ability to resist chemical or electrochemical deterioration.
  • Especially important in marine environments because of seawater exposure.

5.3 Physical Properties

1. Density

  • Mass per unit volume.
  • Important when designing lightweight machinery.
ρ = mV

2. Melting Point

  • Temperature at which a material changes from solid to liquid.
  • Important for components operating at high temperatures.

3. Thermal Conductivity

  • Ability to conduct heat.
  • Important for pistons, cylinder liners, heat exchangers and engine components.

4. Coefficient of Thermal Expansion

  • Measures the change in dimensions due to temperature variation.
  • Important where components operate at high temperatures.

5. Electrical Conductivity

  • Ability to conduct electric current.
  • Important for electrical and electronic components.

5.4 Classification of Engineering Materials

A. Ferrous Materials

  • Ferrous materials contain iron as their principal constituent.
  • They are widely used in mechanical engineering.

1. Cast Iron

  • Contains a relatively high percentage of carbon compared with steel.
  • Has good castability and compressive strength.
  • Generally has good vibration-damping characteristics.
  • Usually has lower ductility than steel.

Applications: Engine blocks, cylinder liners, machine beds, pump casings.

2. Mild Steel

  • A low-carbon steel.
  • Has good ductility, weldability and machinability.
  • Commonly used for general engineering applications.

Applications: Structural supports, brackets, general-purpose shafts and fabricated components.

3. Medium Carbon Steel

  • Contains more carbon than mild steel.
  • Generally offers higher strength and hardness.
  • Can be heat treated to improve its mechanical properties.

Applications: Shafts, gears, axles, machine components.

4. High Carbon Steel

  • Has high carbon content.
  • Can achieve high hardness and wear resistance through suitable heat treatment.
  • Generally has lower ductility and weldability.

Applications: Springs, cutting tools, high-strength wires.

5. Alloy Steel

  • Contains alloying elements such as chromium, nickel, molybdenum and manganese.
  • Provides improved strength, toughness, hardenability or wear resistance depending on composition and treatment.

Applications: Crankshafts, gears, high-strength shafts, heavy-duty machinery.

B. Non-Ferrous Materials

  • Non-ferrous materials do not have iron as their principal constituent.
  • They are used where low weight, corrosion resistance, conductivity or special properties are required.

1. Aluminium and Aluminium Alloys

  • Low density.
  • Good corrosion resistance.
  • Good thermal conductivity.
  • Relatively easy to machine.

Applications: Pistons, heat exchangers, lightweight machine components.

2. Copper

  • Excellent electrical and thermal conductivity.
  • Good corrosion resistance in many environments.
  • Ductile and malleable.

Applications: Electrical wiring, heat exchangers, electrical connections.

3. Brass

  • Alloy of copper and zinc.
  • Good machinability.
  • Good corrosion resistance in many conditions.

Applications: Valves, fittings, instrument components.

4. Bronze

  • Traditionally an alloy of copper and tin, although other bronze alloys contain different alloying elements.
  • Often has good wear resistance and corrosion resistance.

Applications: Bearings, bushes, marine components.

5. Nickel Alloys

  • Good high-temperature strength and corrosion resistance.
  • Used in demanding industrial and marine applications.

C. Non-Metallic Materials

1. Plastics

  • Lightweight.
  • Corrosion resistant.
  • Good electrical insulation.
  • Used in pipes, seals and insulation components.

2. Rubber

  • Elastic and flexible.
  • Good vibration and shock absorption.
  • Used in seals, gaskets and flexible couplings.

3. Ceramics

  • High hardness.
  • Good heat resistance.
  • Generally brittle.
  • Used in insulation and wear-resistant applications.

4. Composite Materials

  • Made by combining two or more different materials.
  • Can offer high strength-to-weight ratios.
  • Used in lightweight structures and specialized engineering components.

5.5 Factors Affecting Material Selection

  1. Strength: Must withstand the applied loads.
  2. Weight: Should meet the weight requirements of the machine.
  3. Cost: Material should be economically suitable.
  4. Availability: Should be readily available in the required size and grade.
  5. Corrosion Resistance: Particularly important for marine machinery.
  6. Temperature Resistance: Must withstand operating temperatures.
  7. Wear Resistance: Important for sliding and rotating components.
  8. Fatigue Resistance: Necessary for cyclically loaded components.
  9. Manufacturability: Must be suitable for casting, forging, machining or welding as required.
  10. Maintenance: Should allow inspection, repair and replacement.
  11. Service Life: Should provide the required durability.
  12. Standards: Must comply with relevant engineering specifications and marine classification requirements.

5.6 Heat Treatment of Materials

  • Heat treatment is a controlled process of heating and cooling a material to modify its mechanical properties.
  • It is commonly used to improve hardness, strength, toughness and wear resistance.

1. Annealing

  • Steel is heated to a suitable temperature and cooled slowly, generally in the furnace.
  • Reduces hardness.
  • Improves ductility.
  • Relieves internal stresses.
  • Improves machinability.

2. Normalizing

  • Steel is heated to a suitable temperature and cooled in still air.
  • Refines the grain structure.
  • Improves mechanical properties.
  • Generally produces a harder and stronger structure than full annealing.

3. Hardening

  • Steel is heated to the required temperature and rapidly cooled by quenching in a suitable medium.
  • Increases hardness and strength.
  • Can increase brittleness if not followed by suitable tempering.

4. Tempering

  • Hardened steel is reheated to a temperature below its critical transformation temperature and then cooled.
  • Reduces brittleness.
  • Improves toughness.
  • Helps achieve a suitable balance between hardness and toughness.

5. Case Hardening

  • Produces a hard outer surface while retaining a comparatively tough inner core.
  • Useful for components subjected to surface wear and impact loading.

Examples: Gears, shafts, pins.

6. Surface Hardening

  • Hardens only the surface region of a component.
  • Examples include induction hardening and flame hardening.

6.1 Introduction

  • In mechanical engineering, two components often need to fit together during assembly.
  • Examples include a shaft fitted into a bearing, a gear mounted on a shaft and a piston fitted inside a cylinder.
  • Manufacturing every component to exactly the same dimension is practically impossible.
  • Therefore, acceptable dimensional variations are specified through limits and tolerances.
  • The relationship between the sizes of two mating components is called a fit.

6.2 Basic Terminology

1. Basic Size

  • The theoretical reference dimension from which limits of size are determined.
  • It is the nominal dimension used for specifying a component.

Example: A shaft may have a basic diameter of 40 mm.

2. Actual Size

  • The measured size of a manufactured component.
  • It is determined using measuring instruments such as micrometers, vernier calipers or bore gauges.

3. Limits of Size

  • The two extreme permissible dimensions of a component.
  • These include the maximum limit and minimum limit.

Maximum Limit: Largest permissible dimension.

Minimum Limit: Smallest permissible dimension.

4. Tolerance

  • The total permissible variation in the dimension of a component.
  • It is the difference between maximum and minimum limits.
Tolerance = Maximum Limit − Minimum Limit

5. Upper Deviation

Difference between the maximum limit and basic size.

Upper Deviation = Maximum Limit − Basic Size

6. Lower Deviation

Difference between the minimum limit and basic size.

Lower Deviation = Minimum Limit − Basic Size

7. Allowance

  • The intentional difference between the dimensions of two mating components at maximum material condition.
  • It represents the tightest possible fit between the mating parts.
  • It may be positive or negative depending on the fit.

8. Basic Hole System

  • A system in which the lower deviation of the hole is zero.
  • The basic hole size is the minimum hole size.
  • Different fits are obtained by changing the shaft dimensions.

9. Basic Shaft System

  • A system in which the upper deviation of the shaft is zero.
  • The basic shaft size is the maximum shaft size.
  • Different fits are obtained by changing the hole dimensions.

6.3 Example of Limits and Tolerances

Consider a shaft with:

  • Basic size = 40 mm.
  • Maximum limit = 40.02 mm.
  • Minimum limit = 39.98 mm.

Therefore:

Tolerance = 40.02 − 39.98
Tolerance = 0.04 mm

Upper deviation:

40.02 − 40 = +0.02 mm

Lower deviation:

39.98 − 40 = −0.02 mm
Remember: Tolerance is always a positive difference between the two permissible extreme dimensions.

6.4 Types of Fits

A fit describes the relationship between the dimensions of two mating components, usually a hole and a shaft. There are three main types of fits:

1. Clearance Fit

  • A clearance fit occurs when the hole is always larger than the shaft.
  • There is always a positive gap between the two components.
  • The shaft can move or rotate freely inside the hole, depending on the application.

Characteristics:

  • Positive clearance.
  • Easy assembly and disassembly.
  • Allows relative movement.
  • Suitable for sliding and rotating applications.

Applications:

  • Sliding shafts.
  • Bearing arrangements designed with clearance.
  • Guide mechanisms.
  • Running fits.
Clearance = Hole Size − Shaft Size
  • Maximum clearance = Maximum hole size − Minimum shaft size.
  • Minimum clearance = Minimum hole size − Maximum shaft size.

2. Interference Fit

  • An interference fit occurs when the shaft is always larger than the hole.
  • The components cannot be assembled by simple free insertion.
  • Force, heating or cooling may be required during assembly.
  • The fit creates pressure between the mating surfaces.

Characteristics:

  • Negative clearance.
  • Tight assembly.
  • Prevents relative movement.
  • Can transmit torque without requiring additional fastening in suitable designs.

Applications:

  • Gear mounted on a shaft.
  • Railway wheels mounted on axles.
  • Bushes fitted into housings.
  • Certain bearing and coupling arrangements.
Interference = Shaft Size − Hole Size
  • Maximum interference = Maximum shaft size − Minimum hole size.
  • Minimum interference = Minimum shaft size − Maximum hole size.

3. Transition Fit

  • A transition fit is a fit in which the actual assembly may have either a small clearance or a small interference.
  • Depending on the manufactured dimensions, the shaft may fit with a slight gap or a slight tightness.
  • It provides accurate positioning and alignment.

Characteristics:

  • May produce clearance or interference.
  • Provides accurate location.
  • Requires controlled manufacturing tolerances.
  • May require light pressing during assembly.

Applications:

  • Couplings.
  • Gear hubs.
  • Precision machine assemblies.
  • Locating components.

6.6 Basic Hole System and Basic Shaft System

A. Basic Hole System

  • The hole is kept at the basic size.
  • The lower deviation of the hole is zero.
  • The shaft dimensions are varied to obtain different types of fits.
  • It is widely used in manufacturing because standard drills and reamers produce standard hole sizes.

Example:

  • Hole minimum size = 40 mm.
  • Hole maximum size = 40.02 mm.
  • Shaft sizes are adjusted according to the required fit.

B. Basic Shaft System

  • The shaft is kept at the basic size.
  • The upper deviation of the shaft is zero.
  • The hole dimensions are varied to obtain different types of fits.
  • It is useful when standard shaft sizes are readily available.

Example:

  • Shaft maximum size = 40 mm.
  • Shaft minimum size = 39.98 mm.
  • Hole sizes are adjusted according to the required fit.

6.7 Tolerance Classification and ISO System

  • The ISO system of limits and fits provides a standardized method for specifying dimensions and permissible variations.
  • It uses letters and numbers to represent tolerance zones and tolerance grades.

1. Fundamental Deviation

  • Determines the position of the tolerance zone relative to the basic size.
  • Represented by letters.
  • Uppercase letters are used for holes.
  • Lowercase letters are used for shafts.

Examples:

  • H – Hole with zero lower deviation.
  • h – Shaft with zero upper deviation.
  • g – Shaft tolerance zone positioned below the basic size.
  • G – Hole tolerance zone positioned above the basic size.

2. International Tolerance Grade (IT Grade)

  • Indicates the magnitude of the tolerance.
  • Represented by IT followed by a number.
  • Lower IT numbers indicate tighter tolerances.
  • Higher IT numbers indicate larger tolerances.

Examples:

  • IT5 – Very fine tolerance.
  • IT6 – Fine tolerance.
  • IT7 – Medium precision.
  • IT8 – Less precise than IT7.
  • IT11 – Relatively coarse tolerance.
Important: The exact tolerance values depend on the basic size range and the specified IT grade.

6.8 Understanding Fit Designations

A designation such as:

40 H7/g6

Indicates:

  • 40 = Basic size in millimetres.
  • H7 = Hole tolerance zone and grade.
  • g6 = Shaft tolerance zone and grade.

Similarly:

  • H7/h6 – Clearance-type fit.
  • H7/k6 – Common transition-fit combination.
  • H7/p6 – Interference-type fit.

These are common examples, but the actual fit and limits should be verified using the applicable ISO tables and basic size.

6.9 Importance of Tolerances in Machine Design

  1. Interchangeability: Allows replacement of components without individual matching.
  2. Accuracy: Ensures components maintain their required dimensions.
  3. Assembly: Makes assembly easier and more reliable.
  4. Performance: Ensures correct operation of moving components.
  5. Reliability: Reduces the possibility of premature failure.
  6. Cost Control: Avoids unnecessary manufacturing precision.
  7. Maintenance: Simplifies replacement of damaged components.
  8. Quality Control: Provides measurable acceptance criteria.

6.10 Measuring Instruments Used for Limits and Tolerances

1. Vernier Caliper

  • Measures external dimensions, internal dimensions and depth.
  • Used for general dimensional inspection.

2. Micrometer

  • Measures small dimensions with high precision.
  • Used for shaft diameter and component thickness measurements.

3. Bore Gauge

  • Measures internal diameters.
  • Used for checking cylinder bores and bearing housings.

4. GO and NO-GO Gauges

  • Used for checking whether manufactured components fall within specified limits.
  • Commonly used in mass production.

5. Slip Gauges

  • Precision blocks used as dimensional standards.
  • Used for calibration and accurate measurement.

6.11 GO and NO-GO Gauge Principle

  • GO and NO-GO gauges are used to check whether a component is within its specified dimensional limits.
  • They are commonly associated with the Taylor principle of limit gauging.

GO Gauge:

  • Checks the maximum material condition (MMC).
  • Should pass over or through the component.
  • For a hole, it checks the minimum permissible hole size.
  • For a shaft, it checks the maximum permissible shaft size.

NO-GO Gauge:

  • Checks the minimum material condition (LMC).
  • Should not pass over or through the component under the specified inspection conditions.
  • For a hole, it checks the maximum permissible hole size.
  • For a shaft, it checks the minimum permissible shaft size.
Remember:
GO gauge must pass.
NO-GO gauge must not pass.

9. Marine Engineering Applications of Design Fundamentals

Understanding these concepts is particularly important when studying marine engines, propulsion systems and auxiliary machinery.

1. Crankshaft

  • Experiences bending and torsional loads.
  • Requires fatigue-resistant material.
  • Fillets reduce stress concentration.
  • Must withstand repeated cyclic loading.

2. Propeller Shaft

  • Transmits torque from the engine to the propeller.
  • Experiences torsional and bending stresses.
  • Requires suitable strength, fatigue resistance and corrosion protection.

3. Connecting Rod

  • Experiences alternating tensile and compressive forces.
  • Requires high strength and fatigue resistance.
  • Buckling resistance is important.

4. Engine Bearings

  • Support rotating shafts.
  • Require suitable clearance and lubrication.
  • Material must resist wear and fatigue.

5. Gears

  • Transmit rotational power.
  • Experience bending and contact stresses.
  • Require suitable hardness, toughness and wear resistance.
  • Correct tolerances are essential for smooth operation.

6. Piston and Cylinder Liner

  • Experience high temperatures and mechanical loading.
  • Require wear resistance and suitable thermal properties.
  • Correct clearance is essential for operation and thermal expansion.

10. Quick Revision – Must Remember

  1. Machine design involves selecting materials, determining dimensions and ensuring safe operation.
  2. Static load remains constant or nearly constant with time.
  3. Dynamic load varies with time.
  4. Impact load acts suddenly.
  5. Cyclic load repeats over time.
  6. Tensile stress tends to elongate a component.
  7. Compressive stress tends to shorten a component.
  8. Shear stress tends to cause sliding between layers.
  9. Bending produces tensile and compressive stresses.
  10. Torsion produces shear stress.
  11. Allowable stress is lower than the selected failure strength.
  12. Factor of safety provides a margin against failure.
  13. Yield strength is particularly important for ductile materials.
  14. Ultimate tensile strength is important for brittle fracture design.
  15. Stress concentration occurs near holes, grooves, keyways and sharp corners.
  16. Fatigue failure occurs due to repeated or fluctuating stresses.
  17. Fatigue cracks generally initiate at stress concentration points.
  18. Fatigue failure has three main stages: initiation, propagation and final fracture.
  19. S-N curves represent stress amplitude versus cycles to failure.
  20. Soderberg uses yield strength.
  21. Goodman uses ultimate tensile strength with a straight-line criterion.
  22. Gerber uses a parabolic criterion.
  23. Tresca is based on maximum shear stress.
  24. Von Mises is based on distortion energy.
  25. Rankine is based on maximum principal stress.
  26. Annealing improves softness and ductility.
  27. Normalizing refines grain structure.
  28. Hardening increases hardness.
  29. Tempering reduces brittleness.
  30. Clearance fit always has a gap.
  31. Interference fit always has a tight fit.
  32. Transition fit can have either clearance or interference.
  33. In the basic hole system, the lower deviation of the hole is zero.
  34. In the basic shaft system, the upper deviation of the shaft is zero.
  35. GO gauge must pass, while NO-GO gauge must not pass.
