2.1 Introduction to Threaded Fasteners

  • Threaded fasteners are mechanical components used to join two or more parts.
  • They generally create detachable joints, allowing components to be assembled and dismantled without damaging the joined parts.
  • Commonly used where maintenance and periodic inspection are required.
  • The fastening action is achieved through mating threads on the fastener and the component.

Main types: Bolts, Nuts, Studs, Screws, Set screws, Threaded rods.

2.2 Bolt

A bolt is a threaded fastener generally consisting of a head, shank and threaded portion. It is normally inserted through aligned holes in the components being joined. A nut is tightened on the threaded end to produce a clamping force.

Main parts of a bolt

  • Head – upper portion; provides a surface for applying tightening torque. Common types: hexagonal, square, countersunk, socket head.
  • Shank – cylindrical portion below the head; may be partially or fully threaded.
  • Thread – helical ridge on the cylindrical surface; engages the internal thread of a nut.
  • Bearing surface – surface below the bolt head that contacts the joined component.

Types of bolts

  • Hexagonal head bolt – most common; general machinery.
  • Square head bolt – larger gripping surface; certain industrial applications.
  • Socket head cap screw – internal hexagonal socket; tightened with Allen key.
  • Countersunk bolt – fits into countersunk hole; flush surface.
  • Flange bolt – integrated flange distributes clamping load.
  • Eye bolt – circular eye for lifting/securing (when appropriately rated).
  • U-bolt – U-shaped; holds pipes and cylindrical components.

2.3 Nut

An internally threaded fastening component, generally used with a bolt or stud. When tightened it creates a clamping force that holds connected components together.

Types of nuts

  • Hexagonal nut – most common; six-sided; tightened with spanner/wrench.
  • Square nut – four-sided; relatively large bearing surface.
  • Lock nut – resists loosening due to vibration; used under dynamic loads.
  • Nyloc nut – nylon insert creates friction against bolt thread.
  • Castle nut – slots on upper surface; used with split pin.
  • Wing nut – two projecting wings; tightened by hand.
  • Cap nut – closed dome end; protects exposed bolt thread.
  • Flange nut – integrated flange distributes clamping force.

2.4 Studs

A stud is a cylindrical metal rod threaded at both ends or along its entire length. Unlike a bolt, it does not normally have a head. One end is screwed into a tapped hole; the other end projects outward and receives a nut.

Advantages of studs

  • Reduce wear on the threads of the main component.
  • Suitable for repeated assembly and dismantling.
  • Useful when the component material is relatively soft.
  • Provide reliable fastening in high-temperature machinery.
  • Commonly used in engine cylinder heads and pressure-containing equipment.

Marine applications of studs

  • Main engine cylinder head fastening.
  • Exhaust manifold connections.
  • Turbocharger connections.
  • Pump casings.
  • Pressure vessel covers.
  • Engine foundation connections.

2.6 Thread Fundamentals

A thread is a helical ridge formed on the external or internal surface of a cylindrical component.

Important thread terminology

  • Major diameter – largest diameter; measured across the thread crests.
  • Minor diameter – smallest diameter; measured across the thread roots.
  • Pitch – axial distance between corresponding points on two adjacent threads (mm).
  • Lead – axial distance travelled by the nut/screw in one complete revolution. Single-start: lead = pitch. Multiple-start: Lead = Pitch × Number of starts.
  • Crest – topmost surface of a thread.
  • Root – bottom surface between adjacent threads.
  • Flank – side surface connecting crest and root.
  • Thread angle – included angle between the two flanks of a thread profile.
  • Helix angle – angle formed by the thread helix with a plane perpendicular to the screw axis.

Types of threads

  • Metric V-thread – ISO standard; 60° included angle; designated by M (e.g. M12 × 1.75).
  • Unified thread – imperial standard; 60° thread angle.
  • Whitworth thread – traditional British; 55° included angle; rounded crests and roots.
  • Square thread – square profile; high efficiency in power transmission; screw jacks, presses.
  • Acme thread – trapezoidal profile; easier to manufacture than square; lead screws, machine tools.
  • Buttress thread – asymmetrical; withstands heavy axial loads in one direction; presses, jacks.

2.7 Right-Hand and Left-Hand Threads

  • Right-hand thread: tightens when rotated clockwise (viewed from head/accessible end).
  • Left-hand thread: tightens when rotated anticlockwise.
  • Right-hand threads are most commonly used.
  • Left-hand threads are used where operating rotation could cause a right-hand thread to loosen.

2.8 Bolt Loading and Stresses

  • Tensile stress: axial pulling force; bolt tends to elongate.
    σt = P / At
    P = applied tensile load; At = tensile stress area.
  • Shear stress: forces parallel to joint interface; bolt tends to be cut across.
    τ = P / As
  • Bearing stress: between bolt surface and hole surface; excessive values cause local crushing or deformation.
  • Fatigue stress: fluctuating loading; can cause failure even when max load is below static strength.
  • Torsional stress: due to torque applied during tightening.

2.9 Preloading of Bolts

Preloading is the initial tensile force introduced into a bolt during tightening. Tightening the nut stretches the bolt; the connected components are compressed, creating a clamping force between the joined surfaces.

Advantages of bolt preload

  • Prevents separation of connected components.
  • Reduces possibility of joint loosening.
  • Improves fatigue resistance when correctly designed.
  • Reduces relative movement between connected parts.
  • Helps maintain sealing in pressure-containing joints.
Important concept: A properly preloaded bolt is already under tensile stress before the external operating load is applied. The external load changes the bolt force and joint compression depending on joint stiffness. Excessive tightening can cause yielding, thread stripping or bolt fracture.

2.10 Bolt Loosening

Common causes

  • Vibration.
  • Repeated cyclic loading.
  • Thermal expansion and contraction.
  • Insufficient tightening torque.
  • Improper thread engagement.
  • Settlement or deformation of joint surfaces.

Methods to prevent loosening

  • Lock nuts.
  • Nyloc nuts.
  • Spring washers (where suitable).
  • Tab washers.
  • Split pins with castle nuts.
  • Thread-locking compounds.
  • Correct bolt preload and tightening procedures.

Marine engineering importance

  • Bolted joints in marine engines, pumps, compressors and piping systems are exposed to vibration and temperature changes.
  • Correct tightening and periodic inspection are essential.

3.1 Keys

A key is a machine element used to connect a rotating shaft to a hub. It prevents relative rotational movement between the shaft and the mounted component, and transmits torque from the shaft to the hub or vice versa.

Keys are fitted into matching slots called keyways – one in the shaft, one in the hub. Torque is transferred through contact pressure and shear resistance.

3.2 Types of Keys

A. Sunk key

Half fitted into the shaft keyway, half into the hub keyway. Commonly used for power transmission.

  • Square key – square cross-section; width ≈ thickness; general shaft-hub connections.
  • Rectangular key – width greater than thickness; larger shaft applications.
  • Gib-head key – projecting head for easy removal; frequent dismantling.
  • Feather key – allows hub to slide axially while transmitting torque; sliding gears and clutches.
  • Woodruff key – semicircular; fits a semicircular recess in the shaft; self-aligning to some extent; smaller shafts and automotive components.

B. Saddle key

Rests on the shaft surface; does not fit into a shaft keyway. Torque transmission depends mainly on friction. Suitable only for comparatively light loads; less reliable than a sunk key for heavy-duty applications.

C. Tangent key

Fitted tangentially to the shaft; commonly used in heavy-duty machinery; suitable for large torques; often used in pairs.

D. Round key

Circular cross-section; fitted into drilled holes in shaft and hub; light-duty applications and positioning.

E. Splined key connection

Uses multiple longitudinal projections on the shaft; torque transmission over several contact surfaces; more suitable than a single key for high torque applications.

3.3 Failure of Keys

  • Shear failure – applied torque causes the key to shear along its cross-section.
    τ = F / (bL)
    F = tangential force; b = width of key; L = length of key.
  • Crushing failure – excessive compressive/bearing stress between key and keyway.
    σc = F / [(t/2)L]
    t = thickness of key.

Important points about keys

  • A key transmits torque between a shaft and hub.
  • A keyway reduces the effective cross-sectional strength of the shaft.
  • Stress concentration occurs near the keyway.
  • Incorrect key dimensions can lead to failure.
  • Excessive clearance can cause fretting and wear.

3.4 Splines

A spline is a series of equally spaced longitudinal teeth or ridges formed on a shaft. These teeth engage with corresponding grooves inside a hub, transmitting torque and distributing load across multiple teeth instead of a single key.

Advantages of splines

  • Higher torque transmission capacity than a single key in many applications.
  • Better load distribution.
  • Improved concentricity.
  • Suitable for sliding axial movement.
  • Reduced risk of localized failure.
  • Useful in heavy-duty power transmission systems.

Types of splines

  • Straight-sided splines – straight sides; common in general power transmission.
  • Involute splines – involute profiles; better load distribution and alignment; modern machinery.
  • Serrated splines – fine, closely spaced teeth; accurate angular positioning.

Spline applications

  • Gearbox shafts.
  • Clutch assemblies.
  • Marine transmission systems.
  • Automotive transmission systems.
  • Sliding gear mechanisms.
  • Propulsion machinery.

3.6 Couplings

A coupling is a mechanical device used to connect two shafts together. Its main function is to transmit torque and rotational motion from one shaft to another. Couplings are commonly used between motors, engines, pumps, compressors and generators. Depending on design, they may also accommodate slight shaft misalignment and absorb shock or vibration.

Functions of couplings

  • Transmit power between two shafts.
  • Connect driving and driven machinery.
  • Accommodate small shaft misalignments.
  • Reduce transmission of shock loads.
  • Absorb torsional vibrations.
  • Allow convenient assembly and dismantling.

3.7 Types of Couplings

A. Rigid couplings

Connect two shafts without allowing relative flexibility; require accurate shaft alignment.

  • Sleeve or muff coupling – hollow cylindrical sleeve; shaft ends inserted; key transmits torque; simple and economical.
  • Split-muff coupling – two halves bolted together; easy to assemble/dismantle; useful where shaft removal is difficult.
  • Flange coupling – two flanges on shaft ends; connected using bolts and nuts; can transmit high torque; industrial and marine machinery.

B. Flexible couplings

Allow a limited amount of relative movement between connected shafts; can accommodate angular, parallel or axial misalignment depending on design; also help reduce shock and vibration transmission.

  • Bushed-pin flexible coupling – two flanges connected using pins with rubber/leather bushes; bushes absorb shocks and vibrations; pumps and motor drives.
  • Oldham coupling – two hubs with slots and an intermediate disc; accommodates parallel misalignment.
  • Universal coupling (Hooke's joint) – connects shafts whose axes intersect at an angle; a single Hooke's joint does not generally maintain constant angular velocity of the driven shaft when operating at an angle.
  • Gear coupling – externally and internally toothed components; high torque; limited angular and axial misalignment; heavy-duty industrial machinery.
  • Jaw coupling – two hubs with interlocking jaws and an elastomeric element; absorbs vibration and shock; pumps, fans, compressors.
  • Tyre coupling – flexible rubber tyre element between two flanges; good shock absorption; accommodates different types of misalignment.

3.8 Coupling Misalignment

  • Angular misalignment – shaft axes intersect at an angle; shafts not parallel.
  • Parallel (offset) misalignment – shaft axes are parallel but displaced from each other.
  • Axial misalignment – shaft ends displaced along their common axis; gap or excessive axial separation.

Important marine engineering point

  • Misalignment between the main engine, gearbox and propeller shaft can produce excessive vibration.
  • It can increase bearing loads and coupling stresses.
  • It can damage shaft seals and reduce bearing life.
  • Proper shaft alignment is essential during installation and maintenance.

4.1 Shaft

A shaft is a rotating machine element used to transmit power and torque. It generally supports rotating components such as gears, pulleys, sprockets and flywheels. Shafts are usually subjected to torsional and bending stress, and may also experience axial loads.

Functions of shafts

  • Transmit mechanical power.
  • Transmit torque.
  • Support rotating machine components.
  • Maintain proper alignment of rotating elements.
  • Transfer rotational motion from one component to another.

Types of shafts

  • Transmission shafts – transmit power from one location to another (line shafts, counter shafts, overhead shafts, propeller shafts).
  • Machine shafts – integral part of a machine (crankshaft, camshaft, gearbox shaft, pump shaft, turbine shaft).
  • Solid shafts – solid circular cross-section; general machinery.
  • Hollow shafts – hollow circular cross-section; lighter than solid shafts of comparable outside diameter; efficient strength-to-weight ratio.

4.2 Axle

An axle is a mechanical element used primarily to support rotating components. It generally does not transmit torque and is usually subjected to bending loads. Axles may be stationary or rotate depending on application.

Examples: vehicle wheel axles, railway axles, idler wheel axles.

4.4 Spindle

A spindle is a short rotating shaft. It generally supports and drives a tool or workpiece. Commonly used in machine tools, drilling machines and precision equipment.

4.5 Materials Used for Shafts

Common materials: mild steel, medium-carbon steel, alloy steel, stainless steel, high-strength alloy steel.

Selection depends on: torque transmission requirements, bending loads, fatigue strength, wear resistance, corrosion resistance, operating temperature, manufacturing and cost considerations. In marine applications, corrosion-resistant materials or protective coatings may be required.

4.6 Stresses Acting on Shafts

  • Torsional stress – developed when torque acts on a shaft; causes twisting. Shear stress is maximum at the outer surface of a solid circular shaft.
    τmax = 16T / (π d³)
    T = torque; d = shaft diameter.
  • Bending stress – due to transverse loads; causes the shaft to bend.
    σb = 32M / (π d³)
    M = bending moment; d = shaft diameter.
  • Axial stress – due to axial tensile or compressive forces.
    σa = P / A
  • Combined bending and torsion – shafts frequently experience both; design must account for combined effect; fatigue and stress concentration must also be considered.

4.8 Critical Speed of Shaft

  • Critical speed is the rotational speed at which the shaft's rotational frequency coincides with its natural transverse vibration frequency.
  • At or near critical speed, resonance may occur.
  • Excessive vibration and deflection may result.
  • It can cause serious mechanical damage if the shaft operates continuously near a critical speed.

Factors affecting critical speed

  • Shaft length.
  • Shaft diameter.
  • Shaft material.
  • Mass of mounted components.
  • Bearing support conditions.
  • Distribution of mass along the shaft.

Marine applications

  • Main propulsion shafts.
  • Intermediate shafts.
  • Generator shafts.
  • Turbocharger rotor shafts.

4.9 Crankshaft

A crankshaft converts reciprocating motion into rotary motion. It is one of the most important components of an internal combustion engine. The connecting rod transfers force from the piston to the crankshaft, which rotates and transmits power to the flywheel and connected machinery.

Main parts of a crankshaft

  • Main journals.
  • Crankpins.
  • Crank webs.
  • Counterweights.
  • Oil passages.
  • Flywheel mounting flange.

Important functions

  • Converts reciprocating motion into rotary motion.
  • Transmits engine power.
  • Supports connecting rods.
  • Transfers torque to the flywheel.
  • Maintains engine balance.

4.10 Propeller Shaft

A propeller shaft transmits power from the main propulsion machinery to the ship's propeller. It is an essential part of the marine propulsion system. It is subjected to torsional loading due to torque transmission, and may also experience bending and axial thrust.

Main components in a typical shafting arrangement

  • Main engine.
  • Reduction gearbox, if fitted.
  • Flexible or rigid coupling.
  • Intermediate shaft.
  • Thrust shaft or integrated thrust-bearing arrangement.
  • Stern tube.
  • Propeller shaft.
  • Propeller.
Important: The propeller shaft transmits torque to the propeller, while the thrust-bearing arrangement transfers propeller thrust to the ship's structure.

5.1 Bearings

A bearing is a machine element that supports and guides a rotating or moving component. It reduces friction between moving surfaces, supports radial and/or axial loads, and helps maintain accurate shaft positioning.

Functions of bearings

  • Support rotating shafts.
  • Reduce friction.
  • Carry applied loads.
  • Maintain alignment.
  • Control shaft movement.
  • Reduce wear.
  • Improve machine efficiency and service life.

5.2 Classification of Bearings

  • Sliding contact bearings.
  • Rolling contact bearings.

5.3 Sliding Contact Bearings

Also called plain or journal bearings. The shaft slides over a bearing surface; a lubricating film separates the moving surfaces during normal operation. Friction and wear are reduced through lubrication.

Types of sliding contact bearings

  • Journal bearing – supports radial loads; shaft rotates inside a cylindrical bearing surface; large rotating machinery; marine diesel engines and turbines.
  • Thrust bearing – supports axial loads; prevents excessive axial movement; marine propulsion systems.
  • Footstep bearing – supports vertical shafts; carries axial loads along the shaft axis.
  • Collar bearing – collar mounted on the shaft transfers axial load to the bearing surface.

5.4 Rolling Contact Bearings

Also called anti-friction bearings. Rolling elements are placed between inner and outer races; balls or rollers reduce sliding friction. They generally have lower starting friction than comparable plain bearings.

Main parts

  • Inner race.
  • Outer race.
  • Rolling elements.
  • Cage or separator.

Types of rolling contact bearings

  • Deep groove ball bearing – primarily radial loads; moderate axial loads in both directions; electric motors, pumps, fans.
  • Angular contact ball bearing – combined radial and axial loads; one direction depending on arrangement; high-speed and precision machinery.
  • Self-aligning ball bearing – accommodates angular misalignment; where shaft deflection or mounting errors may occur.
  • Cylindrical roller bearing – high radial load capacity; heavy radial loads.
  • Tapered roller bearing – combined radial and axial loads; gearboxes and wheel hubs.
  • Spherical roller bearing – barrel-shaped rollers; heavy radial and axial loads; angular misalignment.
  • Needle roller bearing – long, thin cylindrical rollers; small cross-sectional height; where radial space is limited.
  • Thrust ball bearing – mainly axial loads; not suitable for significant radial loads.

5.5 Radial and Axial Loads

  • Radial load – acts perpendicular to the shaft axis; pushes the shaft sideways; belt drives, gears, pulleys.
  • Axial load (thrust load) – acts parallel to the shaft axis; pushes or pulls the shaft along its length.
  • Combined load – radial and axial loads acting simultaneously.

5.7 Lubrication of Bearings

Lubrication is essential for reducing friction and wear. It creates a film between moving surfaces, helps remove heat, protects against corrosion and reduces metal-to-metal contact.

Types of lubrication

  • Hydrodynamic lubrication – relative motion generates pressure in the lubricant film; load supported by pressure; continuous fluid film; large journal bearings.
  • Hydrostatic lubrication – lubricant supplied under external pressure; supports load even at zero or very low speed; specialized precision machinery.
  • Boundary lubrication – very thin film; some surface contact occurs; starting and stopping conditions.
  • Mixed lubrication – combination of fluid-film and boundary contact; film insufficient to completely separate surfaces.

Common bearing lubricants

  • Mineral oil.
  • Synthetic oil.
  • Grease.
  • Special-purpose lubricants.

Bearing failure causes

  • Insufficient lubrication.
  • Excessive lubrication or incorrect lubricant selection.
  • Contamination by dust, dirt or water.
  • Excessive loading.
  • Misalignment.
  • Overheating.
  • Incorrect installation.
  • Fatigue of rolling elements.
  • Corrosion.

5.8 Bearing Failure Modes

  • Wear – gradual loss of material due to friction.
  • Pitting – small cavities on bearing surfaces due to repeated contact stress.
  • Spalling – larger pieces of bearing surface break away; rolling contact fatigue.
  • Scoring – surface damage from excessive friction or inadequate lubrication.
  • Brinelling – permanent indentations on raceways from excessive static loading or impact.
  • False brinelling – wear marks from small oscillatory movements and vibration while stationary.
  • Corrosion – chemical or electrochemical damage to bearing surfaces.
  • Overheating – friction, poor lubrication, excessive preload or incorrect installation.

5.9 Seals

A seal is a mechanical component used to prevent leakage of fluids or gases. It prevents lubricants from escaping and contaminants from entering machinery. Seals are especially important in marine applications because machinery often operates in wet, corrosive and contaminated environments.

Functions of seals

  • Prevent oil leakage.
  • Prevent water leakage.
  • Prevent entry of dust and dirt.
  • Maintain internal pressure.
  • Separate different fluids.
  • Prevent lubricant contamination.
  • Improve equipment reliability.

5.10 Types of Seals

A. Static seals

Used between two stationary components; no relative movement between sealing surfaces.

  • Gasket – compressible material between two mating surfaces; prevents leakage of liquids and gases; flanges, cylinder heads, pipe connections.
  • O-ring – circular elastomeric ring; fits into a designed groove; deforms under compression to form a seal; pumps, hydraulic systems, valves.
  • Gland packing – packing material around a shaft or valve stem; compressed using a gland follower; controls leakage around moving or reciprocating components.

B. Dynamic seals

Used between components with relative motion; prevent leakage during rotation or reciprocation.

  • Oil seal (radial shaft seal) – prevents lubricant leakage around a rotating shaft; also helps exclude dust and contaminants; gearboxes and pumps.
  • Mechanical seal – prevents fluid leakage around rotating shafts; two sealing faces (rotating and stationary) pressed together by spring force and fluid pressure; centrifugal pumps.
  • Labyrinth seal – series of small clearances and passages; restricts fluid or gas leakage without necessarily making direct contact; turbines and compressors.
  • Lip seal – flexible sealing lip contacts the shaft; prevents leakage around rotating shafts.

Marine applications of bearings and seals

  • Main engine crankshaft bearings.
  • Connecting rod bearings.
  • Turbocharger bearings.
  • Generator bearings.
  • Pump bearings.
  • Stern tube bearings.
  • Pump mechanical seals.
  • Hydraulic system seals.
  • Fuel and lubricating oil system seals.
Important: A failed stern tube seal may allow seawater ingress or lubricant leakage, depending on the arrangement. Such failures can have serious operational and environmental consequences.

6.1 Introduction to Springs

A spring is an elastic machine element used to store mechanical energy. It deforms under an applied load and tends to return to its original shape when the load is removed, provided the elastic limit is not exceeded. Springs are commonly used to absorb shock, control motion and maintain contact between components.

Functions of springs

  • Store and release energy.
  • Absorb shocks and impacts.
  • Reduce vibration.
  • Maintain force between components.
  • Control movement.
  • Return components to their original position.
  • Maintain contact between mechanical parts.

6.2 Types of Springs

A. Helical springs

Made by winding wire into a helical shape; can be designed for compression, tension or torsion.

  • Compression spring – resists compressive force; becomes shorter under load; suspension systems, valves, shock absorbers.
  • Tension (extension) spring – resists tensile force; extends under pulling force; hooks or loops at ends; control and return mechanisms.
  • Torsion spring – resists twisting moment; stores energy through angular deformation; hinges, mechanisms, rotating assemblies.

B. Leaf spring

Multiple flat steel plates (leaves) stacked together; vehicle suspension systems; can carry heavy loads and absorb road shocks.

C. Spiral spring

Flat strip wound into a spiral; stores energy through angular deformation; clocks, measuring instruments, spring-driven mechanisms.

D. Disc spring (Belleville spring)

Conical disc shape; relatively large force with small deflection; clutches, valves, bolted assemblies.

E. Volute spring

Strip of metal wound into a conical spiral; heavy-duty applications; can absorb large amounts of energy in a compact space.

6.3 Important Spring Terminology

  • Free length – total length of an unloaded spring.
  • Solid length – length of a compression spring when all coils are in contact.
  • Spring index – ratio of mean coil diameter to wire diameter:
    C = D / d
  • Mean coil diameter – average diameter of the spring coil.
  • Wire diameter – diameter of the material used to manufacture the spring.
  • Pitch – axial distance between corresponding points of adjacent coils.
  • Deflection – change in spring length due to applied load.
  • Spring stiffness – load required to produce unit deflection.

6.4 Hooke's Law

Within the elastic limit, deformation is directly proportional to the applied load. For a linear spring:

F = kx
F = applied force; k = spring stiffness; x = deflection.

Spring stiffness:

k = F / x

High stiffness → more force for a given deflection. Low stiffness → less force for the same deflection. SI unit: N/m or N/mm.

6.5 Energy Stored in a Spring

A spring stores potential energy when deformed. For a linear spring:

U = ½ k x²
U = stored energy; k = spring stiffness; x = deflection.

6.6 Springs in Series and Parallel

Springs in series

  • Connected end-to-end.
  • Same force acts through each spring.
  • Total deflection is the sum of individual deflections.
  • Equivalent stiffness is lower than the individual stiffnesses.
1/keq = 1/k1 + 1/k2

Springs in parallel

  • Arranged side by side.
  • Each spring undergoes the same deflection.
  • Total load is shared between the springs.
  • Equivalent stiffness is the sum of individual stiffnesses.
keq = k1 + k2

6.7 Spring Materials

  • High-carbon spring steel.
  • Alloy spring steel.
  • Stainless spring steel.
  • Phosphor bronze.
  • Beryllium copper.
  • Special corrosion-resistant alloys.

Selection depends on: elasticity, fatigue strength, corrosion resistance, operating temperature, required stiffness, load capacity and resistance to permanent deformation.

6.8 Spring Failure

  • Fatigue failure due to repeated loading.
  • Permanent deformation due to overloading.
  • Corrosion.
  • Surface cracks.
  • Stress concentration near spring ends.
  • Buckling of long compression springs.

6.9 Marine Applications of Springs

Applications

  • Diesel engine fuel injection systems.
  • Engine inlet and exhaust valves.
  • Pressure relief valves.
  • Governor mechanisms.
  • Vibration isolation systems.
  • Shock-absorbing mechanisms.
  • Mechanical controls and actuators.
Important: In a marine diesel engine, valve springs help return the inlet and exhaust valves to their closed positions after the opening force is removed.

7.1 Introduction to Gears

A gear is a toothed mechanical element used to transmit rotational motion and torque between shafts. Two or more gears mesh to transmit power. Gears provide a definite velocity ratio without slip under normal operating conditions. They can increase or decrease speed and torque, and can also change the direction of rotation.

Functions of gears

  • Transmit mechanical power.
  • Change rotational speed.
  • Increase or decrease torque.
  • Change direction of rotation.
  • Change the direction of power transmission.
  • Maintain a constant velocity ratio for correctly designed gear pairs.

7.2 Important Gear Terminology

  • Pitch circle – imaginary circle that rolls without slipping against the pitch circle of the mating gear.
  • Pitch diameter – diameter of the pitch circle.
  • Addendum – radial distance between pitch circle and tooth tip circle.
  • Dedendum – radial distance between pitch circle and root circle.
  • Addendum circle – circle passing through the tips of the gear teeth.
  • Root circle – circle passing through the bottom of the tooth spaces.
  • Base circle – circle from which an involute tooth profile is generated.
  • Circular pitch – distance measured along the pitch circle between corresponding points of adjacent teeth.
  • Module – ratio of pitch circle diameter to number of teeth:
    m = D / Z
    m = module (mm); D = pitch circle diameter (mm); Z = number of teeth.
  • Pressure angle – angle between the line of action and the common tangent to the pitch circles; common standards 20° and 14.5°.
  • Backlash – clearance between mating gear teeth; allows lubrication and accommodates manufacturing and thermal variations; excessive backlash causes noise and impact loading.
  • Gear ratio – ratio of teeth on driven gear to teeth on driving gear.

7.3 Gear Ratio and Speed Relationship

For a pair of external gears:

N1 / N2 = Z2 / Z1
N1 = speed of driving gear; N2 = speed of driven gear; Z1 = teeth on driving gear; Z2 = teeth on driven gear.
  • Smaller driving gear → larger driven gear: reduces speed.
  • When speed decreases, output torque generally increases (neglecting losses).
  • Larger driving gear → smaller driven gear: increases speed.
  • For external gears, the two gears rotate in opposite directions.

Power relationship:

P = Tω
P = power; T = torque; ω = angular velocity.

For an ideal gear system: power input = power output; speed and torque change inversely. In an actual system, output power is lower due to losses.

7.4 Types of Gears

A. Spur gears

  • Teeth parallel to the axis of rotation.
  • Transmit power between parallel shafts.
  • Simple construction; easy to manufacture.
  • Can produce noise and vibration at high speeds.
  • Applications: gearboxes, machine tools, industrial machinery.

B. Helical gears

  • Teeth cut at an angle to the gear axis.
  • Suitable for parallel shafts and, in certain configurations, crossed shafts.
  • Gradual tooth engagement; generally smoother and quieter than spur gears.
  • Produce axial thrust due to inclined teeth.
  • Applications: marine reduction gearboxes, automotive transmissions, industrial drives.

C. Double helical gears

  • Two sets of helical teeth arranged in opposite directions.
  • Also called herringbone gears when the teeth form a continuous V pattern.
  • Axial thrust from one half is balanced by the opposite half.
  • Suitable for high-power transmission.

D. Bevel gears

  • Teeth formed on conical surfaces.
  • Transmit power between intersecting shafts; common shaft angle 90°.
  • Types: straight bevel, spiral bevel, Zerol bevel.
  • Applications: differential mechanisms, right-angle drives, marine auxiliary machinery.

E. Worm and worm wheel

  • Screw-like worm and a toothed worm wheel.
  • Usually transmit power between non-parallel and non-intersecting shafts.
  • High reduction ratios in a compact arrangement.
  • Sliding contact produces significant friction and heat.
  • Some arrangements can resist back-driving, but self-locking is not guaranteed.
  • Applications: winches, hoists, steering gear mechanisms, lifting machinery.

F. Rack and pinion

  • Circular gear (pinion) and a straight toothed bar (rack).
  • Converts rotary motion into linear motion or vice versa.
  • Used in steering systems and linear actuators.

G. Internal gears

  • Teeth cut on the internal surface of a cylindrical ring.
  • Mesh with external gears.
  • Used in planetary gear systems and compact transmissions.

H. Planetary (epicyclic) gear system

  • Consists of sun gear, planet gears, planet carrier and ring gear.
  • Planet gears revolve around the sun gear while rotating about their own axes.
  • Different gear ratios can be obtained by holding, driving or taking output from different members.
  • Compact and capable of transmitting high torque.
  • Applications: marine reduction gearboxes, automatic transmissions, industrial speed reducers.

7.6 Gear Trains

A gear train consists of two or more gears arranged to transmit motion and power.

  • Simple gear train – each shaft carries one gear; intermediate gears are called idler gears.
  • Compound gear train – at least one shaft carries two gears rigidly connected together; used for larger speed reduction or increase.
  • Reverted gear train – input and output shafts are coaxial; used in compact speed-reduction arrangements.
  • Epicyclic gear train – one or more gears rotate around another gear; used in planetary gearboxes.

7.7 Gear Failure Modes

  • Tooth breakage – excessive bending stress; overload or fatigue.
  • Pitting – small cavities on tooth surface due to repeated contact stresses.
  • Scoring – surface damage due to excessive friction and inadequate lubrication.
  • Wear – gradual removal of material from tooth surfaces.
  • Scuffing – severe surface damage due to high friction and local welding between contacting surfaces.
  • Plastic deformation – permanent deformation of tooth surface due to excessive contact pressure.
  • Corrosion – chemical deterioration of gear surfaces.

7.8 Gear Lubrication

Reduces friction between mating teeth, prevents excessive wear, removes heat, reduces noise, protects against corrosion and helps increase gear service life.

Common lubrication methods

  • Splash lubrication.
  • Oil bath lubrication.
  • Forced or pressure lubrication.
  • Oil spray lubrication.

Marine applications of gears

  • Main propulsion reduction gearboxes.
  • Auxiliary engine drives.
  • Steering gear systems.
  • Winches.
  • Cranes.
  • Pumps and compressors.
  • Deck machinery.
Important: Marine reduction gearboxes reduce the high rotational speed of the engine to a speed suitable for the propeller while transmitting the required torque.

8.1 Introduction to Permanent Joints

A permanent joint is a connection that cannot normally be dismantled without damaging the joint or connected components. Welding and riveting are two important methods of permanent joining, widely used in structural and mechanical engineering.

8.2 Welded Joints

Welding is a joining process in which two or more materials are permanently joined by heat, pressure or a combination of both. Filler material may or may not be used. Welding creates a metallurgical bond between components.

Advantages of welding

  • Produces strong permanent joints.
  • No holes required for fastening.
  • Can produce leak-tight joints.
  • Generally lighter than comparable riveted joints.
  • Suitable for complex shapes.
  • Can be automated.
  • Useful for fabrication and repair work.
Disadvantages of welding: Requires skilled operators and proper procedures; heat can cause distortion; residual stresses may develop; heat-affected zones can have altered mechanical properties; inspection may be difficult in some configurations; some materials are difficult to weld.

8.3 Types of Welded Joints

  • Butt joint – two components in the same plane; edges joined by welding; plates and pipes.
  • Lap joint – two components overlap; welding along overlapping edges; can use fillet welds.
  • T-joint – one component approximately perpendicular to another; forms a T shape; structural fabrication.
  • Corner joint – two components joined at their edges to form an L shape; box structures and tanks.
  • Edge joint – two parallel components side by side; adjacent edges welded; certain sheet-metal applications.

8.4 Types of Welds

A. Fillet weld

Used mainly for lap, T and corner joints; approximately triangular cross-section; common in structural fabrication.

  • Leg length: distance from root to toe along either joined surface.
  • Throat thickness: shortest distance from root to weld face.

For an equal-leg 90° fillet weld with a flat or slightly convex profile, theoretical throat:

t = 0.707 s
s = fillet weld leg length; t = theoretical throat thickness.

B. Butt (groove) weld

Used to join components edge-to-edge; edges may be prepared with a groove to achieve required penetration; pipes, plates and pressure vessels.

Groove preparations: square groove, single V-groove, double V-groove, single U-groove, double U-groove, single bevel groove, double bevel groove.

C. Plug weld

Circular hole in one component filled with weld metal to join it to the underlying component; used in certain lap joints.

D. Slot weld

Similar to a plug weld but uses an elongated slot instead of a circular hole.

E. Spot weld

Commonly produced using resistance welding; two metal sheets joined at specific points; heat generated by electrical resistance and pressure; automobile body manufacturing.

F. Seam weld

Continuous or overlapping series of welds along a seam; sheet-metal containers and tanks.

8.5 Common Welding Processes

  • Shielded Metal Arc Welding (SMAW) – manual metal arc; consumable flux-coated electrode; electric arc produces heat; flux produces shielding gases and slag; shipbuilding and repair work.
  • Gas Metal Arc Welding (GMAW / MIG) – continuously fed consumable wire electrode; shielding gas protects molten weld pool; production welding.
  • Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW / TIG) – non-consumable tungsten electrode; filler metal may be added separately; high-quality welds; stainless steel, aluminium, thin materials.
  • Submerged Arc Welding (SAW) – arc covered by granular flux; high deposition rates; long continuous welds; shipbuilding and heavy fabrication.
  • Oxy-acetylene welding – heat from burning acetylene with oxygen; welding, brazing and cutting.

8.6 Welding Defects

  • Porosity – gas cavities in weld metal; trapped gases; reduces weld strength.
  • Slag inclusion – non-metallic slag trapped inside the weld; improper cleaning or technique.
  • Lack of fusion – weld metal does not properly fuse with base metal or previous pass; weak joint.
  • Lack of penetration – weld metal does not penetrate sufficiently to required joint depth.
  • Undercut – groove along weld toe; reduces effective thickness of base material.
  • Cracks – fractures in weld metal or heat-affected zone; residual stresses, unsuitable procedures or material conditions.
  • Spatter – small droplets of molten metal scattered around the weld.
  • Distortion – changes in shape or dimensions due to uneven heating and cooling.

8.7 Heat-Affected Zone (HAZ)

The heat-affected zone is the portion of base metal adjacent to the weld. It experiences high temperatures during welding, does not melt, but its microstructure and mechanical properties may change. Changes may include grain growth, hardness variation, reduced toughness, residual stresses and changes in strength.

Marine importance

  • Welding of ship hull structures, pressure vessels and machinery foundations must follow approved welding procedures because weld quality directly affects structural safety.

8.8 Welding Inspection and Testing

Destructive testing

  • Tensile test.
  • Bend test.
  • Impact test.
  • Hardness test.
  • Macro examination.

Non-destructive testing (NDT)

  • Visual Testing (VT) – inspection of weld appearance; visible surface defects.
  • Dye Penetrant Testing (DPT) – penetrating liquid detects surface-breaking defects; non-porous materials.
  • Magnetic Particle Testing (MPT) – detects surface and near-surface defects in ferromagnetic materials.
  • Ultrasonic Testing (UT) – high-frequency sound waves; internal discontinuities.
  • Radiographic Testing (RT) – X-rays or gamma rays; image showing internal defects.

8.9 Riveted Joints

Riveting is a permanent joining method in which two or more components are connected using rivets. A rivet is a cylindrical metal fastener with a head at one end. It is inserted through aligned holes; the projecting end is plastically deformed to form another head called the shop head or closing head.

Main parts of a rivet

  • Rivet head.
  • Shank.
  • Tail.
  • Shop or closing head.

Riveting process

  1. Holes are made in the components to be joined.
  2. The rivet is inserted through the aligned holes.
  3. The projecting end is formed by hammering or pressing.
  4. A second head is created.
  5. The components are clamped together.

8.10 Types of Rivets

  • Snap head rivet – rounded head; structural applications.
  • Pan head rivet – relatively flat top and rounded sides; mechanical assemblies.
  • Countersunk head rivet – fits into countersunk hole; relatively flush surface.
  • Flat head rivet – flat head; low-profile head required.

8.11 Types of Riveted Joints

  • Lap joint – two plates overlap; rivets through overlapping portion; simple to manufacture; can produce eccentric loading because load paths are offset.
  • Butt joint – two plates edge-to-edge with cover plates (straps).
    • Single-strap butt joint – one cover plate on one side.
    • Double-strap butt joint – one cover plate on each side; more symmetrical connection.
  • Chain riveting – rivets in adjacent rows directly opposite each other.
  • Zigzag riveting – rivets in adjacent rows staggered.

8.12 Failure of Riveted Joints

  • Tearing of plate across a row of rivets – tensile stress in reduced net section.
  • Shearing of rivets – shear force across the rivet cross-section.
  • Crushing of rivets or plate – excessive bearing pressure causes local deformation.
  • Tearing of plate at the edge – insufficient edge distance.
  • Combined failure – multiple failure modes together.

8.13 Advantages and Disadvantages of Riveted Joints

Advantages

  • No welding heat required.
  • Suitable for joining certain dissimilar materials.
  • Can be used where welding is difficult.
  • Provides a mechanically fastened permanent joint.
  • Inspection of rivet heads and certain joint conditions is possible.
Disadvantages: Requires holes, which reduce net cross-sectional area; adds weight; requires more labour and manufacturing time; may produce stress concentration; can develop leakage unless properly designed; generally less economical than welding for many modern structural applications.

  • Welding: ship hull construction, deck structures, bulkheads, fuel and water tanks, engine foundations, pipelines, exhaust systems, machinery supports.
  • Riveting: older ship hull construction, historical ships, certain specialized structures and assemblies, selected applications where mechanical fastening is preferred.

11.1 Practical Concepts to Understand Before Joining IMS Goa

  1. Main engine fastening: Understand why cylinder head studs are used instead of ordinary bolts in many engine designs. Learn the importance of correct tightening sequence and preload.
  2. Crankshaft bearings: Understand why proper lubrication is essential. Learn the difference between main bearings and crankpin bearings.
  3. Propeller shaft: Understand how engine power reaches the propeller. Learn the function of intermediate shafts, thrust bearings and stern tube seals.
  4. Pump mechanical seals: Understand how rotating and stationary sealing faces prevent fluid leakage. Learn why dry running can damage mechanical seals.
  5. Gearbox: Understand how reduction gearing decreases speed and increases torque. Learn why gear lubrication and tooth contact are important.
  6. Couplings: Understand the need for shaft alignment. Learn the difference between rigid and flexible couplings.
  7. Springs in engine valves: Understand how springs return valves to their closed position. Learn how spring fatigue can affect valve operation.
  8. Welding in ship structures: Understand the importance of weld inspection. Learn common defects such as porosity, cracks and lack of fusion.

Fasteners

  • Bolt + nut = detachable fastening.
  • Stud = threaded rod without a head.
  • Metric thread angle = 60°.
  • Whitworth thread angle = 55°.
  • Preloading creates tensile force in the bolt and compression in the joint.

Keys and Couplings

  • Key = connects shaft and hub.
  • Spline = multiple teeth for torque transmission.
  • Feather key = allows axial sliding.
  • Coupling = connects two shafts.
  • Oldham coupling = parallel misalignment.
  • Universal joint = angular misalignment.

Shafts and Axles

  • Shaft = transmits torque.
  • Axle = supports rotating components.
  • Crankshaft = reciprocating to rotary motion.
  • Propeller shaft = transmits power to the propeller.
  • Critical speed = resonance condition.

Bearings and Seals

  • Journal bearing = radial load.
  • Thrust bearing = axial load.
  • Ball bearing = rolling contact.
  • Roller bearing = higher radial load capacity for comparable sizes in many applications.
  • Mechanical seal = rotating shaft sealing.
  • Gasket = static joint sealing.
  • O-ring = sealing through elastic compression.

Springs

  • Compression spring = resists compression.
  • Extension spring = resists tension.
  • Torsion spring = resists twisting.
  • Hooke's law = F = kx.
  • Spring energy = ½ kx².

Gears

  • Spur = parallel shafts.
  • Bevel = intersecting shafts.
  • Worm = non-parallel, non-intersecting shafts.
  • Rack and pinion = rotary to linear motion.
  • Helical gears = smoother engagement, axial thrust.
  • Planetary gears = compact high-ratio transmission.

Welded and Riveted Joints

  • Welding = metallurgical joining.
  • Riveting = mechanical permanent joining.
  • Fillet weld = commonly used in T and lap joints.
  • Butt weld = joins edges of components.
  • HAZ = heat-affected zone.
  • NDT = testing without damaging the component.
  • Common weld defects = porosity, cracks, undercut, slag inclusion and lack of fusion.
