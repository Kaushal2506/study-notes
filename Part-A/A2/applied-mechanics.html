1.1 Introduction to Simple Machines

  1. A machine is a device that helps us perform work more easily by modifying the magnitude, direction or speed of an applied force.
  2. Machines are used to:
    • Multiply the applied force.
    • Change the direction of force.
    • Increase or decrease speed.
    • Transmit motion from one point to another.
    • Reduce human effort when lifting or moving heavy loads.
  3. A simple machine consists of a basic mechanical arrangement that makes work easier.
  4. Simple machines generally operate using the principles of:
    • Levers.
    • Pulleys.
    • Inclined planes.
    • Wedges.
    • Screws.
    • Wheels and axles.
  5. Machines do not create energy. They exchange force for distance or speed.
  6. In an ideal machine, the input work is equal to the output work.

Examples of simple machines

  • Lever.
  • Pulley.
  • Inclined plane.
  • Screw jack.
  • Wheel and axle.
  • Wedge.

1.2 Important Terms Related to Machines

1. Load (W)

  • Load is the resistance or weight that a machine is required to overcome.
  • It is usually represented by W.
  • Its SI unit is Newton (N).

2. Effort (P)

  • Effort is the external force applied to operate a machine.
  • It is usually represented by P.
  • Its SI unit is Newton (N).

3. Mechanical Advantage (MA)

  • Mechanical advantage is the ratio of the load lifted by a machine to the effort applied.
  • It indicates how much a machine multiplies the applied force.
MA = W / P

Where:

  • MA = Mechanical Advantage.
  • W = Load.
  • P = Effort.

Interpretation:

  • MA greater than 1: Machine multiplies force.
  • MA equal to 1: Load and effort are equal.
  • MA less than 1: Machine does not multiply force, but may increase speed or distance.

4. Velocity Ratio (VR)

  • Velocity ratio is the ratio of the distance moved by the effort to the distance moved by the load in the same time.
  • It indicates the distance or speed relationship between the effort and load.
VR = Distance moved by effort / Distance moved by load

Alternatively,

VR = VP / VW

Where:

  • VP = Velocity of effort.
  • VW = Velocity of load.

5. Efficiency (η)

  • Efficiency is the ratio of useful output work to input work.
  • It indicates how effectively a machine converts the supplied work into useful work.
η = (Output Work / Input Work) × 100

Since:

MA = W / P

And:

VR = dP / dW

Therefore:

η = (MA / VR) × 100%

Important points:

  • Ideal machine efficiency = 100%.
  • Actual machine efficiency is always less than 100%.
  • Friction, deformation and other mechanical losses reduce efficiency.
  • A machine cannot have efficiency greater than 100% under normal operating conditions.

1.3 Ideal Machine and Actual Machine

Ideal Machine:

  1. An ideal machine is a theoretical machine in which there are no energy losses.
  2. Friction is assumed to be zero.
  3. No energy is wasted due to heat, vibration or deformation.
  4. Input work is equal to output work.
  5. Efficiency is 100%.
MA = VR
η = 100%

Actual Machine:

  1. An actual machine experiences friction and other mechanical losses.
  2. Some input energy is converted into heat, sound and vibration.
  3. Output work is less than input work.
  4. Efficiency is always below 100%.
MA < VR
η < 100%

1.4 Law of Machine

  1. The law of machine represents the relationship between the effort applied and the load lifted by a machine.
  2. It is expressed as:
P = mW + C

Where:

  • P = Effort applied.
  • W = Load lifted.
  • m = Constant related to the machine.
  • C = Constant representing the effort required to overcome friction and other initial resistance.
  1. The value of m is the slope of the effort-load relationship.
  2. The value of C is the effort required when the load is zero.
  3. The law of machine is useful for determining the effort required to lift different loads.

1.5 Reversibility and Self-Locking of Machines

Reversible Machine:

  1. A machine is reversible if the load can drive the machine in the reverse direction when the effort is removed.
  2. The machine can transmit motion from the input side to the output side and vice versa.
  3. Reversible machines are generally more efficient.
  4. They are useful in mechanisms where reverse motion is required.

Self-Locking Machine:

  1. A machine is self-locking if the load cannot descend by itself when the applied effort is removed.
  2. The load remains in its lifted position without requiring continuous external effort.
  3. Friction prevents reverse motion.
  4. Self-locking is important in lifting and holding equipment.

Important relationship for a conventional lifting machine

  • If efficiency is greater than 50%, the machine is generally reversible.
  • If efficiency is less than or equal to 50%, the machine is generally self-locking.

This is a standard result for conventional frictional lifting machines, not a universal rule for every machine design.

1.6 Types of Simple Machines

1. Lever

  • A rigid bar that rotates about a fixed point called the fulcrum.
  • Used to lift or move heavy loads with comparatively less effort.
  • Examples: Crowbar, spanner, seesaw and pliers.

2. Pulley

  • A grooved wheel over which a rope or cable passes.
  • Used to lift heavy loads and change the direction of applied force.
  • Examples: Cranes, hoists and rigging systems.

3. Inclined Plane

  • A sloping surface used to raise or lower heavy objects.
  • Reduces the required effort by increasing the distance over which force acts.
  • Examples: Loading ramps and ship loading arrangements.

4. Screw

  • An inclined plane wrapped around a cylindrical shaft.
  • Converts rotational motion into linear motion.
  • Examples: Screw jacks, vices and mechanical presses.

5. Wheel and Axle

  • Consists of a large wheel connected to a smaller axle.
  • Rotation of the wheel produces rotation of the axle.
  • Examples: Winches, steering wheels and winding mechanisms.

6. Wedge

  • Consists of two inclined planes joined together.
  • Converts applied force into forces acting perpendicular to its inclined surfaces.
  • Examples: Chisels, knives and splitting tools.

2.1 Introduction to Lifting Machines

  1. A lifting machine is a mechanical device used to raise, lower or hold heavy loads.
  2. It reduces the effort required to lift a load by using mechanical advantage.
  3. Lifting machines are commonly used in industries, workshops, construction sites, ships and engine rooms.
  4. These machines may operate manually, electrically, hydraulically or pneumatically.
  5. The main purpose is to lift heavy objects safely and efficiently.

Marine engineering applications

  • Engine room overhead cranes.
  • Provision cranes.
  • Lifeboat davits.
  • Anchor windlass.
  • Mooring winches.
  • Cargo handling equipment.
  • Workshop chain blocks.
  • Stores and spare-parts lifting arrangements.

2.2 Classification of Lifting Machines

1. Manual Lifting Machines

  • Operated using human effort.
  • Usually use gears, pulleys, chains or screws.
  • Suitable for relatively smaller loads.
  • Examples: Chain blocks, hand winches and screw jacks.

2. Mechanical Lifting Machines

  • Use mechanical power transmission systems.
  • May include gears, shafts, drums and pulleys.
  • Examples: Mechanical hoists and geared winches.

3. Electrical Lifting Machines

  • Use electric motors to operate lifting mechanisms.
  • Provide faster and more convenient lifting.
  • Usually incorporate brakes, gearboxes and limit switches.
  • Examples: Electric overhead cranes and electric chain hoists.

4. Hydraulic Lifting Machines

  • Operate using pressurised hydraulic fluid.
  • Work according to Pascal's law.
  • Can produce large lifting forces.
  • Examples: Hydraulic jacks, lifts and hydraulic cranes.

5. Pneumatic Lifting Machines

  • Use compressed air to generate motion.
  • Suitable for certain industrial lifting and handling applications.
  • Examples: Pneumatic hoists and air-operated lifting equipment.

2.3 Pulley Systems

  1. A pulley consists of a grooved wheel mounted on an axle.
  2. A rope, wire or chain passes over the pulley.
  3. It helps lift loads by changing the direction or magnitude of an applied force.
  4. Pulley systems are widely used in cranes, hoists and shipboard lifting arrangements.

A. Single Fixed Pulley

  1. A single fixed pulley is attached to a stationary support.
  2. The pulley does not move along with the load.
  3. One end of the rope is attached to the load.
  4. Effort is applied at the other end.
  5. The main function is to change the direction of the applied force.
  6. It allows the operator to pull downward to lift a load upward.

For an ideal single fixed pulley:

MA = 1
VR = 1
η = 100%
Important: A single fixed pulley does not multiply force. It only changes the direction of the effort.

B. Single Movable Pulley

  1. A single movable pulley is attached directly to the load.
  2. The pulley moves together with the load.
  3. One end of the rope is fixed to a stationary support.
  4. The rope passes around the movable pulley.
  5. The effort is applied at the free end of the rope.
  6. The load is supported by two rope segments.
  7. Each rope segment carries approximately half the load under ideal conditions.

For an ideal single movable pulley:

MA = 2
VR = 2
P = W / 2
Important: A single movable pulley theoretically reduces the required effort to half the load.

C. Combined Pulley System (Block and Tackle)

  1. A block and tackle consists of two groups of pulleys.
  2. One block is fixed to a stationary support.
  3. The other block is attached to the load.
  4. A rope passes alternately around the fixed and movable pulleys.
  5. Multiple rope segments support the load.
  6. The number of supporting rope segments determines the ideal mechanical advantage.
  7. It is used for lifting heavy loads with comparatively smaller effort.

For an ideal system:

MA = n
VR = n
P = W / n

Where n is the number of rope segments supporting the movable block.

Important: The number of pulleys and the number of supporting rope segments are not necessarily the same. The rope arrangement determines the mechanical advantage.

2.4 Velocity Ratio of Pulley Systems

  1. Velocity ratio depends on the number of rope segments supporting the moving load.
  2. When the load rises through a certain distance, the free end of the rope must move through a greater distance.
  3. More supporting rope segments mean less effort is required but a greater rope distance must be pulled.

For a simple ideal block and tackle:

VR = n

Therefore:

dP = n × dW

Where:

  • dP = Distance moved by effort.
  • dW = Distance moved by load.
  • n = Supporting rope segments.

2.5 Chain Block

  1. A chain block is a manually operated lifting device.
  2. It uses a hand chain, load chain, sprockets and gears.
  3. Pulling the hand chain rotates the internal gearing mechanism.
  4. The gear arrangement provides mechanical advantage.
  5. The load chain moves through the mechanism and lifts the load.
  6. A mechanical brake holds the load at the required position.
  7. Chain blocks are commonly used in workshops, ship engine rooms and maintenance operations.

Main components

  • Hand chain.
  • Load chain.
  • Load hook.
  • Suspension hook.
  • Gear mechanism.
  • Chain wheel.
  • Brake mechanism.
  • Housing.

Marine applications

  • Removing heavy engine components.
  • Lifting pumps and motors.
  • Handling spare parts.
  • Supporting equipment during maintenance.
  • Installing machinery in restricted spaces.

2.6 Winch

  1. A winch is a mechanical device used for pulling, lifting or lowering loads.
  2. It generally consists of a rotating drum around which a wire rope or cable is wound.
  3. The drum is driven manually, electrically, hydraulically or pneumatically.
  4. A gearbox may be used to increase torque and reduce rotational speed.
  5. A braking mechanism controls the movement and holds the load.
  6. Winches are widely used on ships.

Important components

  • Drum.
  • Gearbox.
  • Driving motor.
  • Brake.
  • Wire rope or cable.
  • Clutch.
  • Control mechanism.

Marine applications

  • Mooring operations.
  • Anchor handling.
  • Cargo handling.
  • Towing operations.
  • Lifeboat and rescue equipment, depending on the arrangement.

2.7 Crane

  1. A crane is a lifting machine designed to lift and move heavy loads.
  2. It uses wire ropes, pulleys, drums, motors and structural arrangements.
  3. It can move loads vertically and horizontally.
  4. Cranes may be electrically or hydraulically operated.
  5. Shipboard cranes are used for cargo, provisions and machinery handling.

Types

  • Overhead crane.
  • Gantry crane.
  • Jib crane.
  • Mobile crane.
  • Deck crane.

2.8 Important Safety Points for Lifting Machines

  1. Never exceed the Safe Working Load (SWL).
  2. Inspect ropes, chains, hooks and lifting accessories before use.
  3. Check the condition of brakes.
  4. Ensure the load is properly secured.
  5. Avoid shock loading and sudden jerks.
  6. Never stand or walk under a suspended load.
  7. Ensure the lifting equipment is correctly positioned.
  8. Check that the supporting structure can withstand the applied load.
  9. Use suitable slings and shackles.
  10. Follow the ship's lifting procedures and permit requirements where applicable.

Important terminology

  • SWL (Safe Working Load): The maximum load that equipment is permitted to lift safely under specified conditions.
  • WLL (Working Load Limit): The maximum load specified by the manufacturer for the equipment under stated conditions.
  • Breaking Load: The load at which the equipment or material fails.
  • Factor of Safety: Ratio of failure load to the allowable working load.
FOS = Breaking Load / Safe Working Load

3.1 Inclined Plane

  1. An inclined plane is a flat surface placed at an angle to the horizontal.
  2. It is used to move heavy objects between two different heights.
  3. It reduces the effort required to raise an object by increasing the distance through which the effort acts.
  4. It is one of the six classical simple machines.
  5. It is based on the principle of mechanical advantage.

Examples

  • Loading ramps.
  • Ship gangways.
  • Vehicle ramps.
  • Staircases.
  • Inclined conveyor systems.

3.2 Important Terms

  • Load (W): Weight of the object being moved.
  • Effort (P): Force applied parallel to the inclined plane.
  • Length (L): Length of the inclined plane.
  • Height (h): Vertical height through which the load is raised.
  • Angle (θ): Angle made by the inclined plane with the horizontal.

3.3 Mechanical Advantage of an Inclined Plane

For an ideal inclined plane:

Input Work = Output Work
P × L = W × h

Therefore:

P = Wh / L

Mechanical advantage:

MA = W / P

Substituting:

MA = L / h

Velocity ratio:

VR = L / h

For an ideal inclined plane:

MA = VR

Efficiency:

η = 100%

3.4 Inclined Plane Without Friction

  1. When friction is neglected, the effort required to move a load depends on the component of its weight acting parallel to the plane.
  2. The weight of the object acts vertically downward.
  3. This weight can be resolved into two components:
    • Component perpendicular to the plane.
    • Component parallel to the plane.
  4. The parallel component acts down the inclined plane.

The force component parallel to the plane is:

P = W sin θ

The perpendicular component is:

N = W cos θ

Where:

  • P = Effort required to move the load upwards at constant speed.
  • N = Normal reaction.
  • W = Weight.
  • θ = Inclination angle.

Since:

sin θ = h / L

Therefore:

P = W (h / L)

3.5 Inclined Plane With Friction

  1. In an actual inclined plane, friction opposes the motion of the load.
  2. When the load moves upward, friction acts downward along the inclined plane.
  3. Therefore, additional effort is required to overcome friction.
  4. Frictional force is calculated as:
F = μN

Where:

  • F = Frictional force.
  • μ = Coefficient of friction.
  • N = Normal reaction.

For upward movement at constant speed:

P = W sin θ + μW cos θ

Therefore:

P = W (sin θ + μ cos θ)

This formula assumes the effort is applied parallel to the plane and the load is sliding upward at constant speed.

3.6 Angle of Repose

  1. Angle of repose is the maximum angle of an inclined plane at which a body remains just on the point of sliding down.
  2. At this condition, friction is at its limiting value.
  3. The angle of repose is represented by θr.
tan θr = μ

Where:

  • θr = Angle of repose.
  • μ = Coefficient of friction.
Important relationship: The angle of repose is equal to the angle of friction for the same pair of contacting surfaces under the standard limiting-friction model.

3.7 Screw and Screw Mechanism

  1. A screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a cylindrical surface.
  2. It consists of a helical thread formed around a cylindrical shaft.
  3. Rotational movement of the screw produces linear movement.
  4. It can convert torque into a large axial force.
  5. Screws are used in lifting devices, presses, vices and fastening arrangements.

Important parts of a screw

  • Thread.
  • Crest.
  • Root.
  • Pitch.
  • Major diameter.
  • Minor diameter.
  • Lead.
  • Helix angle.

3.8 Important Screw Terminology

1. Pitch (p)

  • Pitch is the axial distance between corresponding points on two adjacent threads.
  • It is measured parallel to the screw axis.

2. Lead (l)

  • Lead is the axial distance travelled by the nut or screw during one complete revolution.
  • For a single-start screw, lead is equal to pitch.
  • For a multiple-start screw, lead is greater than pitch.
Lead = Pitch × Number of starts

3. Helix Angle (α)

  • It is the angle made by the thread helix with a plane perpendicular to the screw axis, when measured at the pitch diameter.
tan α = l / (π dm)

Where:

  • l = Lead.
  • dm = Mean diameter of screw.

4. Mean Diameter (dm)

  • Mean diameter is the diameter measured approximately halfway between the major and minor diameters.
  • It is used in screw friction and torque calculations.

3.9 Screw Jack

  1. A screw jack is a lifting device used to raise heavy loads through the rotation of a threaded screw.
  2. It converts rotary motion into linear motion.
  3. It operates using the mechanical advantage of a screw thread.
  4. It can generate a large lifting force using comparatively small effort.
  5. It is commonly used in automobile workshops, machinery maintenance and engineering applications.

Main components

  • Screw.
  • Nut.
  • Handle.
  • Load-bearing head.
  • Base.
  • Supporting body.

3.10 Working Principle of a Screw Jack

  1. The screw jack is positioned beneath the load.
  2. A handle is attached to the screw mechanism.
  3. When the handle is rotated, the screw rotates relative to the nut.
  4. The threaded arrangement causes the screw or load-bearing head to move vertically.
  5. The load is gradually raised.
  6. When rotation stops, friction between the threads helps prevent the load from descending.
  7. A suitable locking arrangement or additional safety support may be required depending on the design.

3.11 Mechanical Advantage of a Screw Jack

For an ideal screw jack:

Work Input = Work Output

If the effort P is applied at the end of a handle of length R:

Distance travelled by effort in one revolution:

2πR

Distance travelled by load in one revolution:

l

Therefore:

P × 2πR = W × l

Rearranging:

MA = W / P = 2πR / l

Velocity ratio:

VR = 2πR / l

For an ideal screw jack:

MA = VR

Efficiency:

η = (MA / VR) × 100%

3.12 Torque Required in a Screw Jack

  1. Torque is the turning moment required to rotate the screw.
  2. The applied effort at the handle produces torque.
  3. Torque increases with the applied force and handle length.
T = P × R

Where:

  • T = Torque.
  • P = Applied effort.
  • R = Handle length.

For an ideal screw jack:

T = Wl / (2π)

For a real screw jack, additional torque is required to overcome friction.

3.13 Types of Screw Jacks

Mechanical Screw Jack

  • Operated by a handle.
  • Uses threaded screw and nut.

Worm Gear Screw Jack

  • Uses worm and worm wheel.
  • Provides high reduction and lifting force.

Electric Screw Jack

  • Operated by an electric motor.
  • Suitable for powered lifting.

Hydraulic Jack

  • Uses pressurised fluid.
  • Works on Pascal's law.

4.1 Introduction to Rotational Equilibrium

  1. Rotational equilibrium is a condition in which a body has no angular acceleration.
  2. It occurs when the net moment or torque acting on a body is zero.
  3. A body in rotational equilibrium does not experience any change in its angular velocity.
  4. It may either:
    • Remain stationary.
    • Rotate at constant angular velocity.
  5. Rotational equilibrium is important in machines, engines, shafts, levers, cranes and rotating equipment.
Important: Rotational equilibrium does not necessarily mean that the body is stationary. It means that its angular acceleration is zero.

4.2 Moment of a Force

  1. The moment of a force is the turning effect produced by a force about a fixed point or axis.
  2. It is also called the torque of a force.
  3. A force can produce rotational motion when its line of action does not pass through the axis of rotation.
  4. The turning effect depends on:
    • Magnitude of force.
    • Perpendicular distance from the axis to the line of action of force.
M = F × d

Where:

  • M = Moment of force.
  • F = Applied force.
  • d = Perpendicular distance.

SI unit:

N·m
Important: The perpendicular distance is called the moment arm or lever arm.

4.3 Clockwise and Anticlockwise Moments

  1. A force may rotate a body clockwise or anticlockwise.
  2. Clockwise moment tends to rotate the body in the direction of clock hands.
  3. Anticlockwise moment tends to rotate the body in the opposite direction.
  4. For rotational equilibrium, the total clockwise moment must equal the total anticlockwise moment.
Σ MCW = Σ MACW

Alternatively, using a sign convention:

Σ M = 0

4.4 Principle of Moments

  1. The principle of moments states that for a body in rotational equilibrium, the algebraic sum of moments about any point is zero.
  2. The total clockwise moment is equal to the total anticlockwise moment.
  3. It is used in the analysis of levers, beams, supports and mechanical systems.
Σ M = 0

4.5 Conditions of Equilibrium

For a rigid body to be in complete static equilibrium in a two-dimensional plane, two types of equilibrium must be satisfied.

1. Translational Equilibrium

  • The resultant force acting on the body must be zero.
  • The body must have no linear acceleration.
Σ Fx = 0
Σ Fy = 0

2. Rotational Equilibrium

  • The resultant moment acting on the body must be zero.
  • The body must have no angular acceleration.
Σ M = 0

Therefore, the three equilibrium equations for a 2D rigid body are:

Σ Fx = 0   Σ Fy = 0   Σ M = 0

For a three-dimensional rigid body, there are six independent equilibrium equations:

  • Three force equations.
  • Three moment equations.

4.6 Torque

  1. Torque is the rotational effect of a force about an axis.
  2. It is responsible for producing angular acceleration in rotating bodies.
  3. It is important in engines, gearboxes, shafts, turbines and propeller systems.
  4. Torque depends on the applied force and the perpendicular distance from the axis.
T = F × r

For a force acting at an angle:

T = rF sin θ

Where:

  • T = Torque.
  • r = Distance from axis.
  • F = Applied force.
  • θ = Angle between position vector and force.

SI unit: Newton-metre (N·m).

Important distinction:
  • Moment is commonly used in the context of the turning effect of forces in statics.
  • Torque is commonly used in the context of rotational motion and power transmission.
  • Both have the same SI unit and mathematical basis.

4.7 Couple

  1. A couple consists of two equal and opposite parallel forces acting along different lines.
  2. The resultant force of a couple is zero.
  3. However, it produces a pure rotational effect.
  4. The turning effect of a couple is independent of the reference point.
  5. A couple cannot produce translational acceleration of the body.

Moment of a couple:

M = F × d

Where:

  • F = Magnitude of either force.
  • d = Perpendicular distance between the two lines of action.

Examples:

  • Turning a steering wheel.
  • Tightening a nut using a spanner with two opposing forces.
  • Turning a shaft using a couple.

4.8 Lever and Principle of Moments

A lever is a rigid bar that rotates about a fixed point called the fulcrum.

Types of Levers:

1. First-Class Lever

  • Fulcrum lies between effort and load.
  • It may change the direction of force.
  • Mechanical advantage may be greater than, equal to or less than one.

Examples:

  • Crowbar.
  • Seesaw.
  • Pliers.
  • Scissors.

2. Second-Class Lever

  • Load lies between fulcrum and effort.
  • Mechanical advantage is generally greater than one.
  • It multiplies force.

Examples:

  • Wheelbarrow.
  • Nutcracker.
  • Bottle opener.

3. Third-Class Lever

  • Effort lies between fulcrum and load.
  • Mechanical advantage is generally less than one.
  • It increases the speed and distance of movement of the load.

Examples:

  • Tweezers.
  • Human forearm.
  • Fishing rod.

4.9 Conditions for Rotational Equilibrium in Shafts

  1. Rotating shafts transmit torque from one component to another.
  2. For a shaft in static torsional equilibrium, the algebraic sum of external torques must be zero.
  3. Driving torque must balance resisting torque.
  4. In a shaft rotating at constant speed, the net torque is zero when angular acceleration is zero.
Σ T = 0

Examples:

  • Engine crankshaft.
  • Intermediate shaft.
  • Propeller shaft.
  • Gearbox output shaft.

4.10 Relationship Between Torque, Angular Acceleration and Moment of Inertia

According to Newton's second law for rotational motion:

T = Iα

Where:

  • T = Net torque.
  • I = Mass moment of inertia.
  • α = Angular acceleration.

This is the rotational equivalent of Newton's second law:

F = ma

Comparison:

Force (F)Torque (T)
Mass (m)Moment of inertia (I)

Velocity (v)Angular velocity (ω)

4.11 Power in Rotating Machinery

  1. Power is the rate of doing work.
  2. In rotating machinery, power depends on torque and angular velocity.
  3. A rotating shaft transmits mechanical power through torque.
P = Tω

Where:

  • P = Power in watts.
  • T = Torque in N·m.
  • ω = Angular velocity in rad/s.

Angular velocity:

ω = 2πN / 60

Where N is rotational speed in RPM.

Therefore:

P = 2πNT / 60

For practical engineering calculations:

P (kW) = 2πNT / 60,000

Or:

P (kW) = TN / 9550

Where:

  • T = Torque in N·m.
  • N = Speed in RPM.

Marine engineering application

  • Main engine power transmission.
  • Propeller shaft rotation.
  • Auxiliary engine operation.
  • Generator shaft power.
  • Pump and compressor drives.
  • Turbine and gearbox systems.

5.1 Introduction to Moment of Inertia

  1. Moment of inertia is a property of a body that describes its resistance to angular acceleration about a particular axis.
  2. It depends on:
    • Mass of the body.
    • Distribution of mass.
    • Position of the axis of rotation.
  3. A body with a larger moment of inertia requires more torque to produce the same angular acceleration.
  4. It plays an important role in the design and analysis of rotating machinery.
  5. Moment of inertia is also called rotational inertia.

The basic equation is:

I = Σ mr²

For a continuous mass distribution:

I = ∫ r² dm

Where:

  • I = Mass moment of inertia.
  • m = Mass of a particle.
  • r = Perpendicular distance from the axis of rotation.
  • dm = Small mass element.

SI unit:

kg·m²
Important concept: Mass located farther from the axis contributes more to the moment of inertia because distance is squared.

5.3 Types of Moment of Inertia

There are two important types of moment of inertia in engineering mechanics.

1. Mass Moment of Inertia

  • Describes the resistance of a body to angular acceleration.
  • Depends on mass distribution about an axis.
  • Used in dynamics and rotational motion.
  • SI unit is kg·m².
I = ∫ r² dm

2. Area Moment of Inertia

  • Also called the second moment of area.
  • Describes the resistance of a cross-section to bending or deflection.
  • Used in strength of materials and structural engineering.
  • SI units are m⁴ or mm⁴.

Formula about the x-axis:

Ix = ∫ y² dA

Formula about the y-axis:

Iy = ∫ x² dA
Important distinction: Mass moment of inertia and area moment of inertia are different physical properties. They must not be confused.

5.4 Mass Moment of Inertia of Common Bodies

Standard formulae for uniform bodies:

1. Thin Rod About Its Centre

Axis perpendicular to the rod through its centre.

I = (1/12) ML²

2. Thin Rod About One End

Axis perpendicular to the rod through one end.

I = (1/3) ML²

3. Solid Disc About Central Axis

Axis perpendicular to the disc through its centre.

I = (1/2) MR²

4. Solid Cylinder About Its Longitudinal Axis

Axis passes through the cylinder's central length.

I = (1/2) MR²

5. Thin Hollow Cylinder About Its Axis

All mass is assumed concentrated at radius R.

I = MR²

6. Solid Sphere About Its Diameter

Axis passes through the centre.

I = (2/5) MR²

7. Thin Hollow Sphere About Its Diameter

Mass is assumed distributed over its spherical surface.

I = (2/3) MR²

8. Rectangular Plate About Central Perpendicular Axis

For sides a and b:

I = (M/12)(a² + b²)

Where:

  • M = Mass of the body.
  • R = Radius.
  • L = Length.
  • a, b = Dimensions of the rectangular plate.

5.5 Parallel Axis Theorem

  1. The parallel axis theorem is used to calculate the moment of inertia about an axis parallel to an axis passing through the centre of mass.
  2. It is useful when the moment of inertia about the centre of mass is known, but the required axis is located elsewhere.
  3. The two axes must be parallel.
  4. The distance between the axes is represented by d.
I = IG + Md²

Where:

  • I = Moment of inertia about the new axis.
  • IG = Moment of inertia about the parallel centroidal axis.
  • M = Total mass.
  • d = Distance between the two axes.
Important points:
  • The moment of inertia about an axis through the centre of mass is the minimum among all parallel axes.
  • Moving the axis farther away increases the moment of inertia.
  • The theorem applies to mass moment of inertia.

5.6 Perpendicular Axis Theorem

  1. The perpendicular axis theorem applies to a thin, flat lamina.
  2. It states that the moment of inertia about an axis perpendicular to the plane is equal to the sum of the moments of inertia about two mutually perpendicular axes lying in the plane.
  3. All three axes must intersect at the same point.
Iz = Ix + Iy

Where:

  • Iz = Moment of inertia about the perpendicular axis.
  • Ix = Moment of inertia about the x-axis.
  • Iy = Moment of inertia about the y-axis.
Important: This theorem applies to a planar lamina. It should not be applied directly to arbitrary three-dimensional bodies.

5.7 Radius of Gyration

  1. Radius of gyration is the distance from an axis at which the entire mass of a body may be assumed concentrated without changing its moment of inertia about that axis.
  2. It represents how far the mass is distributed from the axis.
  3. It is represented by k.
I = Mk²

Therefore:

k = √(I / M)

Where:

  • k = Radius of gyration.
  • I = Mass moment of inertia.
  • M = Total mass.
Important points:
  • A larger radius of gyration indicates that the mass is distributed farther from the axis.
  • Radius of gyration is measured in metres.
  • It is useful in rotating machinery and structural analysis.

For area moment of inertia, the corresponding radius of gyration is:

k = √(IA / A)

Where IA is the area moment of inertia and A is the cross-sectional area.

5.8 Area Moment of Inertia of Common Sections

These formulae are important for understanding the bending strength and stiffness of structural members.

SectionArea moment of inertia
Rectangle about centroidal x-axisIx = bh³ / 12
Rectangle about centroidal y-axisIy = hb³ / 12
Solid circle about a centroidal diameterI = πd⁴ / 64
Hollow circle about a centroidal diameterI = π(D⁴ − d⁴) / 64
Solid circle about polar axisJ = πd⁴ / 32

Where:

  • b = Width of rectangle.
  • h = Height of rectangle.
  • d = Diameter of solid circle.
  • D = Outer diameter of hollow circle.
  • d = Inner diameter of hollow circle in the hollow-circle formula.
Note: For circular sections, the polar second moment of area is J = Ix + Iy. It is used in torsion calculations and is not the mass moment of inertia.

5.9 Moment of Inertia in Rotating Machinery

  1. Rotating machinery contains components such as shafts, flywheels, turbines, rotors and gears.
  2. These components have mass distributed around their rotational axes.
  3. The mass distribution determines the moment of inertia.
  4. Moment of inertia influences how quickly a rotating machine can accelerate or decelerate.
  5. It also affects the energy stored in rotating machinery.

Marine engineering applications

  • Main engine crankshaft.
  • Flywheel.
  • Propeller shaft.
  • Marine diesel engine components.
  • Turbocharger rotor.
  • Electric motor rotor.
  • Generator rotor.
  • Centrifugal pumps.
  • Turbines.

5.10 Flywheel and Moment of Inertia

  1. A flywheel is a rotating mechanical component designed to store rotational kinetic energy.
  2. It is generally a heavy rotating disc or wheel.
  3. Its mass is distributed around the rotational axis.
  4. A larger moment of inertia allows the flywheel to store more rotational energy at the same angular velocity.
  5. It helps reduce fluctuations in rotational speed.
  6. It releases stored energy when the driving torque decreases.
  7. It absorbs energy when the driving torque increases.

Rotational kinetic energy:

KE = (1/2) Iω²

Where:

  • KE = Rotational kinetic energy.
  • I = Mass moment of inertia.
  • ω = Angular velocity.

Applications:

  • Internal combustion engines.
  • Marine propulsion machinery.
  • Mechanical presses.
  • Energy storage systems.
  • Reciprocating machinery.
Important: A flywheel reduces speed fluctuations; it does not generate energy.

5.11 Moment of Inertia and Angular Momentum

  1. Angular momentum is the rotational equivalent of linear momentum.
  2. It depends on moment of inertia and angular velocity.
  3. A rotating body with greater moment of inertia has greater angular momentum at the same angular velocity.
L = Iω

Where:

  • L = Angular momentum.
  • I = Mass moment of inertia.
  • ω = Angular velocity.

For a system with no external resultant torque, angular momentum remains constant.

I1ω1 = I2ω2

This principle is called the conservation of angular momentum.
