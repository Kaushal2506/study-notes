Mechanical Engineering Fundamentals

Statics is an important branch of Engineering Mechanics. It helps us understand how forces act on stationary structures, machine components, supports and mechanical systems. These notes cover fundamental concepts, important formulas, physical interpretations and practical marine engineering applications.

1.1 Basic concept

In engineering mechanics, physical quantities are classified into two main categories:

  • Scalar quantity: A physical quantity that has magnitude only.
  • Vector quantity: A physical quantity that has both magnitude and direction.

Understanding the difference is important because forces, moments and velocities must be handled using vector mathematics.

1.2 Scalar quantities

A scalar is completely described by its numerical value and unit.

Examples

  1. Mass – 50 kg
  2. Time – 10 seconds
  3. Temperature – 80°C
  4. Distance – 100 metres
  5. Speed – 20 m/s
  6. Energy – 500 joules
  7. Work – 200 joules
  8. Power – 10 kW
  9. Volume – 5 m³
  10. Density – 850 kg/m³

Important points

  • Scalars have magnitude but no direction.
  • Scalar quantities are added or subtracted using ordinary algebra.
  • Negative values can indicate a reference convention or a decrease, but do not give the scalar a physical direction.
  • Mass and weight are different: mass is scalar, whereas weight is a force and therefore a vector.

1.3 Vector quantities

A vector has both magnitude and direction.

Examples

  1. Force – 100 N towards the right.
  2. Displacement – 5 m towards the north.
  3. Velocity – 20 m/s towards the east.
  4. Acceleration – 9.81 m/s² vertically downward.
  5. Momentum – 50 kg·m/s in a specified direction.
  6. Weight – 500 N vertically downward.
  7. Torque or moment – has an associated rotational direction.

Important points

  • Vectors are represented by arrows.
  • The length of the arrow represents magnitude.
  • The arrowhead represents direction.
  • Vector quantities must be added using vector addition, not ordinary scalar addition.
  • The direction of a vector is important in solving equilibrium problems.

1.4 Representation of vectors

A. Graphical representation

  • Arrow length represents magnitude.
  • Arrowhead represents direction.
  • The starting point represents the point of application when depicting a force.

B. Component representation

A vector can be resolved into perpendicular components.

For a force F making an angle θ with the positive horizontal X-axis:

Fx = F cos θ
Fy = F sin θ

Where

  • Fx = Horizontal component.
  • Fy = Vertical component.
  • F = Resultant force.
  • θ = Angle measured from the positive X-axis.
Important: The sine and cosine expressions depend on how the angle is measured. If the angle is measured from the vertical axis, the component expressions are interchanged.

1.5 Unit vectors

A unit vector is a vector having magnitude equal to one. It represents direction only.

Three-dimensional Cartesian unit vectors

  • î = Unit vector along X-axis.
  • ĵ = Unit vector along Y-axis.
  • k̂ = Unit vector along Z-axis.

A vector can be written as:

A = Ax î + Ay ĵ + Az k̂

Magnitude of a three-dimensional vector:

|A| = √(Ax² + Ay² + Az²)

1.6 Vector addition

Vector addition is used to determine the combined effect of two or more vectors.

Methods

  1. Triangle law of vector addition.
  2. Parallelogram law of vector addition.
  3. Resolution into rectangular components.

For two perpendicular forces:

R = √(Fx² + Fy²)

Direction:

θ = tan−1(Fy / Fx)

For multiple forces:

ΣFx = F1x + F2x + F3x + ⋯
ΣFy = F1y + F2y + F3y + ⋯
Remember: Resolve forces into components before adding them whenever their directions differ.

1.7 Vector subtraction

Vector subtraction means adding a vector in the opposite direction.

A − B = A + (−B)

The negative vector has the same magnitude but opposite direction.

1.8 Important vector properties

  • Commutative property: A + B = B + A
  • Associative property: (A + B) + C = A + (B + C)
  • Distributive property: k(A + B) = kA + kB
  • A zero vector has zero magnitude.
  • Equal vectors have the same magnitude and direction.
  • Opposite vectors have equal magnitudes but opposite directions.

Marine engineering applications

  • Calculating forces acting on lifting equipment.
  • Understanding forces in crane cables.
  • Analysing forces acting on ship structures.
  • Resolving forces acting on engine foundations.
  • Understanding the weight and direction of loads during cargo handling.

2.1 Force

Force is an external action that changes or tends to change the state of rest or motion of a body, or causes deformation.

According to Newton's Second Law:

F = ma

Where

  • F = Force (N)
  • m = Mass (kg)
  • a = Acceleration (m/s²)

SI unit: Newton (N).

1 N = 1 kg·m/s²

In statics, acceleration is zero, so the resultant force acting on a body in equilibrium must be zero.

2.2 Characteristics of a force

A force is completely specified by the following characteristics:

  1. Magnitude – Strength of the force.
  2. Direction – Direction in which it acts.
  3. Line of action – Imaginary straight line along which the force acts.
  4. Point of application – Location where the force is applied.

2.3 Types of forces

A. Contact forces

These forces act when two bodies are in physical contact.

  • Normal reaction.
  • Frictional force.
  • Tension.
  • Applied force.
  • Spring force.

B. Non-contact forces

These act without direct physical contact.

  • Gravitational force.
  • Magnetic force.
  • Electrostatic force.

C. External forces

Forces applied to a body from outside the system.

  • Weight.
  • Applied load.
  • Support reaction.

D. Internal forces

Forces developed within a body due to external loading.

  • Internal tension.
  • Internal compression.
  • Internal shear force.

2.4 Types of loading

1. Point load (concentrated load)

  • Acts at a particular point or over a very small region.
  • Measured in Newton (N) or kilonewton (kN).
  • Example: Load applied by a suspended component.

2. Uniformly Distributed Load (UDL)

  • Load is distributed uniformly over a given length.
  • Measured in N/m or kN/m.
  • Resultant load: R = wL
  • Acts through the midpoint of the loaded length.

Where w is load intensity and L is loaded length.

3. Uniformly Varying Load (UVL)

  • Load intensity changes linearly along the length.
  • A triangular load is a common example.
  • Resultant load equals the area under the loading diagram.
  • For triangular loading: R = ½ wmaxL
  • Its line of action passes through the centroid of the triangle, at one-third of the length from the larger-load end.

2.5 Moment of a force

A moment is the turning effect produced by a force about a point or axis. It measures the tendency of a force to rotate a body.

M = F × d

Where

  • M = Moment (N·m)
  • F = Applied force (N)
  • d = Perpendicular distance from the reference point to the line of action of the force (m).

SI unit: Newton-metre (N·m).

Important points

  • Greater force produces a greater moment if the distance remains constant.
  • Greater perpendicular distance produces a greater moment if the force remains constant.
  • If the line of action passes through the pivot, the moment is zero.
  • The moment depends on the perpendicular distance, not simply the distance between the force application point and pivot.

2.6 Clockwise and anticlockwise moments

A force can produce rotation in two directions.

  • Clockwise moment: Tends to rotate the body clockwise.
  • Anticlockwise moment: Tends to rotate the body anticlockwise.

A common sign convention is:

  • Anticlockwise moment = Positive.
  • Clockwise moment = Negative.

Always use one consistent sign convention throughout a calculation.

2.7 Principle of moments (Varignon's theorem)

The algebraic sum of the moments of the component forces about a point is equal to the moment of their resultant about the same point.

MO(R) = Σ MO(Fi)

This principle is useful when calculating the turning effect of multiple forces.

Applications

  • Lever mechanisms.
  • Spanners and wrenches.
  • Crankshafts.
  • Engine components.
  • Beam reactions.

2.8 Couple

A couple consists of two equal, opposite and parallel forces acting along different lines of action. It produces pure rotation without producing a resultant translational force.

Moment of a couple:

M = F × d

Where

  • F = Magnitude of either force.
  • d = Perpendicular distance between the two lines of action.

Characteristics of a couple

  • Two forces are equal in magnitude.
  • Forces act in opposite directions.
  • Forces are parallel.
  • Their lines of action are separated by a perpendicular distance.
  • Resultant force is zero.
  • Resultant moment is non-zero.
  • A couple produces rotation only.
  • The moment of a couple is independent of the reference point.

2.9 Difference between moment and couple

Moment of a forceCouple
Can produce rotation and translationProduces pure rotation
May be caused by a single forceRequires two equal and opposite forces
Depends on the reference pointIndependent of the reference point
Resultant force may be non-zeroResultant force is zero
Moment may be zero about a point on its line of actionCouple moment is non-zero

2.10 Moment of a force in vector form

For a three-dimensional system:

MO = r × F

Where

  • r = Position vector from reference point to point of application.
  • F = Applied force.
  • × = Vector cross product.

The magnitude is:

M = rF sin θ

Where θ is the angle between the position vector and force vector.

Marine engineering applications

  • Tightening nuts and bolts.
  • Operating valves using handwheels.
  • Rotating crankshafts.
  • Understanding torque transmission.
  • Analysing forces acting on engine mounts.
  • Understanding turning effects on rudder mechanisms.

3.1 Resultant force

The resultant force is a single force that produces the same external effect as a system of multiple forces acting together. It is obtained by vector addition of all the forces.

R = ΣF

For a two-dimensional system:

Rx = ΣFx
Ry = ΣFy

Magnitude:

R = √(Rx² + Ry²)

Direction:

θ = tan−1(Ry / Rx)

The correct quadrant must be considered when determining the direction.

3.2 Types of force systems

1. Collinear force system

  • All forces act along the same straight line.
  • Forces may act in the same or opposite directions.

2. Concurrent force system

  • All lines of action meet at one common point.
  • Forces may act at different angles.

3. Parallel force system

  • All forces have parallel lines of action.
  • Forces may act in the same or opposite directions.

4. Coplanar force system

  • All forces act in the same plane.
  • This includes coplanar concurrent and non-concurrent systems.

5. Non-coplanar force system

  • Forces act in different planes.
  • Common in three-dimensional engineering structures and machinery.

3.3 Resolution and combination of forces

The resultant of a force system is calculated by resolving each force into perpendicular components.

For a two-dimensional force system:

ΣFx = F1x + F2x + ⋯
ΣFy = F1y + F2y + ⋯

Then:

R = √((ΣFx)² + (ΣFy)²)
Important: Always assign positive and negative signs according to the chosen coordinate directions.

3.4 Equilibrium

A body is said to be in equilibrium when the resultant external force and resultant external moment acting on it are zero. A body in equilibrium has no linear or angular acceleration.

Newton's First Law forms the basis of static equilibrium.

There are two important types:

A. Static equilibrium

  • The body remains at rest.
  • Linear acceleration is zero.
  • Angular acceleration is zero.

B. Dynamic equilibrium

  • The body moves with constant velocity.
  • Linear acceleration is zero.
  • Angular acceleration is zero.
  • The body may be moving, but the resultant force and moment are zero.

3.5 Conditions of equilibrium

For a two-dimensional body

There are three independent equilibrium equations.

ΣFx = 0
ΣFy = 0
ΣMO = 0

Where O is any convenient reference point.

These three equations are fundamental in solving two-dimensional statics problems.

For a three-dimensional body

There are six independent equilibrium equations.

ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0, ΣFz = 0
ΣMx = 0, ΣMy = 0, ΣMz = 0

3.6 Types of equilibrium based on stability

1. Stable equilibrium

  • A body returns to its original position after a small disturbance.
  • Its centre of gravity rises when displaced slightly.
  • Example: A ball resting at the bottom of a concave bowl.

2. Unstable equilibrium

  • A body moves farther away from its original position after a small disturbance.
  • Its centre of gravity lowers when displaced slightly.
  • Example: A ball balanced on top of a convex surface.

3. Neutral equilibrium

  • A body remains in its new position after a small disturbance.
  • Its centre of gravity stays at the same height.
  • Example: A ball resting on a horizontal flat surface.

3.7 Principle of transmissibility

For a rigid body, a force can be moved anywhere along its line of action without changing its external effect on the body.

Important limitation

  • This principle applies to the external effect on a rigid body.
  • Internal stresses and deformations may change when the actual point of application changes.

3.8 Equilibrant force

An equilibrant is a force that balances the resultant force of a system.

E = −R

Important characteristics

  • Equal in magnitude to the resultant.
  • Opposite in direction.
  • Acts along the same line of action as the resultant.

Marine engineering applications

  • Checking the equilibrium of engine foundations.
  • Calculating forces in supporting structures.
  • Analysing suspended machinery.
  • Understanding static loading on ship decks.
  • Checking lifting arrangements before cargo handling.

4.1 Definition

A Free-Body Diagram is a simplified diagram showing a body isolated from its surroundings, with all external forces and moments acting on it.

It is one of the most important tools in engineering mechanics. The purpose of an FBD is to identify all the forces acting on a body before applying equilibrium equations.

4.2 Why FBD is important

  • Helps identify all external forces.
  • Makes complicated systems easier to analyse.
  • Helps determine unknown support reactions.
  • Prevents missing forces in equilibrium calculations.
  • Provides the foundation for solving statics problems.
  • Helps understand how loads are transferred through mechanical systems.

4.3 Steps to draw an FBD

  1. Identify the body — Select the object or component that needs to be analysed.
  2. Isolate the body — Imagine separating it from all surrounding components and supports.
  3. Replace supports with reactions — Remove the physical supports from the diagram and represent their effects using reaction forces and moments.
  4. Add external forces — Show all applied forces, weight, tension, friction and other external loads.
  5. Show dimensions and distances — Mark all relevant distances, angles and moment arms.
  6. Establish coordinate axes — Choose suitable positive X, Y and Z directions.
  7. Apply equilibrium equations — Use the appropriate equations to calculate unknown forces and moments.

4.4 Forces commonly shown in an FBD

  1. Applied force – External force applied to the body.
  2. Weight – Gravitational force acting vertically downward.
  3. Normal reaction – Force perpendicular to the contact surface.
  4. Frictional force – Force parallel to the contact surface.
  5. Tension – Pulling force transmitted through a cable or rope.
  6. Spring force – Force exerted by a spring.
  7. Support reaction – Force exerted by a supporting structure.
  8. Applied moment – External turning effect.
  9. Couple moment – Pure rotational effect.

4.5 Important rules of FBD

Rules

  • Draw only external forces acting on the isolated body.
  • Do not show internal forces between parts of the isolated body.
  • Every removed support must be replaced by appropriate reactions.
  • Weight acts through the centre of gravity.
  • Normal reaction acts perpendicular to the contact surface.
  • Friction acts parallel to the contact surface.
  • Tension in an ideal cable acts along the cable and pulls away from the body.
  • Show unknown reaction directions using reasonable assumed directions.
  • If the calculated result is negative, the actual direction is opposite to the assumed direction.

4.6 FBD of common systems

A. Block resting on a horizontal surface

  • Weight acting downward.
  • Normal reaction acting upward.
  • Applied force, if present.
  • Friction, if the surface is rough and friction is relevant.

B. Suspended body

  • Weight acting vertically downward.
  • Tension acting along the supporting cable.

C. Simply supported beam

  • Applied loads.
  • Vertical support reactions.
  • Horizontal reactions if applicable.
  • Applied moments, if present.

Marine engineering applications

  • Analysing engine mounting arrangements.
  • Studying pipe support systems.
  • Calculating loads on brackets.
  • Analysing suspended components.
  • Understanding forces acting on pumps, compressors and generators.

5.1 Definition

A support reaction is the force or moment developed by a support to resist the external loads acting on a body. Supports prevent or restrict certain types of movement.

When a structure is loaded, its supports generate reaction forces and sometimes reaction moments to maintain equilibrium.

5.2 Types of supports

The most important support types in two-dimensional statics are:

  1. Roller support.
  2. Pin or hinged support.
  3. Fixed support.
  4. Link support.
  5. Cable support.

5.3 Roller support

Characteristics

  • Allows horizontal movement.
  • Allows rotation.
  • Prevents movement perpendicular to the supporting surface.
  • Develops only one reaction force.
  • The reaction acts normal to the supporting surface.

For a horizontal supporting surface, the reaction is vertical.

Example: A beam resting on rollers.

5.4 Pin or hinged support

Characteristics

  • Prevents horizontal movement.
  • Prevents vertical movement.
  • Allows rotation.
  • Develops two reaction components in a two-dimensional system.

Reactions: Rx, Ry

The resultant reaction is:

R = √(Rx² + Ry²)

Example: Hinged connections and pinned structural joints.

5.5 Fixed support

Characteristics

  • Prevents horizontal movement.
  • Prevents vertical movement.
  • Prevents rotation.
  • Develops three reaction components in a two-dimensional system.

Reactions: Rx, Ry, M

Example: A cantilever beam fixed into a wall.

5.6 Link support

Characteristics

  • A link is a member connected through pins at both ends.
  • For an ideal two-force member, forces act along the link's axis.
  • The link may be in tension or compression.
  • Reaction direction depends on the loading and assumed direction.

5.7 Cable support

Characteristics

  • Cable can carry tension only.
  • It cannot resist compression.
  • Tension acts along the cable.
  • An ideal flexible cable cannot resist bending moment.

Example: Crane lifting cables and suspended machinery.

5.8 Summary of support reactions

Support type Horizontal reaction Vertical reaction Moment reaction

5.9 Determination of support reactions

Support reactions are determined using equilibrium equations.

For a two-dimensional structure:

ΣFx = 0
ΣFy = 0
ΣM = 0

Recommended procedure

  1. Draw the complete structure.
  2. Identify the type of supports.
  3. Draw the FBD.
  4. Replace supports with unknown reactions.
  5. Select a convenient point for taking moments.
  6. Apply the three equilibrium equations.
  7. Solve the unknown reactions.
  8. Verify the results using the remaining equations.

5.10 Determinate and indeterminate structures

Statically determinate structure

  • All unknown reactions can be calculated using equilibrium equations alone.
  • In a two-dimensional system, there are three independent equilibrium equations.

Statically indeterminate structure

  • There are more unknown reaction components than independent equilibrium equations.
  • Additional information is required from deformation and compatibility conditions.

Marine engineering applications

  • Supports for pipelines.
  • Foundation bolts of marine engines.
  • Generator mounting arrangements.
  • Supporting brackets for auxiliary machinery.
  • Structural support systems on ships.

These two concepts are closely related but are not identical.

6.1 Centroid

The centroid is the geometrical centre of an area or plane figure. It represents the point at which the entire area can be considered concentrated for geometric calculations.

Important points

  • Centroid is a geometrical concept.
  • It depends on the shape and dimensions of the area.
  • It does not require mass or weight.
  • It is used for determining the geometrical centre of plane areas.
  • For a uniform lamina of constant thickness and density, its centroid coincides with its centre of gravity.

6.2 Centre of gravity (CG)

The centre of gravity is the point through which the resultant gravitational force (weight) of a body acts.

Important points

  • It is related to the weight distribution of a body.
  • The entire weight of a body can be considered to act through its centre of gravity in a uniform gravitational field.
  • It depends on the body's mass distribution and gravitational field.
  • It may lie inside or outside the physical body.

6.3 Centre of mass

The centre of mass is the point at which the entire mass of a body can be considered concentrated for translational motion.

Important points

  • It depends on mass distribution.
  • It is independent of gravity.
  • For a body in a uniform gravitational field, centre of mass and centre of gravity coincide.

6.6 Centroid of composite areas

A composite area consists of multiple simple geometrical shapes combined together. It may include rectangles, triangles, circles, semicircles, cut-outs and holes.

The centroid is calculated using the area-weighted average.

x̄ = Σ Ai xi / Σ Ai
ȳ = Σ Ai yi / Σ Ai

Where

  • Ai = Area of each individual shape.
  • xi = X-coordinate of the centroid of each shape.
  • yi = Y-coordinate of the centroid of each shape.
  • x̄, ȳ = Coordinates of the composite centroid.
Important rule for holes:
  • Treat removed areas as negative areas.
  • Subtract their area and first moments from the total.
  • Use the same reference axes for all components.

6.7 Centre of gravity of a body

For a body consisting of several masses:

x̄ = Σ mi xi / Σ mi
ȳ = Σ mi yi / Σ mi
z̄ = Σ mi zi / Σ mi

Where

  • mi = Mass of each component.
  • xi, yi, zi = Coordinates of each component's centre of mass.

If gravitational acceleration is uniform, the same equations can be written using weights instead of masses.

6.8 Centroid of a line

For a uniform thin wire or line:

x̄ = Σ Li xi / Σ Li
ȳ = Σ Li yi / Σ Li

Where Li is the length of each segment.

6.9 Centre of gravity and ship stability

Centre of gravity is particularly important in marine engineering because it influences the stability of a ship.

Important terms

1. Centre of Gravity (G)

  • Point through which the total weight of the ship acts.
  • Weight acts vertically downward through G.

2. Centre of Buoyancy (B)

  • Centroid of the displaced underwater volume.
  • Buoyant force acts vertically upward through B.

3. Metacentre (M)

  • Reference point used in the initial stability analysis of a floating vessel.
  • For small angles of heel, the metacentre is the intersection of the original and slightly inclined lines of action of buoyancy.

4. Metacentric Height (GM)

The vertical distance between the centre of gravity and metacentre.

GM = KM − KG

Also:

GM = BM − BG

Where

  • K = Keel reference point.
  • M = Metacentre.
  • G = Centre of gravity.
  • B = Centre of buoyancy.

Basic interpretation

  • Positive GM: Initial stability is positive.
  • Negative GM: Initial stability is negative.
  • Zero GM: Neutral initial stability in the idealised small-angle sense.
Important marine engineering concept: A higher centre of gravity generally reduces GM, assuming the other relevant geometric quantities remain unchanged. This is why proper loading, stowage and securing of cargo are important for ship stability.

Marine engineering applications

  • Ship stability calculations.
  • Cargo loading and distribution.
  • Engine and generator positioning.
  • Centre of gravity of machinery.
  • Structural design of engine-room equipment.
  • Understanding weight distribution on board.

7.1 Friction

Friction is the resisting force that develops between two surfaces in contact when there is relative motion or a tendency for relative motion. It acts parallel to the contact surface and opposes relative motion or impending relative motion.

Important characteristics

  • Friction acts tangentially to the contact surface.
  • It opposes relative motion or the tendency of relative motion.
  • It depends on the nature and condition of the contacting surfaces.
  • It depends on the normal reaction in the basic dry-friction model.
  • Friction is a contact force.

7.2 Types of friction

1. Static friction

Static friction acts between two surfaces that are in contact but are not sliding relative to each other.

  • Prevents the beginning of sliding.
  • Its magnitude adjusts according to the applied tangential force.
  • It increases up to a maximum value.
0 ≤ Fs ≤ Fmax

2. Limiting friction

Limiting friction is the maximum value of static friction, acting when the body is just about to start sliding.

Fmax = μs N

Where

  • Fmax = Limiting friction.
  • μs = Coefficient of static friction.
  • N = Normal reaction.

3. Kinetic or dynamic friction

Kinetic friction acts when two surfaces are sliding relative to each other.

Fk = μk N

Where

  • Fk = Kinetic friction.
  • μk = Coefficient of kinetic friction.

4. Rolling friction

Rolling friction is the resistance encountered when a body rolls over a surface.

  • Wheels.
  • Bearings.
  • Rollers.
  • Rolling machinery components.

Rolling resistance is generally smaller than sliding friction in many practical applications, although the actual resistance depends on the materials and operating conditions.

7.3 Laws of dry friction (Coulomb's laws)

Basic laws

  1. Friction acts tangentially to the contact surface.
  2. Friction opposes relative motion or impending relative motion.
  3. Limiting friction is proportional to the normal reaction.
  4. The ratio of limiting friction to normal reaction is the coefficient of static friction.
  5. For ideal dry friction, friction is approximately independent of the apparent area of contact.
  6. Kinetic friction is generally less than limiting static friction for the same surfaces.
  7. Friction depends on the nature and condition of the contacting surfaces.

These are idealised engineering laws; real friction can also depend on lubrication, temperature, surface roughness, speed and other conditions.

7.4 Coefficient of friction

The coefficient of friction is the ratio of limiting friction to the normal reaction.

μs = Fmax / N

For kinetic friction:

μk = Fk / N

Important points

  • Coefficient of friction is dimensionless.
  • It has no SI unit.
  • It depends on the materials and surface conditions.
  • It is generally different for static and kinetic friction.
  • It is not a force.

Usually:

μs > μk

7.5 Angle of friction

The angle of friction is the angle between the resultant reaction and the normal reaction when friction reaches its limiting value. It is represented by φ.

tan φ = Fmax / N

Therefore:

μs = tan φ
Remember: Angle of friction is related to the coefficient of static friction.

7.6 Angle of repose

The angle of repose is the maximum angle of inclination of a plane with the horizontal at which a body remains just on the point of sliding down.

At limiting equilibrium:

tan θ = μs

Where

  • θ = Angle of repose.
  • μs = Coefficient of static friction.

Therefore:

θ = φ

The angle of repose is equal to the angle of friction for the same contact surfaces under the ideal dry-friction model.

7.7 Friction on a horizontal surface

For a body resting on a horizontal surface, if there are no other vertical forces:

N = W

Since:

W = mg

Therefore:

N = mg

Maximum static friction:

Fmax = μs mg

Kinetic friction:

Fk = μk mg
Important: Actual static friction is not always equal to μsN. It takes whatever value is necessary to maintain equilibrium up to its limiting value.

7.8 Friction on an inclined plane

When a body is placed on an inclined plane, its weight acts vertically downward. The weight is resolved into two components.

Component parallel to the plane:

W∥ = W sin θ

This component tends to move the body down the inclined plane.

Component perpendicular to the plane:

W⊥ = W cos θ

For a body with no other perpendicular forces:

N = W cos θ

At limiting equilibrium:

Fmax = μs W cos θ

When the body is just about to slide down:

W sin θ = μs W cos θ

Therefore:

tan θ = μs

7.9 Friction cone

The friction cone is a geometrical representation of the possible directions of the resultant contact reaction under the ideal Coulomb friction model.

  • Inside the friction cone: Static equilibrium is possible.
  • On the boundary of the friction cone: Limiting equilibrium exists.
  • Outside the friction cone: Static equilibrium cannot be maintained under the assumed friction model.

7.10 Advantages and disadvantages of friction

Advantages

  • Allows vehicles to move without slipping.
  • Helps brakes stop rotating machinery.
  • Enables belts to transmit power.
  • Helps screws and bolts remain tightened.
  • Allows gripping and holding objects.
  • Provides traction between wheels and surfaces.

Disadvantages

  • Causes wear and tear.
  • Produces heat.
  • Reduces mechanical efficiency.
  • Causes energy loss.
  • Increases power requirements.
  • Can damage machine components if lubrication is insufficient.

7.11 Methods of reducing friction

  1. Lubrication using oil or grease.
  2. Using ball bearings and roller bearings.
  3. Polishing or improving surface finish where appropriate.
  4. Using suitable low-friction materials.
  5. Proper alignment of shafts and rotating components.
  6. Maintaining correct bearing clearances.
  7. Using hydrodynamic or hydrostatic lubrication where applicable.

7.12 Methods of increasing friction

  1. Increasing surface roughness where appropriate.
  2. Using high-friction materials.
  3. Increasing normal force within safe operating limits.
  4. Using suitable friction linings.
  5. Keeping braking surfaces in proper operating condition.

Marine engineering applications

  • Braking systems.
  • Shaft bearings.
  • Couplings.
  • Belt drives.
  • Winches and lifting equipment.
  • Bolted joints.
  • Friction clutches.
  • Deck machinery and mooring equipment.
