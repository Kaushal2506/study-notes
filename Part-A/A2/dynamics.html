1.1 Introduction to Dynamics

  • Dynamics is the branch of Engineering Mechanics that deals with the motion of bodies and the forces responsible for producing or changing that motion.
  • It explains how objects move, how fast they move, how their velocity changes and how forces affect their motion.
  • Dynamics is important in mechanical and marine engineering because machines, engines, pumps, turbines, propellers and rotating shafts involve motion.

Branches of Dynamics

KinematicsKinetics

Types of Motion

  1. Translational Motion – A body moves from one position to another. Every point of a rigid body undergoes the same displacement in the same direction at a given instant. Example: A piston moving inside a cylinder.
  2. Rotational Motion – A body rotates about a fixed axis. Every point of the body moves along a circular path around the axis. Example: Crankshaft, flywheel, turbine rotor and propeller.
  3. Oscillatory Motion – A body moves repeatedly back and forth about a mean or equilibrium position. Example: A vibrating spring, engine vibration and ship machinery vibration.
  4. Circular Motion – A body moves along a circular path around a fixed centre or axis. Example: A rotating fan blade or a rotating turbine shaft.
  5. Projectile Motion – Motion of an object projected into the air under the influence of gravity, neglecting air resistance. The horizontal component of velocity remains constant, while the vertical component changes due to gravity.

1.2 Distance and Displacement

Distance

  • The total length of the actual path travelled by a moving body.
  • It is a scalar quantity — magnitude but no direction.
  • Distance is always positive or zero.
  • It depends on the actual path followed by the body.
  • SI unit: metre (m).

Displacement

  • The shortest straight-line distance between the initial and final positions of a moving body, along with its direction.
  • It is a vector quantity — magnitude and direction.
  • Displacement depends only on the initial and final positions, not on the actual path travelled.
  • SI unit: metre (m).
Displacement = xf − xi

Where xf = final position, xi = initial position.

Important points

  • Distance travelled can never be less than the magnitude of displacement.
  • If an object moves in a straight line without changing direction, distance and displacement magnitudes are equal.
  • If an object moves along a curved path or changes direction, distance is generally greater than displacement magnitude.
  • If an object returns to its starting position, its net displacement is zero.
  • Displacement may be zero even when distance travelled is not zero.
  • In one-dimensional motion, positive and negative signs indicate direction.

1.3 Speed and Velocity

Speed

  • The rate of change of distance with respect to time.
  • It tells us how fast a body is moving.
  • Speed is a scalar quantity.
  • SI unit: metre per second (m/s).
Average Speed = Total DistanceTotal Time
  • Instantaneous Speed: Speed of a body at a particular instant of time. It is the magnitude of instantaneous velocity.
  • Uniform Speed: A body covers equal distances in equal intervals of time.
  • Non-uniform Speed: A body covers unequal distances in equal intervals of time.

Velocity

  • The rate of change of displacement with respect to time.
  • It describes how quickly and in which direction a body changes its position.
  • Velocity is a vector quantity.
  • SI unit: metre per second (m/s).
vavg = Total DisplacementTotal Time
v = dxdt
  • Instantaneous Velocity: Velocity of a body at a particular instant — the instantaneous rate of change of displacement with respect to time.
  • Uniform Velocity: A body covers equal displacements in equal intervals of time in the same direction. Both magnitude and direction of velocity remain constant.
  • Variable Velocity: Velocity changes when either its magnitude or direction changes. A vehicle moving around a circular track at constant speed has variable velocity because its direction continuously changes.

1.4 Acceleration

  • The rate of change of velocity with respect to time.
  • It describes how quickly the velocity of a body changes.
  • Acceleration is a vector quantity.
  • SI unit: metre per second squared (m/s²).
aavg = v − ut

Where u = initial velocity, v = final velocity, t = time taken.

a = dvdt

Types of Acceleration

  • Uniform Acceleration: Velocity changes by equal amounts in equal intervals of time. Acceleration remains constant.
  • Non-uniform Acceleration: Velocity changes by unequal amounts in equal intervals of time. Acceleration varies with time.
  • Positive Acceleration: Acceleration acts in the chosen positive direction. It does not necessarily mean that the body is speeding up.
  • Negative Acceleration: Acceleration acts in the chosen negative direction. It does not necessarily mean that the body is slowing down.
  • Retardation or Deceleration: Acceleration acts opposite to the direction of velocity. The speed of the body decreases.

Important concept to remember

  • When velocity and acceleration act in the same direction, speed increases.
  • When velocity and acceleration act in opposite directions, speed decreases.
  • When velocity is zero at an instant, acceleration need not be zero.
  • A body can have constant speed but changing velocity, as in uniform circular motion.

Applicable when acceleration is constant.

1.6 Motion Under Gravity

When a body moves vertically under the influence of gravity alone, it experiences gravitational acceleration. This motion is called free fall when air resistance is neglected.

g = 9.81 m/s²

Gravity always acts vertically downward, regardless of whether the body is moving upward or downward.

For a body falling freely from rest:

v = gt
h = ½gt²
v² = 2gh

Where h = vertical distance fallen, t = time, v = velocity after falling.

Important concepts:
  • When a body is thrown vertically upward, its velocity decreases while moving upward.
  • At the highest point, its instantaneous velocity becomes zero.
  • Its acceleration at the highest point is still g downward.
  • During downward motion, its speed increases due to gravity.
  • For a body returning to its original height, the time taken to rise equals the time taken to fall, assuming no air resistance.

2.1 Newton's First Law – Law of Inertia

Statement: A body remains at rest or continues moving with uniform velocity in a straight line unless acted upon by an external unbalanced force.

  • A stationary object will remain stationary unless an external force changes its state of rest.
  • A moving object will continue moving at constant velocity unless an unbalanced external force acts on it.
  • A body naturally resists changes in its state of motion. This resistance is called inertia.
  • The mass of a body is a measure of its inertia. Greater mass means greater inertia.

Types of Inertia

  1. Inertia of Rest: A body at rest tends to remain at rest. Example: When a stationary bus suddenly starts, passengers tend to fall backward.
  2. Inertia of Motion: A moving body tends to continue moving with the same velocity. Example: When a moving bus suddenly stops, passengers tend to fall forward.
  3. Inertia of Direction: A body resists a change in its direction of motion. Example: Passengers in a moving vehicle tend to lean sideways when the vehicle takes a sharp turn.

Marine Engineering Applications

  • Heavy rotating machinery resists changes in rotational speed because of rotational inertia.
  • A ship's large mass makes it difficult to stop or change its direction quickly.
  • Flywheels use rotational inertia to maintain more uniform rotational speed.

2.2 Newton's Second Law – Law of Acceleration

Statement: The rate of change of momentum of a body is directly proportional to the applied net external force and takes place in the direction of that force.

F = ma

Where F = net external force (N), m = mass (kg), a = acceleration (m/s²).

General form

F = dpdt

Since p = mv, for constant mass: F = m·(dv/dt) = ma.

  • Force is a vector quantity.
  • The SI unit of force is Newton (N).
  • One Newton is the force required to produce an acceleration of 1 m/s² in a mass of 1 kg.
1 N = 1 kg·m/s²

Marine Engineering Applications

  • Calculating the force required to accelerate a moving component.
  • Understanding piston acceleration inside an engine cylinder.
  • Determining forces acting on moving machinery.
  • Understanding ship acceleration and deceleration.

2.3 Newton's Third Law – Action and Reaction

Statement: For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.

  • Forces always occur in pairs.
  • If body A exerts a force on body B, body B simultaneously exerts an equal and opposite force on body A.
  • The two forces have equal magnitude and opposite directions.
  • These forces act on two different bodies, so they do not cancel each other on a single-body free-body diagram.
Examples:
  • Walking: foot pushes the ground backward, ground pushes the person forward.
  • Gun recoil when a bullet is fired forward.
  • Rocket moves upward by expelling gases downward.
  • Ship's propeller pushes water backward; water exerts a forward force on the propeller, producing thrust.

Mass

  • Also a measure of inertia.

  • Vector quantity; SI unit: Newton (N).

2.6 Types of Forces

Contact Forces

  • Forces that act when two bodies are in physical contact.
  • Examples: frictional force, normal reaction, tension, applied force, spring force.

Non-contact Forces

  • Forces that act without direct physical contact.
  • Examples: gravitational force, magnetic force, electrostatic force.

Frictional Force

  • The resisting force that opposes relative motion or the tendency of relative motion between two surfaces in contact.
  • Acts parallel to the contact surface.
TypeDescription
Static frictionPrevents motion from starting
Limiting frictionMaximum static friction just before motion begins
Kinetic / sliding frictionActs when surfaces slide over one another
Rolling frictionResistance experienced by a rolling body
Ff = μN

Where Ff = frictional force, μ = coefficient of friction, N = normal reaction.

Normal Reaction

  • Force exerted by a surface on a body in contact with it.
  • Acts perpendicular to the contact surface.
  • Prevents the body from penetrating the surface.

Tension

  • Pulling force transmitted through a stretched rope, cable or string.
  • Acts along the length of the rope or cable.
  • Always pulls rather than pushes.

2.7 Equilibrium of a Body

  • A body is in equilibrium when the resultant of all external forces and moments acting on it is zero.
  • A body in equilibrium has zero acceleration.
  • It may be at rest or moving with constant velocity.

Conditions of Equilibrium (2D)

ΣFx = 0
ΣFy = 0
ΣM = 0

Types of Equilibrium

  • Static Equilibrium: The body remains at rest. Both resultant force and resultant moment are zero.
  • Dynamic Equilibrium: The body moves with constant velocity. Resultant force and resultant moment are zero.
Important: Equilibrium does not necessarily mean that no forces are acting. Several forces may act on a body but balance one another.

3.1 Linear Motion

  • Motion of a body along a straight line or a curved path in which its position changes through translation.
  • In pure translation of a rigid body, all points have the same instantaneous velocity and acceleration.
  • Described using displacement, velocity and acceleration.

Types of Linear Motion

  1. Uniform Linear Motion: Straight line with constant velocity. Acceleration is zero.
  2. Uniformly Accelerated Linear Motion: Straight line with constant acceleration. Velocity changes by equal amounts in equal time intervals.
  3. Non-uniform Linear Motion: Velocity changes irregularly with time. Acceleration may vary.

Examples in Mechanical Engineering

  • Piston movement inside an engine cylinder.
  • Reciprocating pump piston movement.
  • Movement of a sliding component.
  • Elevator movement.

3.2 Angular Motion

  • Motion of a body around a fixed axis or centre.
  • Every point of a rigid body follows a circular path around the axis of rotation.

Angular Displacement (θ)

  • The angle through which a body rotates about a fixed axis.
  • Measured in radians (rad).
  • Vector quantity in the axial-vector representation (right-hand rule).
θ = sr

Where s = arc length (m), r = radius (m).

360° = 2π rad   ·   180° = π rad   ·   1 revolution = 2π rad

Angular Velocity (ω)

  • The rate of change of angular displacement with respect to time.
  • SI unit: rad/s.
ω = ΔθΔt   ·   ω = dθdt

Uniform Angular Velocity: Equal angular displacements in equal intervals of time.

Angular Acceleration (α)

  • The rate of change of angular velocity with respect to time.
  • SI unit: rad/s².
α = ΔωΔt   ·   α = dωdt
  • Positive angular acceleration: Acts in the chosen positive rotational direction.
  • Negative angular acceleration: Acts in the chosen negative rotational direction.
  • Uniform angular acceleration: Angular velocity changes at a constant rate.

3.3 Relationship Between Linear and Angular Motion

Points at different distances from the axis have different linear speeds but the same angular velocity.

3.7 Moment of Inertia

  • The rotational equivalent of mass in linear motion.
  • Measures the resistance of a body to a change in its rotational motion about a specified axis.
  • Depends on the mass of the body and how that mass is distributed relative to the axis of rotation.
  • SI unit: kg·m².
I = Σ mr²   ·   I = ∫ r² dm
  • Greater mass generally produces greater moment of inertia when its distribution is unchanged.
  • Mass farther from the axis contributes more strongly to moment of inertia.
  • A body can have different moments of inertia about different axes.

Parallel Axis Theorem

  • Moment of inertia about any axis parallel to an axis through the centre of mass:
I = IG + Md²
  • IG = moment of inertia about the parallel axis through the centre of mass.
  • M = total mass, d = distance between the two axes.

Perpendicular Axis Theorem (plane lamina)

Iz = Ix + Iy

The moment of inertia about an axis perpendicular to the plane equals the sum of the moments of inertia about two mutually perpendicular axes in the plane, intersecting at the same point.

3.8 Torque or Moment of Force

  • The turning effect of a force about a fixed point or axis.
  • Measures the tendency of a force to rotate a body.
  • SI unit: Newton-metre (N·m).
τ = F × r⊥
τ⃗ = r⃗ × F⃗   ·   τ = rF sin θ
  • Torque increases with applied force.
  • Torque increases with perpendicular distance from the axis.
  • A force acting directly through the axis produces zero torque about that axis.
  • The direction of torque can be determined using the right-hand rule.

Examples in Marine Engineering

  • Torque transmitted by a crankshaft.
  • Torque produced by a marine diesel engine.
  • Torque transmitted through a gearbox.
  • Torque acting on a propeller shaft.

3.9 Relationship Between Torque and Angular Acceleration

τnet = Iα
  • Rotational equivalent of Newton's second law.
  • Greater torque produces greater angular acceleration for a given moment of inertia.
  • Greater moment of inertia produces smaller angular acceleration for a given torque.

4.1 Work

  • Work is said to be done when a force causes displacement of a body.
  • Work is a scalar quantity.
  • SI unit: Joule (J).
W = Fs cos θ

Where W = work done (J), F = applied force (N), s = displacement (m), θ = angle between force and displacement.

Special Cases

  • Force and displacement in the same direction: W = Fs (positive work).
  • Force and displacement in opposite directions: W = −Fs (negative work).
  • Force perpendicular to displacement: W = 0.
  • No displacement: W = 0.

Types of Work

  • Positive Work: Force has a component in the direction of displacement. Example: A motor lifting a load upward.
  • Negative Work: Force has a component opposite to displacement. Example: Friction acting on a sliding object.
  • Zero Work: No displacement or force perpendicular to displacement. Example: Centripetal force in ideal uniform circular motion.

4.2 Work Done by a Variable Force

  • When force changes with displacement, the simple formula W = Fs cannot be directly applied using a single force value.
  • Work is calculated by integrating the force over the displacement.
W = ∫s₁s₂ F(s) ds

On a force–displacement graph, the signed area under the curve represents work done.

4.3 Energy

  • The capacity of a body or system to do work.
  • Scalar quantity; SI unit: Joule (J).
  • Energy can exist in different forms and can be converted from one form to another.

Kinetic Energy

  • Energy possessed by a body due to its motion.
  • Depends on mass and the square of velocity.
KE = ½mv²
  • If velocity doubles, kinetic energy becomes four times.
  • If mass doubles at constant velocity, kinetic energy doubles.
  • A body at rest has zero kinetic energy in the chosen reference frame.

Potential Energy

  • Energy possessed by a body due to its position or configuration.
  • Gravitational potential energy is commonly considered in mechanical engineering.
PE = mgh
  • Potential energy increases with height above the selected reference level.
  • Potential energy is relative to the chosen reference level.
  • A body raised to a greater height has greater gravitational potential energy.

Elastic Potential Energy

PEspring = ½kx²

Where k = spring stiffness (N/m), x = extension or compression (m).

4.4 Mechanical Energy

ME = KE + PE = ½mv² + mgh
  • Mechanical energy can change between kinetic and potential forms.
  • In an ideal system with only conservative forces doing work, total mechanical energy remains constant.

4.5 Law of Conservation of Energy

Statement: Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transformed from one form to another. The total energy of an isolated system remains constant.

  • Energy may change from chemical to thermal, mechanical, electrical or other forms.
  • The total energy is conserved even though useful mechanical energy may decrease due to friction and other dissipative effects.

Examples in Marine Engineering

  • Marine Diesel Engine: Chemical energy of fuel → thermal energy through combustion → high-pressure gases → mechanical work on the piston → crankshaft converts reciprocating motion into rotational mechanical energy.
  • Steam Turbine: Thermal energy of steam → mechanical rotational energy.
  • Electric Motor: Electrical energy → mechanical energy.
  • Generator: Mechanical energy → electrical energy.

4.6 Work–Energy Theorem

Statement: The net work done by all external forces acting on a body is equal to the change in its kinetic energy.

Wnet = KEf − KEi = ½mv² − ½mu²
  • Positive net work increases kinetic energy.
  • Negative net work decreases kinetic energy.
  • Zero net work means no change in kinetic energy.
  • Useful for analysing motion without directly calculating acceleration and time.

4.7 Power

  • The rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.
  • SI unit: Watt (W).
P = Wt
P = dWdt   ·   1 kW = 1000 W   ·   1 MW = 1000 kW

Power in Linear Motion

P = Fv   ·   P = Fv cos θ

Power in Rotational Motion

P = τω

Where P = power (W), τ = torque (N·m), ω = angular velocity (rad/s).

4.8 Engine Power and Rotational Speed

  • Engine power represents the rate at which an engine delivers mechanical work.
  • Marine engines are commonly rated in kilowatts (kW) or megawatts (MW).
  • The power delivered by a rotating shaft depends on its torque and rotational speed.
P = 2πNT60

Where P = power (W), N = rotational speed (RPM), T = torque (N·m).

P(kW) = 2πNT60 × 1000

Brake Power (BP)

  • The useful mechanical power available at the engine output shaft.
  • Commonly measured using a dynamometer or other suitable measurement system.

Indicated Power (IP)

  • The power developed by the gases acting on the piston inside an engine cylinder.
  • Determined from the pressure–volume diagram or an appropriate indicated-power measurement.

Friction Power (FP)

  • The power consumed in overcoming mechanical friction and other internal mechanical losses.
FP = IP − BP

Mechanical Efficiency

ηm = BPIP × 100%

The percentage of indicated power available as brake power.

5.1 Linear Momentum

  • The quantity of motion possessed by a moving body.
  • Defined as the product of mass and velocity.
  • Vector quantity; SI unit: kg·m/s.
p = mv
  • A body at rest has zero linear momentum.
  • Momentum increases with mass or velocity.
  • For the same mass, a faster-moving body has greater momentum.
  • Momentum has the same direction as velocity.
  • A change in velocity produces a change in momentum.
Examples: A moving ship has considerable momentum because of its large mass. A loaded truck has greater momentum than a smaller vehicle moving at the same speed.

5.2 Impulse

  • The product of force and the time interval for which the force acts.
  • Measures the effect of a force acting over a particular duration.
  • Vector quantity; SI unit: Newton-second (N·s).
J = F Δt

For variable force:

J = ∫t₁t₂ F(t) dt

The signed area under a force–time graph represents impulse.

5.3 Impulse–Momentum Theorem

Statement: The impulse of the resultant external force acting on a body is equal to the change in its linear momentum.

J = Δp = mv − mu
F Δt = m(v − u)
  • A force changes the momentum of a body.
  • A greater force acting for the same duration produces a greater change in momentum.
  • The same change in momentum can be achieved using a smaller force acting over a longer duration.
  • The direction of impulse is the direction of the change in momentum.

Practical Applications

  • Airbags in Vehicles: Increase the time over which a passenger's momentum changes during a collision, reducing the average impact force.
  • Catching a Ball: Moving hands backward increases the stopping time and reduces the average force on the hands.
  • Hammering: A hammer produces a large force over a short time interval, creating a significant change in momentum.
  • Ship Propulsion: A propeller changes the momentum of water flowing through it; the change in water momentum is associated with the thrust produced.

5.4 Conservation of Linear Momentum

Statement: If the net external force acting on a system is zero, the total linear momentum of the system remains constant.

m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂
  • Momentum is conserved in an isolated system.
  • External forces may be present, but their resultant impulse must be zero or negligible over the time considered.
  • Internal forces between bodies occur in equal and opposite pairs and cancel when considering the entire system.
  • Momentum conservation applies to elastic and inelastic collisions.

5.5 Collision of Bodies

A collision occurs when two or more bodies come into contact and exert large forces on each other over a relatively short time. For an isolated system, total momentum is conserved.

Elastic Collision

  • Both total momentum and total kinetic energy are conserved.
  • Bodies rebound without a net loss of total kinetic energy.
  • Coefficient of restitution e = 1.

Inelastic Collision

  • Total momentum is conserved, but total kinetic energy is not conserved.
  • Some kinetic energy is transformed into heat, sound, vibration or deformation.
  • Coefficient of restitution 0 < e < 1 for ordinary passive collisions.

Perfectly Inelastic Collision

  • The two bodies stick together after collision and move with a common velocity.
  • Loss of kinetic energy is maximum for a given initial state, subject to momentum conservation.
  • Coefficient of restitution e = 0.

Coefficient of Restitution (e)

  • Measures the degree of elasticity of a collision.
  • Defined as the ratio of relative velocity of separation to relative velocity of approach, measured along the line of impact.
e = Relative velocity of separationRelative velocity of approach
e = v₂ − v₁u₁ − u₂

Assuming body 1 approaches body 2 from behind (u₁ > u₂) and they separate with v₂ > v₁.

Collision TypeCoefficient of Restitution
Perfectly elastice = 1
Partially inelastic0 < e < 1
Perfectly inelastice = 0

5.7 Angular Momentum

  • The rotational equivalent of linear momentum.
  • Represents the quantity of rotational motion possessed by a body.
  • For a rigid body rotating about a fixed principal axis:
L = Iω

Where L = angular momentum (kg·m²/s), I = moment of inertia (kg·m²), ω = angular velocity (rad/s).

  • Angular momentum depends on the moment of inertia and angular velocity.
  • A body with greater rotational speed has greater angular momentum if its moment of inertia remains constant.
  • Angular momentum is a vector quantity.

Angular Impulse

Jθ = τ Δt
τ Δt = Iω₂ − Iω₁ = I(ω₂ − ω₁)

5.8 Conservation of Angular Momentum

Statement: If the resultant external torque acting on a system is zero, its total angular momentum remains constant.

I₁ω₁ = I₂ω₂
  • When the moment of inertia decreases, angular velocity increases if angular momentum is conserved.
  • When the moment of inertia increases, angular velocity decreases.
  • This principle explains changes in the rotational speed of a rotating system when its mass distribution changes.

Applications

  • Flywheels.
  • Rotating machinery.
  • Gyroscopes.
  • Turbine rotors.
  • Rotating bodies changing their mass distribution.

Circular motion is the motion of a body along a circular path around a fixed centre or axis. It is particularly important in mechanical and marine engineering because many rotating components operate on the principles of circular motion.

Examples

  • Marine engine crankshaft.
  • Flywheel.
  • Ship propeller.
  • Turbine rotor.
  • Electric motor rotor.
  • Centrifugal pump impeller.
  • Fan blades.

6.1 What is Circular Motion?

  • A body moves along a circular path.
  • The distance of the body from the centre of rotation remains constant.
  • The direction of the velocity continuously changes.
  • Even when speed remains constant, velocity changes because velocity is a vector.
  • Therefore, circular motion involves acceleration even when the speed is uniform.

Types of Circular Motion

  • Uniform Circular Motion: Constant speed; magnitude of velocity remains constant; direction of velocity continuously changes; centripetal acceleration is present.
  • Non-uniform Circular Motion: Changing speed; both magnitude and direction of velocity change; both tangential and centripetal acceleration are present.

6.2 Angular Displacement in Circular Motion

θ = sr
  • One complete revolution = 360° = 2π radians.
  • Half a revolution = 180° = π radians.
  • One-quarter revolution = 90° = π/2 radians.

6.3 Angular Velocity in Circular Motion

ω = 2πT = 2πf

Where T = time period (s), f = frequency (Hz).

  • Time Period: Time required to complete one full revolution.
  • Frequency: Number of complete revolutions per second: f = 1/T.

6.4 Tangential Velocity

  • The linear velocity of a body moving along a circular path.
  • Acts along the tangent to the circular path at any given point.
  • Direction is perpendicular to the radius drawn from the centre to the body.
v = rω
  • Tangential velocity is always perpendicular to the radius vector in circular motion.
  • At a constant angular velocity, points farther from the axis have greater tangential velocity.
  • All points on a rigid rotating body have the same angular velocity, but their linear velocities can differ.

6.5 Centripetal Acceleration

  • The acceleration directed towards the centre of a circular path.
  • Responsible for continuously changing the direction of velocity during circular motion.
  • Present even when the body moves at constant speed.
  • SI unit: m/s².
ac = v²r = rω²
  • Centripetal acceleration always acts towards the centre.
  • Directly proportional to the square of linear velocity.
  • Increases when velocity increases.
  • For constant speed, it increases when the radius decreases.
  • Responsible for changing the direction of motion, not the magnitude of velocity.

6.6 Centripetal Force

  • The resultant inward force required to keep a body moving along a circular path.
  • Acts towards the centre of the circular path.
  • Not a separate fundamental type of force — it is the name given to the net inward force responsible for circular motion.
  • May be provided by tension, friction, gravity, normal reaction or a combination of forces.
Fc = mv²r = mrω²
  • Centripetal force always acts towards the centre.
  • Directly proportional to mass and to the square of tangential velocity.
  • Inversely proportional to the radius when mass and linear velocity are constant.
  • If velocity doubles (radius and mass constant), centripetal force becomes four times.
  • If mass doubles (velocity and radius constant), centripetal force doubles.

6.7 Centrifugal Force

  • An apparent or pseudo force observed in a rotating reference frame.
  • Acts radially outward, away from the centre of rotation.
  • Same magnitude as the centripetal force in the corresponding uniform circular motion description, but opposite direction in rotating-frame analysis.
Fcentrifugal = mv²r
  • Centripetal force is the real inward resultant force in an inertial reference frame.
  • Centrifugal force is introduced when analysing motion from a rotating, non-inertial reference frame.
  • Centrifugal force is not an additional outward force acting on the body in the inertial-frame description.
  • These two forces should not be treated as an action–reaction pair under Newton's third law.
Example: When a vehicle takes a sharp turn, passengers feel as if they are pushed outward. This sensation is associated with inertia and can be described using an apparent centrifugal force in the vehicle's rotating frame.

6.8 Difference Between Centripetal and Centrifugal Force

Centripetal ForceCentrifugal Force
Acts towards the centreActs away from the centre

6.9 Tangential Acceleration

  • The component of acceleration acting along the tangent to a circular path.
  • Responsible for changing the magnitude of tangential velocity or speed.
  • Occurs when the rotational speed of a body increases or decreases.
at = rα
  • Centripetal acceleration changes the direction of velocity.
  • Tangential acceleration changes the magnitude of velocity.

6.10 Total Acceleration in Non-uniform Circular Motion

The body experiences both centripetal and tangential acceleration, which are perpendicular to each other.

a = √(ac² + at²)
Remember: Uniform circular motion → only centripetal acceleration. Non-uniform circular motion → both centripetal and tangential acceleration.

6.11 Applications in Marine Engineering

Applications

  • Marine Propeller: Rotates about its shaft axis; blades move in circular paths; rotational motion produces hydrodynamic forces for thrust.
  • Centrifugal Pump: Impeller rotates at high speed; fluid receives energy from the rotating impeller; centrifugal effects and pressure gradients move fluid.
  • Marine Diesel Engine: Crankshaft rotates; connecting rod and crank convert reciprocating piston motion into rotational motion; rotating crankshaft and flywheel experience centripetal acceleration.
  • Turbocharger: Turbine and compressor wheels rotate at very high speeds; rotating components experience centripetal acceleration; proper balancing is essential.
  • Flywheel: Stores rotational kinetic energy and helps reduce fluctuations in rotational speed.
  • Centrifugal Separator: Rotating bowls create high centrifugal acceleration; density differences separate oil, water and solid contaminants in fuel-oil and lubricating-oil purification.
