2.1 Definition

  • Orthographic projection is a method of representing a three-dimensional object using multiple two-dimensional views.
  • The projection lines are perpendicular (90°) to the projection plane.
  • It allows engineers to understand the exact shape, dimensions, and features of an object.
  • It is one of the most commonly used methods in mechanical engineering drawings.

2.2 Principle of Orthographic Projection

  • Imagine an object placed inside an imaginary transparent box.
  • The object is viewed from different directions.
  • Each view is projected onto a corresponding plane.
  • The resulting views show the object's length, width, and height.

The three principal dimensions are:

  1. Length – Horizontal dimension.
  2. Width – Depth dimension.
  3. Height – Vertical dimension.

2.3 Principal Views

The six principal views are:

  1. Front view (elevation).
  2. Top view (plan).
  3. Bottom view.
  4. Rear view.
  5. Left-side view.
  6. Right-side view.

Three most commonly used views:

  • Front view: Shows length and height.
  • Top view: Shows length and width.
  • Side view: Shows width and height.

Usually, three views are sufficient to represent a simple mechanical component. Complex objects may require additional views.

2.4 Types of Orthographic Projection

There are two main projection systems used in engineering drawing:

A. First-Angle Projection

  • The object is placed between the observer and the projection plane.
  • The object is considered to be in the first quadrant.
  • Views are arranged on the opposite side of the direction from which they are viewed.

Arrangement:

  • Top view is placed below the front view.
  • Bottom view is placed above the front view.
  • Right-side view is placed on the left side.
  • Left-side view is placed on the right side.

Application:

  • Commonly used in India and many European engineering drawing practices.
  • Used in mechanical manufacturing drawings.

B. Third-Angle Projection

  • The projection plane is between the observer and the object.
  • The object is considered to be in the third quadrant.
  • Views are placed on the same side from which they are observed.

Arrangement:

  • Top view is placed above the front view.
  • Bottom view is placed below the front view.
  • Right-side view is placed on the right side.
  • Left-side view is placed on the left side.

Application:

  • Commonly used in the United States and Canada.

2.5 Projection Symbol

The symbol generally consists of a truncated cone and its circular end view.

2.6 Important Characteristics

  • Projection lines are perpendicular to the projection plane.
  • Parallel edges remain parallel in orthographic projection.
  • Actual dimensions can be obtained from the appropriate view when the surface is parallel to the projection plane.
  • Hidden details are represented using dashed lines.
  • Centre lines represent cylindrical features and axes.
  • Different views must be properly aligned.

  • Cylinder head drawings.
  • Piston and connecting rod drawings.
  • Pump casing drawings.
  • Valve body drawings.
  • Shaft and bearing drawings.
  • Propeller shaft arrangement drawings.

3.1 Definition

  • Isometric projection is a pictorial method of representing a three-dimensional object on a two-dimensional surface.
  • It shows three dimensions simultaneously.
  • The three principal axes are equally inclined to the projection plane.
  • The angle between any two isometric axes is 120°.
  • It provides a clear visual representation of the overall shape of an object.

3.2 Isometric Axes

In a standard isometric drawing:

  • One axis is vertical.
  • The other two axes are inclined at 30° to the horizontal.
  • The angle between any two axes is 120°.
  • Length, width, and height are represented along these three axes.

3.3 Isometric Projection vs Isometric View

These terms are related but technically different.

Isometric projection:

  • Uses an isometric scale.
  • All three axes are equally foreshortened.
  • The projected lengths are approximately 81.65% of the actual lengths.
Li = 0.8165 L

Where:

  • Li = Isometric projected length.
  • L = Actual length.

Isometric view (isometric drawing):

  • Uses actual dimensions along the isometric axes.
  • It is approximately 1.225 times larger than the corresponding isometric projection for the same actual dimensions.
  • Commonly used for practical engineering illustrations.

3.4 Characteristics of Isometric Drawing

  1. Three faces of an object can be seen simultaneously.
  2. Vertical edges remain vertical.
  3. Horizontal edges are drawn at 30° to the horizontal.
  4. Parallel edges remain parallel.
  5. Circles appear as ellipses on isometric planes.
  6. All three principal axes have equal scale in isometric projection.
  7. It helps understand complex components more easily.

3.5 Isometric Drawing of Common Shapes

Cube:

  • Three visible faces are represented.
  • All edges have equal length.
  • The three principal axes are 120° apart.

Cylinder:

  • Circular ends appear elliptical.
  • The cylindrical surface is represented using parallel inclined edges.

Cone:

  • Circular base appears elliptical.
  • The apex is connected to the elliptical base boundary.

Sphere:

  • A sphere appears circular in isometric projection.
  • Its overall outline remains circular.

Hole and circular features:

  • Circles on isometric planes appear as ellipses.
  • Ellipses represent openings, bores, and cylindrical features.

4.1 Definition

  • A sectional view is a drawing that shows the internal construction of an object by imagining it cut along a particular plane.
  • It is used when internal details cannot be clearly understood from ordinary external views.
  • The imaginary cutting plane removes a portion of the object to expose its internal features.
  • The exposed surfaces are represented using hatching or section lines.

4.2 Purpose of Sectional Views

  1. Show internal holes and cavities.
  2. Show internal passages.
  3. Explain the construction of complicated components.
  4. Reduce the number of hidden lines.
  5. Improve drawing clarity.
  6. Help manufacturing and inspection.
  7. Help understand assembly arrangements.

4.3 Cutting Plane

  • A cutting plane is an imaginary plane passing through an object.
  • It identifies the location where the object is considered cut.
  • Its position is shown using a cutting-plane line.
  • Arrows indicate the direction of viewing.
  • Letters such as A–A or B–B identify the section.

4.4 Types of Sectional Views

A. Full Section

  • The cutting plane passes completely through the object.
  • The entire internal construction along the cutting plane is exposed.
  • Used when complete internal details are required.

B. Half Section

  • Used mainly for symmetrical objects.
  • One half shows the external view.
  • The other half shows the internal sectional view.
  • The dividing line is generally the centre line.

Application: Symmetrical components such as pulleys, bearings, flanges, and valve bodies.

C. Offset Section

  • The cutting plane changes direction to pass through multiple important features.
  • Used when internal features are not located along one straight plane.
  • The resulting section is represented as though the cutting plane were straightened.

D. Broken-Out Section

  • Only a small portion of the object is removed.
  • The internal details of that particular region are exposed.
  • A freehand or irregular break line identifies the removed portion.

E. Revolved Section

  • The cross-section of an object is rotated through 90° and displayed directly on the view.
  • Useful for components having changing cross-sections.
  • Commonly used for shafts, rods, arms, and similar parts.

F. Removed Section

  • A cross-section is drawn separately from the main view.
  • Used when the section cannot be clearly shown on the original view.

4.5 Hatching and Section Lines

  • Hatching indicates material that has been cut by the imaginary cutting plane.
  • Normally, hatching lines are thin and equally spaced.
  • They are generally inclined at 45° to the main outlines or axes.
  • Adjacent components in an assembly section may use different hatch directions or spacing.
  • The same component should retain consistent hatching throughout the sectional representation.
Important conventions:
  • Ribs, webs, spokes, shafts, bolts, nuts, and similar standard components are often not hatched when cut longitudinally, according to applicable drawing conventions.
  • A hollow component shows the wall thickness and internal cavity.
  • Section lines should not be confused with centre lines or hidden lines.

Marine Engineering Applications

  • Marine diesel engine cylinder heads.
  • Piston and piston-ring arrangements.
  • Fuel injection valves.
  • Starting-air valves.
  • Lubricating-oil pumps.
  • Centrifugal pump impellers and casings.
  • Heat exchangers.
  • Sea-water valves.
  • Boiler components.
  • Bearings and stern-tube arrangements.

5.1 Dimensioning

  • Dimensioning is the process of specifying the size, location, and geometric characteristics of an object on an engineering drawing.
  • Dimensions are required to manufacture and inspect components.
  • They communicate the actual intended size of an object.

Types of dimensions:

  1. Linear dimensions – Length, width, thickness, and height.
  2. Angular dimensions – Angles in degrees.
  3. Diameter dimensions – Diameter of circular features.
  4. Radius dimensions – Radius of arcs and curved surfaces.
  5. Chamfer dimensions – Size and angle of chamfers.
  6. Location dimensions – Distance between features.
  7. Depth dimensions – Depth of holes and recesses.
  8. Thread dimensions – Thread diameter, pitch, and other specifications.

5.2 Elements of Dimensioning

1. Dimension line

  • Thin continuous line showing the extent and direction of a dimension.
  • Generally terminates with arrowheads.

2. Extension line

  • Thin line extending from the object to indicate the limits of the dimension.
  • Usually begins slightly away from the object's outline.

3. Arrowheads

  • Indicate the beginning and end of a dimension.
  • Normally point towards the extension lines.

4. Dimension figure

  • Indicates the numerical value of the dimension.
  • Usually expressed in millimetres in mechanical engineering.

5. Leader line

  • Connects a note or dimension to a particular feature.
  • Used for hole specifications, surface finish, material notes, etc.

6. Centre line

  • Indicates the centre of circular or symmetrical features.
  • Helps position dimensions accurately.

5.4 Methods of Dimensioning

A. Chain Dimensioning

  • Dimensions are arranged consecutively from one feature to another.
  • Each dimension starts where the previous dimension ends.
  • Easy to apply for simple components.
  • Errors can accumulate because each dimension has its own tolerance.

B. Baseline or Datum Dimensioning

  • All dimensions are measured from a common reference point or datum.
  • Reduces the accumulation of dimensional errors.
  • Useful for precision components.

C. Combined Dimensioning

  • Uses both chain and baseline dimensioning.
  • Used when different parts of a component require different dimensioning arrangements.

D. Parallel Dimensioning

  • Several dimensions are measured from a common datum.
  • Dimension lines are arranged parallel to each other.

E. Coordinate Dimensioning

  • Defines feature positions using X and Y coordinates.
  • Commonly used in CNC machining and CAD systems.

5.5 Tolerances

Definition:

  • Tolerance is the permissible variation in a dimension from its specified nominal value.
  • No manufacturing process can produce every component with perfectly identical dimensions.
  • Tolerances specify the acceptable limits of variation.

Example

A shaft is specified as:

25 ± 0.05 mm

Therefore:

  • Maximum size = 25.05 mm.
  • Minimum size = 24.95 mm.
  • Tolerance = 0.10 mm.
Tolerance = Upper Limit − Lower Limit

5.6 Types of Tolerances

1. Unilateral tolerance

Variation is permitted in only one direction from the nominal dimension.

25+0.02−0.00 mm

Permissible size: 25.00–25.02 mm.

2. Bilateral tolerance

Variation is permitted in both positive and negative directions.

25 ± 0.05 mm

Permissible size: 24.95–25.05 mm.

3. Limit dimensioning

Maximum and minimum permissible dimensions are directly specified.

  • Maximum = 25.05 mm.
  • Minimum = 24.95 mm.

5.7 Fits

A fit describes the relationship between two mating components, usually a hole and a shaft.

A. Clearance Fit

  • The hole is always larger than the shaft.
  • There is always clearance between the components.
  • Allows relative movement or easy assembly.

Applications:

  • Rotating shafts.
  • Sliding mechanisms.
  • Bearings with specified running clearance.

B. Interference Fit

  • The shaft is always larger than the hole.
  • Assembly requires force, heating, cooling, or another suitable fitting method.
  • Produces a tight connection.

Applications:

  • Press-fitted gears.
  • Flywheels on shafts.
  • Bearing inner rings mounted on shafts where specified.

C. Transition Fit

  • May produce either a small clearance or a small interference.
  • Used when accurate positioning is required.
  • Provides a close fit between mating parts.

Applications:

  • Precision couplings.
  • Locating components.
  • Accurate mechanical assemblies.

5.8 Hole Basis and Shaft Basis Systems

Hole Basis System:

  • The hole size is kept as the basic reference.
  • Different fits are obtained by changing the shaft dimensions.
  • The basic hole generally has a lower deviation of zero.
  • Widely used in manufacturing.

Shaft Basis System:

  • The shaft size is kept as the basic reference.
  • Different fits are obtained by changing the hole dimensions.
  • The basic shaft generally has an upper deviation of zero.

6.1 Definition

  • GD&T stands for Geometric Dimensioning and Tolerancing.
  • It is a standardized engineering drawing system used to specify the allowable variation in the geometry of a component.
  • Ordinary dimensional tolerances control the size of a feature.
  • GD&T controls geometric characteristics such as form, orientation, location, and runout.
  • It ensures that components fit and function correctly even when manufactured within permissible variations.

6.2 Why GD&T Is Required

Consider a shaft having the correct diameter but a bent or misaligned axis.

  • Its diameter may satisfy the dimensional tolerance.
  • However, it may not rotate properly.
  • It may cause vibration, excessive bearing wear, or misalignment.

GD&T helps control these geometric variations.

6.3 Datums

  • A datum is a theoretically exact reference point, axis, plane, or surface used for measurement.
  • Datums provide a reference system for controlling geometric features.
  • They are generally identified using capital letters such as A, B, and C.

Example

  • Datum A – Bottom mounting surface.
  • Datum B – Central axis of a hole.
  • Datum C – Side reference surface.

These datums establish a reference for inspecting the position and orientation of other features.

6.4 Categories of Geometric Tolerances

There are five main categories commonly taught in GD&T.

A. Form Tolerances

Control the shape of individual features without requiring a datum reference.

  1. Straightness – Controls how straight a line element or axis must be. Used for shafts, guide surfaces, and linear features.
  2. Flatness – Controls the flatness of a surface. Important for mating and sealing surfaces.
  3. Circularity (Roundness) – Controls the roundness of a circular cross-section. Important for shafts, holes, and cylindrical components.
  4. Cylindricity – Controls the overall cylindrical form of a surface. Controls circularity and straightness of the cylindrical surface together.

B. Orientation Tolerances

Control the angular relationship between features.

  1. Parallelism – Ensures that one surface or axis remains parallel to a datum.
  2. Perpendicularity – Ensures that a surface or axis is at 90° to a datum.
  3. Angularity – Controls a feature at a specified angle relative to a datum.

C. Location Tolerances

Control the position of features.

  1. Position – Controls the allowable variation in the location of a feature. Commonly used for bolt holes, mounting holes, and drilled patterns.
  2. Concentricity – Controls the relationship between the median points of circular features. It is a relatively complex control and is not commonly preferred for general applications.
  3. Symmetry – Controls the location of a median plane relative to a datum. Also relatively uncommon in modern GD&T applications.

D. Profile Tolerances

Control the shape of lines and surfaces.

  1. Profile of a Line – Controls the allowable variation of a two-dimensional profile. Useful for curved sections and contours.
  2. Profile of a Surface – Controls the allowable variation of a three-dimensional surface. Used for complex surfaces such as castings and turbine components.

E. Runout Tolerances

Control the variation of a surface during rotation about a datum axis.

  1. Circular Runout – Controls the variation at each circular cross-section when the component rotates around its datum axis. Used for shafts, rotating discs, and cylindrical parts.
  2. Total Runout – Controls the variation over the entire surface during rotation. Provides more comprehensive control than circular runout.

6.5 GD&T Feature Control Frame

  • A feature control frame is a rectangular box used to specify geometric tolerances.
  • It contains the geometric characteristic symbol, tolerance value, and datum references where required.

6.6 GD&T Material Condition Modifiers

MMC – Maximum Material Condition

  • Represents the condition in which a feature contains the maximum amount of material.
  • For a shaft, it corresponds to the largest permitted diameter.
  • For a hole, it corresponds to the smallest permitted diameter.

LMC – Least Material Condition

  • Represents the condition in which a feature contains the minimum amount of material.
  • For a shaft, it corresponds to the smallest permitted diameter.
  • For a hole, it corresponds to the largest permitted diameter.

RFS – Regardless of Feature Size

  • Geometric tolerance applies regardless of the actual size of the feature.
  • It is the default condition for many geometric tolerances under ASME GD&T rules.

Importance in Marine Engineering

  • Crankshaft alignment.
  • Shaft runout.
  • Bearing housing geometry.
  • Engine mounting surfaces.
  • Gear alignment.
  • Coupling alignment.
  • Propeller shaft components.
Remember the five GD&T categories:
  • Form – Shape.
  • Orientation – Direction.
  • Location – Position.
  • Profile – Contour.
  • Runout – Rotational variation.

7.1 Definition

  • Surface finish describes the texture and quality of a manufactured surface.
  • It is influenced by machining processes, material properties, cutting tools, and manufacturing conditions.
  • Surface finish affects friction, wear, lubrication, sealing, fatigue strength, and corrosion behaviour.

7.2 Surface Texture Parameters

The most commonly encountered parameter is Ra.

Ra – Arithmetic Average Roughness

  • Ra is the arithmetic average of the absolute deviations of the measured surface profile from its mean line over the sampling length.
  • It is commonly expressed in micrometres (µm).
Ra = 1L ∫0L |y(x)| dx

Where:

  • L = Sampling length.
  • y(x) = Deviation of the measured profile from the mean line.

7.3 Surface Finish Symbols

Three basic symbol conditions are important:

1. Basic Surface Texture Symbol

  • Indicates that a surface texture requirement is specified.
  • Does not by itself indicate whether material removal is required or prohibited.

2. Material Removal Required

  • A bar is added to the basic symbol.
  • Indicates that material removal, such as machining or grinding, is required.

3. Material Removal Prohibited

  • A circle is added to the basic symbol.
  • Indicates that material removal is not permitted.
  • Often used for cast, forged, or other as-produced surfaces.

7.6 Importance of Surface Finish in Marine Machinery

  1. Crankshaft journals require suitable surface finish to reduce friction and bearing wear.
  2. Cylinder liners require a controlled surface texture for proper piston-ring lubrication and running-in.
  3. Piston-ring surfaces require suitable finish to promote sealing and minimize wear.
  4. Valve seats require good surface finish to prevent leakage.
  5. Pump shaft sealing surfaces require appropriate finish for mechanical seals.

  7. Gasket mating surfaces require suitable finish to ensure proper sealing.

8.1 Definition

  • Welding symbols are standardized graphical representations used in engineering drawings to communicate welding requirements.
  • They indicate the type of weld, location, size, length, pitch, and other welding details.
  • They help welders manufacture joints according to design specifications.

8.2 Basic Elements of Welding Symbols

A welding symbol generally consists of:

  1. Arrow – Points towards the joint to be welded.
  2. Reference line – Contains the welding information.
  3. Basic weld symbol – Identifies the type of weld.
  4. Dimensions – Specify weld size, length, and pitch.
  5. Tail – Contains additional information such as welding process or specification.
  6. Supplementary symbols – Provide additional weld requirements.
Important: The side of the reference line on which the symbol is placed determines the weld side according to the applicable standard.

8.3 Common Types of Welds

1. Fillet Weld

  • Used to join two surfaces approximately perpendicular to each other.
  • Commonly used in T-joints, lap joints, and corner joints.
  • Has an approximately triangular cross-section.
  • Common in structural fabrication.

2. Butt Weld

  • Used to join two components in approximately the same plane.
  • Common in pipelines, plates, and pressure vessels.
  • Can be made using different groove preparations.

3. Square Groove Weld

  • Used when two plates have square edges.
  • Requires little or no edge preparation depending on thickness and welding procedure.

4. V-Groove Weld

  • The edges are prepared to form a V-shaped groove.
  • Used for thicker materials to achieve proper penetration.

5. Double V-Groove Weld

  • Both sides are prepared in a V-shaped configuration.
  • Can reduce weld metal volume compared with some single-sided preparations.

6. Plug Weld

  • Used to join overlapping components through a circular hole in one component.

7. Slot Weld

  • Similar to a plug weld but uses an elongated slot.

8. Seam Weld

  • A continuous weld made along a joint.
  • Used in tanks, sheet metal structures, and certain fabricated components.

9. Spot Weld

  • Produces localized welded joints.
  • Commonly used for sheet metal fabrication.

8.4 Fillet Weld Dimensions

Important terms:

  • Leg length – Distance from the root of the weld to the toe along each adjoining member.
  • Throat thickness – Shortest distance from the root to the face of the fillet weld.
  • Weld face – Exposed surface of the weld.
  • Weld root – Point where the weld joins the base materials.
  • Weld toe – Junction between the weld face and base material.

For an ideal equal-leg, 90° fillet weld:

t = 0.707 s

Where:

  • t = Theoretical throat thickness.
  • s = Fillet weld leg length.

8.5 Types of Welding Joints

  1. Butt joint – Two members are joined edge to edge.
  2. Lap joint – One member overlaps another.
  3. T-joint – One member is positioned approximately perpendicular to another.
  4. Corner joint – Two members meet at a corner.
  5. Edge joint – Two parallel or nearly parallel edges are joined.

8.6 Supplementary Welding Symbols

  • All-around weld symbol – Indicates welding around the complete joint.
  • Field weld symbol – Indicates welding performed at the installation site rather than in the workshop.
  • Contour symbol – Indicates the required final weld surface shape.
  • Finish symbol – Indicates how the weld surface should be finished.
  • Backing symbol – Indicates a backing arrangement for the weld.
  • Melt-through symbol – Indicates the required penetration condition for applicable joint configurations.

9.1 Definition

  • An assembly drawing represents the arrangement of different components when assembled to form a complete machine or mechanism.
  • It shows the relationship between individual components.
  • It helps engineers understand how parts fit, interact, and function together.
  • It is mainly used for assembly, installation, inspection, maintenance, and dismantling.

9.2 Information Contained in Assembly Drawings

  1. Overall dimensions.
  2. Component arrangement.
  3. Component identification numbers.
  4. Part names.
  5. Fastening arrangements.
  6. Mating surfaces.
  7. Fits and clearances.
  8. Assembly orientation.
  9. Sectional views.
  10. Bill of Materials (BOM).

9.3 Bill of Materials (BOM)

  • BOM is a structured list of all components required to manufacture or assemble a product.
  • It identifies the individual parts and their quantities.

9.4 Types of Assembly Drawings

1. General Assembly Drawing

  • Shows the overall arrangement of the machine.
  • Identifies major components.
  • Used to understand the complete machine.

2. Detailed Assembly Drawing

  • Shows individual parts and their assembly relationships in greater detail.
  • Provides additional information for assembly work.

3. Subassembly Drawing

  • Represents a smaller assembly that forms part of a larger machine.
  • Used when a machine consists of several major units.

4. Sectional Assembly Drawing

  • Shows internal construction and relationships between assembled parts.
  • Useful for complicated machinery.

10.1 Definition

  • An exploded view is a pictorial representation of an assembly in which its individual components are separated from their normal assembled positions.
  • Components are arranged along imaginary lines called explosion lines.
  • It shows how different parts fit together and their relative positions.
  • It is particularly useful for assembly and dismantling instructions.

10.2 Main Features

  1. Components are separated from their assembled positions.
  2. Relative positions and orientations are maintained.
  3. Explosion lines indicate the direction of assembly.
  4. Each component is identified by a balloon number or item number.
  5. A BOM is generally provided.
  6. Fasteners and small components are shown separately.
  7. The view helps understand the assembly sequence.

10.3 Advantages

  • Easy identification of individual components.
  • Simplifies assembly and dismantling.
  • Helps identify replacement parts.
  • Useful for maintenance manuals.
  • Reduces assembly mistakes.
  • Helps technicians understand complicated machinery.

11.1 Definition

  • P&ID stands for Piping and Instrumentation Diagram.
  • It is a detailed schematic representation of a process system.
  • It shows piping, equipment, valves, instruments, control devices, and their connections.
  • It explains how fluids move through a system and how the process is monitored and controlled.
  • It is one of the most important technical drawings used in marine engineering.

11.2 Purpose of P&ID

  1. Understand fluid flow through a system.
  2. Identify equipment and pipelines.
  3. Locate valves and control devices.
  4. Understand instrumentation arrangements.
  5. Understand automatic control systems.
  6. Assist in maintenance and troubleshooting.
  7. Help identify isolation points.
  8. Support safe operation of machinery.

11.3 Common P&ID Symbols

Equipment symbols:

  • Pump – Used to move liquids.
  • Compressor – Used to increase gas pressure.
  • Heat exchanger – Transfers heat between fluids.
  • Tank – Stores liquids.
  • Boiler – Produces steam.
  • Filter – Removes unwanted particles.
  • Separator – Separates different phases.

Valve symbols:

  • Gate valve – Used mainly for isolation.
  • Globe valve – Used for throttling and flow regulation.
  • Check valve – Prevents reverse flow.
  • Butterfly valve – Used for isolation and regulation.
  • Ball valve – Provides quick shut-off.
  • Safety valve – Protects equipment against excessive pressure.
  • Control valve – Automatically regulates flow or pressure.

Instrumentation tags usually contain letters and numbers.

Examples:

  • PI – Pressure Indicator – Measures and displays pressure.
  • PT – Pressure Transmitter – Measures pressure and transmits a signal to a control system.
  • PS – Pressure Switch – Changes electrical contact state at a specified pressure.
  • TI – Temperature Indicator – Displays temperature.
  • TT – Temperature Transmitter – Transmits measured temperature.
  • TC – Temperature Controller – Controls temperature based on a setpoint.
  • FI – Flow Indicator – Displays the flow rate.
  • FT – Flow Transmitter – Transmits measured flow.
  • LI – Level Indicator – Displays liquid level.
  • LT – Level Transmitter – Transmits liquid-level measurement.
  • LC – Level Controller – Controls liquid level.

11.5 Basic Control Loop

A basic automatic control loop contains:

  1. Sensor – Detects the process variable.
  2. Transmitter – Converts measurement into a suitable signal.
  3. Controller – Compares actual measurement with the setpoint.
  4. Final control element – Changes the process, such as a control valve.
  5. Process – The system being controlled.

Example: Temperature control of a heat exchanger

  • A temperature sensor measures outlet temperature.
  • A transmitter sends the measured value to the controller.
  • The controller compares it with the desired temperature.
  • The control valve adjusts the heating-medium flow.
  • The outlet temperature changes accordingly.

11.6 Common P&ID Line Types

  • Process lines – Carry the main process fluid.
  • Utility lines – Carry steam, cooling water, compressed air, etc.
  • Instrument signal lines – Carry control and measurement signals.
  • Electrical signal lines – Represent electrical connections.
  • Pneumatic signal lines – Represent compressed-air instrument signals.
  • Hydraulic signal lines – Represent hydraulic control connections.

Line conventions can vary depending on the applicable P&ID standard and drawing legend.

11.9 Example: Marine Cooling-Water System

A typical jacket cooling-water system may include:

  1. Cooling-water pump.
  2. Engine jacket-water passages.
  3. Temperature-control valve.
  4. Heat exchanger.
  5. Expansion tank.
  6. Pressure and temperature indicators.
  7. Isolation valves.
  8. Vent and drain connections.

Working principle:

  • The pump circulates cooling water through the engine.
  • The water absorbs heat from engine components.
  • Hot water flows to a temperature-control arrangement.
  • Heat is rejected through a cooler.
  • The cooled water returns to the engine.
  • Instruments monitor pressure and temperature to ensure safe operation.

The exact arrangement varies between engine designs.

Important: A P&ID is not a physical piping installation drawing. It primarily communicates system function and connections rather than exact pipe routing and dimensions.

12.1 Definition

  • An electrical schematic is a graphical representation of an electrical circuit.
  • It uses standardized symbols to represent electrical components and their connections.
  • It explains how electricity flows through a circuit and how components operate.
  • Electrical schematics are important for understanding marine electrical systems, control circuits, and automation.

12.2 Common Electrical Symbols

A. Basic Electrical Components

  • Resistor – Opposes the flow of electric current. Its unit is ohm (Ω). Used to limit current and divide voltage.
  • Variable Resistor – Resistance can be adjusted. Used for controlling voltage or current in suitable circuits.
  • Capacitor – Stores electrical energy in an electric field. Its unit is farad (F). Used in filtering, timing, and power-factor correction applications.
  • Inductor – Stores energy in a magnetic field. Its unit is henry (H). Used in filtering and electrical machines.
  • Diode – Allows current to flow mainly in one direction. Used for rectification and circuit protection.
  • LED – Light Emitting Diode. Produces light when forward biased.
  • Transistor – Used for switching and amplification. Important in electronic control circuits.

B. Power Supply Symbols

  • Cell – Represents a single electrochemical cell.
  • Battery – Represents multiple cells or a battery arrangement. Supplies DC electrical power.
  • AC Supply – Represents an alternating-current supply.
  • DC Supply – Represents a direct-current supply.
  • Earth/Ground – Represents an electrical grounding or reference connection according to the circuit convention.

C. Switching and Protection Components

  • Switch – Opens or closes an electrical circuit.
  • Push Button – Momentary-operated switching device. Can be normally open (NO) or normally closed (NC).
  • Fuse – Protects a circuit by melting when excessive current flows.
  • Circuit Breaker – Automatically interrupts current during abnormal conditions. Can generally be reset after the fault has been cleared.
  • Relay – An electrically operated switch. Used to control one circuit through another circuit.
  • Contactor – An electrically controlled switching device. Commonly used for switching motors and other power loads.

D. Electrical Machines

  • Motor – Converts electrical energy into mechanical energy.
  • Generator – Converts mechanical energy into electrical energy.
  • Transformer – Transfers electrical energy between circuits through electromagnetic induction. Changes voltage and current levels according to its turns ratio.
  • Three-Phase Motor – Operates using a three-phase AC supply. Commonly used in pumps, fans, compressors, and other shipboard machinery.

E. Measuring Instruments

  • Ammeter (A) – Measures electric current.
  • Voltmeter (V) – Measures voltage.
  • Wattmeter (W) – Measures electrical power.
  • Ohmmeter (Ω) – Measures resistance.
  • Frequency meter (Hz) – Measures frequency.
  • Energy meter (kWh) – Measures electrical energy.

12.3 Normally Open and Normally Closed Contacts

Normally Open (NO):

  • Contact remains open in its normal, unoperated condition.
  • Current cannot flow through the contact until it is operated.

Normally Closed (NC):

  • Contact remains closed in its normal, unoperated condition.
  • Current can flow through the contact until it is operated.

These terms refer to the normal state of the device, not necessarily the energized state.

12.4 Electrical Circuit Diagrams

1. Power Circuit

  • Carries electrical power to loads.
  • Contains main switching and protection devices.
  • Used for motors and other high-power equipment.

2. Control Circuit

  • Controls the operation of electrical equipment.
  • Uses relays, contactors, push buttons, and sensors.

3. Single-Line Diagram (SLD)

  • Represents a three-phase electrical system using a single line.
  • Shows the major electrical components and power distribution arrangement.

4. Wiring Diagram

  • Shows actual electrical connections between terminals and components.
  • Used during installation and troubleshooting.

  • Main switchboard.
  • Emergency switchboard.
  • Generator control panels.
  • Motor starter circuits.
  • Bilge pump electrical circuits.
  • Navigation lighting circuits.

Important marine concepts:

  • Electrical drawings help identify circuit protection, earthing, isolation, and interlocking arrangements.

14.1 Definition of CAD

  • CAD stands for Computer-Aided Design.
  • CAD is the use of computer software to create, modify, analyze, and document engineering designs.
  • It replaces or supplements traditional manual drafting.
  • It helps engineers create accurate 2D drawings and 3D models.
  • CAD is widely used in mechanical engineering, manufacturing, shipbuilding, and marine machinery design.

14.2 Advantages of CAD

  1. High drawing accuracy.
  2. Faster drawing creation.
  3. Easy modification and editing.
  4. Better visualization.
  5. Easy dimensioning.
  6. Reusable design components.
  7. Improved documentation.
  8. Reduced manual drafting errors.
  9. Easy storage and sharing.
  10. Supports manufacturing and simulation workflows.

14.3 Types of CAD

1. 2D CAD

  • Creates drawings in two dimensions.
  • Uses length and width.
  • Represents objects through lines, circles, arcs, and other geometric entities.
  • Commonly used for engineering layouts, floor plans, and mechanical detail drawings.

2. 3D CAD

  • Creates three-dimensional digital models.
  • Uses length, width, and height.
  • Helps visualize the actual shape of components.
  • Supports assembly design, interference checking, and engineering analysis.

14.6 Important 2D CAD Concepts

1. Layers

  • Layers organize different drawing elements.
  • Different components can be placed on separate layers.
  • Layers can have different colours, line types, and line weights.
  • Example: Dimensions, centre lines, hidden lines, and object outlines can be maintained separately.

2. Object Snap (OSNAP)

  • Helps select exact geometric points.
  • Improves drawing accuracy.

Common object snaps:

  • Endpoint – End of a line.
  • Midpoint – Middle of a line.
  • Centre – Centre of a circle or arc.
  • Intersection – Intersection of objects.
  • Tangent – Tangent point on a curve.
  • Perpendicular – Point forming 90° with another object.

3. Ortho Mode

  • Restricts cursor movement to horizontal and vertical directions.

4. Polar Tracking

  • Helps draw lines at specified angular increments.

5. Grid and Snap

  • Grid displays a reference pattern.
  • Snap restricts cursor movement to specified intervals.

14.7 Basic 3D CAD Concepts

1. Sketching

  • The first step in many parametric modelling workflows.
  • Creates a 2D profile using lines, circles, arcs, and other geometric elements.
  • The sketch is used to generate a 3D feature.

2. Extrude

  • Converts a 2D sketch into a 3D solid by extending it along a specified direction.
  • Example: A rectangle extruded to form a rectangular block.

3. Revolve

  • Rotates a 2D profile around an axis to create a 3D shape.
  • Example: A shaft, pulley, or cylindrical component.

4. Sweep

  • Moves a profile along a defined path.
  • Example: A curved pipe or tube.

5. Loft

  • Creates a 3D shape by connecting multiple cross-sectional profiles.
  • Example: A transition duct or complex housing.

6. Fillet

  • Adds a rounded transition between edges or surfaces.

7. Chamfer

  • Creates a bevelled edge.

8. Shell

  • Removes material from the interior of a solid to create a hollow component with a specified wall thickness.

9. Hole

  • Creates holes with specified diameters, depths, and other characteristics.

10. Pattern

  • Repeats features in linear, circular, or other arrangements.

14.8 Parametric Modelling

  • Parametric modelling is a CAD method in which geometry is controlled by dimensions, constraints, and relationships.
  • When a parameter changes, the model updates automatically according to its defined relationships.

Example

A circular plate is designed with:

  • Outer diameter = 100 mm.
  • Thickness = 10 mm.
  • Centre hole = 20 mm.

If the outer diameter is changed to 120 mm, the model updates while retaining the specified relationships and constraints.

Important terms:

  • Parameter – A value controlling geometry.
  • Constraint – A condition defining geometric relationships.
  • Dimension – A numerical measurement controlling size or position.
  • Feature tree – A history of operations used to create the model.
